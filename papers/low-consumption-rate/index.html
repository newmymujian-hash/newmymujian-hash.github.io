China's Low Consumption Rate and Stage-Based Household Expenditure

Full English translation. Explains China's low consumption rate through stage-based household expenditure and the intergenerational expenditure cycle.

✦ 28 min read

Jian Mu

School of Economics, Renmin University of China, Beijing 100872

Originally published in Chinese in Lanzhou Academic Journal, 2010, No. 3, pp. 50–54. Translated from the author’s manuscript.

Abstract: Building on life-cycle theory, permanent income theory, and the theory of precautionary saving, and taking into account China’s deep-rooted family values, this paper puts forward the concepts of stage-based expenditure and the intergenerational expenditure cycle. On the basis of these two concepts, it analyses the income and expenditure behaviour of Chinese households and the relationships among asset prices, income, and the consumption rate, and offers, from a new perspective, an explanation of China’s problem of under-consumption and directions for reform.

Keywords: stage-based expenditure; intergenerational expenditure cycle; low consumption rate

Under-consumption is a persistent malady that many years of economic development in China have yet to resolve effectively. Whether through reform and opening up or through the financial crisis, the saving rate of Chinese residents has remained stubbornly high, while the main forces driving economic growth, apart from the foreign trade sector, have been government fiscal investment. Such an economic structure is not only, in theory, a structure incapable of sustainable growth; in reality, its fragility is laid fully bare when the foreign trade sector is hit hard — as the impact on China of this global financial crisis best proves. The crisis caused by excessive consumption in the United States has also had a very large impact on a country of excessive saving such as China. This cannot but make us ask: why is China’s domestic demand so low by international standards? What can be done to put China, too, on a path on which sound and sustained growth is maintained by relying on domestic demand?

I. A Brief Review of the Literature

The theories that study the links among consumption, saving, and asset prices are, first, life-cycle theory and permanent income theory and, second, the theory of precautionary saving and the theory of consumption-based capital asset pricing. These theories examine the relationships among the three from the perspectives, respectively, of a smoothed consumption path and of risk.

1. Life-Cycle Theory and Permanent Income Theory

The life-cycle hypothesis (Life Cycle Hypothesis, LCH), also known as the life-cycle consumption function model, was put forward jointly by Franco Modigliani, R. Brumberg, and Alberto Ando (1954, 1957, 1963). Its main idea is that the consumption behaviour of a rational consumer is based on the individual’s entire life cycle rather than myopically on the current period. At the same time, Friedman proposed the permanent income hypothesis (Permanent Income Hypothesis, PIH), according to which the consumption of a rational person satisfies the following process:

$$YP_t = \theta Y_t + (1 - \theta)Y_{t-1}$$

where the left-hand side of the equation is current permanent income, and the right-hand side gives, respectively, current income and income in the previous period. It can be seen that, although the two theories differ slightly in the way the model is constructed, their main ideas and conclusions are basically the same; the two models are therefore referred to collectively as the “life-cycle–permanent income hypothesis” (PIH–LCH). Its most central idea is this: consumers earn income when they are young and save part of it, in order to finance consumption in the period after retirement when they have no income, so that consumption can be kept as stable as possible over the whole life cycle and violent fluctuations avoided. This idea can be expressed in Figure 1 below:

Figure 1: The consumption path under the PIH–LCH hypothesis (Dornbusch and Fisher, 2002)

This hypothesis has been quite well verified in the West and largely explains the pattern of variation in Western residents’ saving, but its applicability in China is disputed. This shows, at least from one aspect, that the behaviour of Chinese households and China’s economic situation are distinctive.

2. Precautionary Saving and Consumption-Based Capital Asset Pricing

Leland (H. E. Leland, 1968) restudied the saving problem from the perspective of risk, arguing that the risk of income fluctuation, measured by variance, could explain that part of saving which the PIH–LCH theory cannot explain; Leland called this part of saving “precautionary saving” (Precautionary Saving). Deaton (1991) further explained the origins and rationality of precautionary saving from the perspective of liquidity constraints: if borrowing constraints exist, residents will increase saving to guard against uncertainty about future income and to provide insurance for smoothing consumption; the more violent the income fluctuation, the more precautionary saving there will be. Carroll (1992) regarded precautionary saving as an “inventory” (buffer stock), which residents hold, again, in order to withstand uncertainty about future income and fluctuations in consumption over the life cycle. The consumption-based capital asset pricing model (CCAPM) was first proposed by Hansen and Singleton (1982) on the basis of the capital asset pricing model; it, too, considers how consumers trade off among the assets they hold, under the condition that consumers pursue the maximisation of consumption utility over the life cycle. Although this model does not incorporate a rich macroeconomic structure, its final result yields the relationship between consumption and asset prices: a risky asset is worth buying only if its future return can compensate for the reduction in current marginal utility. This theory likewise explains, from the perspective of risk, the difference between the actual and theoretical values of consumption — that is, part of the income that should have been used for consumption is used to hold risky assets in order to obtain higher returns in the future.

These two classes of theory have developed into the standard theories for studying the relationships among consumption, saving, and asset prices, with PIH–LCH as the foundation and the other theories as improvements and deepening built upon it. But, as noted above, none of these theories has yet explained China’s resident consumption and saving problems very well; in particular, their power to explain China’s current problem of under-consumption is limited. The root of this lies in the fact that the theories above grasp what is common to market economies but do not grasp what is uniquely individual to the Chinese economy. This paper therefore carries out a further revision on the basis of these theories.

II. The Causes of the Stage-Based Character of Chinese Households’ Target Expenditure

Before analysing China’s specific problems, it should be made clear that before 1978 China’s economic system was a planned economy, in which households and enterprises had not yet become rational economic agents. After 1978 China gradually entered a market economy, and households acquired considerably more economic freedom than before; only from this point can analysis proceed on the basis of Western theory. More importantly, China’s market economic system is still imperfect, and this, together with its distinctive culture, has jointly produced the stage-based character of Chinese household consumption.

1. The “Holism” of the Chinese Household

The most prominent difference between Chinese households and Western households is their “holism” (household holism — the household as an integrated unit). This holism is manifested in the indistinct division between generations, in the sociological sense as much as in the economic sense. Parents’ care for their children does not last only until the children are 18 or able to support themselves; rather, so long as they have the means, it continues until the end of their life cycle. Likewise, children depend on their parents when they have no income, and after they obtain income they must also take the needs of the older generation into account. The formation of this state of affairs bears both the imprint of Confucian culture and the constraints of economic conditions. Confucian culture places great emphasis on wholeness and hierarchy, whether for the “state” or for the “family”: the upper level should be responsible for the lower level and set an example for it, while the lower level should obey and respect the upper level. The holistic conception of the family, parents’ love for their children, and children’s “filial piety” towards their parents are therefore all factors that must be considered in analysing the behaviour of Chinese households. In economic terms, China’s large population means that the economic aggregate is always insufficient relative to the population needing employment, so employment opportunities are scarce and individual income is thereby constrained. Under these conditions, it is rational behaviour for family members to act together as a whole and provide insurance for one another. The starting point for consumption optimisation by Chinese consumers is thus the household encompassing several generations, unlike consumers in developed Western countries, who consider individual optimisation. (In Western households, joint optimisation that includes children mostly stops when the children reach adulthood, so it is short-term behaviour; and old-age support for parents is socialised, so it does not enter the calculation.) This paper therefore holds that the individual life cycle, which serves as the basis in Western consumption theory, is inappropriate in China; the actual situation in China is a “life cycle” whose base point is the household. Total household income changes as the proportion of household members earning income changes, but the time horizon of optimisation is not strongly constrained by the end of an individual’s life: although retirement and death affect fluctuations in total household income, they do not mark the end of the optimisation interval. Once this time constraint is broken through, household behaviour changes: the older generation will consider the consumption behaviour belonging to its own life cycle over a longer period of time.

2. Insufficient and Uncertain Income, and Borrowing Constraints

Compared with developed Western countries, although the per capita income of Chinese residents has grown rapidly, the total is still very low: by the end of 2007 China still ranked among the lower-middle-income countries of the world.

Moreover, regional, sectoral, and urban–rural disparities are comparatively large. “The gap between rich and poor has now widened from 4.5:1 at the beginning of reform and opening up to 12.66:1. The income of urban residents relative to that of rural residents has widened from 2.52:1 in 1998 to 3.31:1 in 2008. Income disparities between the central and western regions and the rest have also continued to widen. Incomes in sectors such as electricity, petroleum, and finance are far above the national average, with gaps as high as tenfold.” Moreover, “according to World Bank data, the Gini coefficient of Chinese residents’ income has risen from 0.16 before reform and opening up to 0.47 at present.” If the rapid rise of this figure is not curbed, it will not be long before it reaches 0.6, the danger line by international standards.

The government has already noticed the negative effects of this aggregate constraint, and of structural change, on sustained growth and social stability, and is therefore pressing ahead with deeper market-oriented reform and improvement. Reform plans have been issued for such major livelihood issues as employment, education, medical care, old-age support, and housing, but it is clear that the effort is still insufficient: in the short term these problems have not been substantively resolved, and in the long term residents’ expectations about future income have been left unclear, so residents will still face great uncertainty in the future. Under the influence of this aggregate income constraint and uncertainty about future income, residents’ choice of precautionary saving becomes inevitable. On the other hand, because China’s market economy is imperfectly developed, many of the financial instruments that Western consumers can use to smooth their consumption path are still insufficiently used in China — for example, loan financing for individuals. The large formal banks, unable to know the credit standing of any given consumer, are unwilling to extend consumer credit casually, while small and medium-sized banks, because of their inherent regional character, find it hard to do business in other places; and consumers, while harbouring misgivings about borrowing to consume, are equally worried about default. Under the combined action of these several factors, when a household faces a large expenditure, the most reliable method is to choose to pay out of personal saving. Provided the price of the object purchased is stable, however, this method of payment not only postpones the time of purchase; more importantly, it raises the household’s saving rate and reduces current consumption.

3. Expenditure Characteristics under the Constraint of a Finite Life Cycle

In addition to the characteristics and constraints above, the finiteness of the individual life cycle is also an important aspect. Because life is finite, individuals, when optimising, consider obtaining as much satisfaction as possible within a finite time. One point that needs emphasis in China is that a person’s life cycle is divided, naturally, by society and by physiology, into different stages. The important dividing markers are, respectively: the present generation takes up work; marries; the third generation appears; the first generation is supported in old age; the third generation is educated; then the present generation becomes the first generation, and the third generation becomes the second generation, and a new cycle begins. For a Chinese household, between each pair of markers there exists a large expenditure that must be completed: the marriage expenses of the present generation and its housing; old-age support and medical expenses for the present generation and the generation above; and educational expenses for the third generation.

Figure 2: The intergenerational expenditure cycle of the household

More importantly, these large expenditures have a definite order of payment; paying in advance is often impossible, and is not optimising behaviour either. Just as a person cannot, before marrying, first set aside sufficient old-age and education funds for the previous generation or the next generation and only then consider the present generation’s housing and marriage expenditure. It can be imagined that, if there were no financial constraint, a lump-sum expenditure could fully optimise the various needs within the life cycle; but financial constraints do exist in reality, so the household must choose stage-based expenditure, completing each item of expenditure within the life cycle one by one in sequence in order to reach the optimum. In order to pass one “hurdle” after another within the life cycle, all members of a household must strive together, paying according to the different goals of each stage. More importantly, for most Chinese households the optimum can be reached only by completing several more items within one’s own life cycle, so, under all kinds of constraints, shortening the time taken to complete each item of expenditure becomes an inevitable choice. If, at some stage, the “task” of that stage cannot be completed, then postponing the time of payment and crowding out the resources for tasks at other stages will reduce the household’s total utility. The worst situation arises when a stage-based expenditure is so high that it cannot be completed within the life cycle.

To sum up, therefore, the characteristics of Chinese households’ consumption and large-expenditure behaviour can be summarised as follows: (1) the household acts as a whole in optimising; (2) the large expenditures listed in this paper have very low elasticity — housing demand in particular is rigid; (3) payment is perpetual across generations. Under the condition that income is constrained, these behavioural characteristics are expressed as: income = saving + most basic consumption + affluent consumption, where saving is held precisely in order to complete housing and other large expenditures; most basic consumption cannot be cut, but affluent consumption, when income falls, is partly or wholly converted into saving.

III. Asset Prices and Under-Consumption in Relation to Stage-Based Expenditure

This paper holds that the ultimate causes of China’s current under-consumption can be analysed using the theory above. Relative to the income of Chinese households, housing, medical care, and children’s education are all very expensive; under the condition of stage-based expenditure, housing becomes still more of an obstacle to raising household utility, and one of the reasons why residents save.

1. A Graphical Illustration of Single-Cycle, Single-Stage Expenditure

For convenience of exposition, suppose first, simplifying, that housing expenditure is the expenditure of the first stage, that one’s own old-age needs are the last stage, and that there are no other stages. In the figure, the curve is the function of house prices over time, and OAT is the function of household income over time. By the definitions above, the areas under the curves should be the total house price and total income.

Figure 3: Single-cycle, single-stage expenditure

At point $T_1$, the total income accumulated by the household is wholly insufficient to bear the total price of the house. If the household lives within this range throughout period $T$, it will never be able to buy the property, and likewise will be unable to optimise. If, however, after some time, at point $T_2$, the household’s total income has risen considerably as the number of working members has increased, then the household can fully complete the house-purchase task through the total accumulated between $T_1$ and $T_2$ (the area of the rectangle $T_1CBT_2$). After point $T_2$, the whole household can consume its total income ($S_1 + S_2$) after deducting $S_3$, the consumption of the post-retirement period without income, and thereby raise the household’s overall utility. Consider the extreme case in which points C and A in the figure coincide: the household’s total income will then never be able to purchase the property, and optimisation is impossible. If points B and A coincide, the household’s total income will be used entirely to purchase the property, and no funds will be available after retirement. In this situation, the household can also lock in the total house price in advance through a loan, and then complete the task of repaying the loan by reducing post-retirement consumption. But if the cost of the housing loan is too high, this method is also infeasible. Looking at this figure alone, then, in order to raise the consumption share one may restrain house prices from growing too fast, raise the speed of income growth, lower the cost of housing loans, or reduce the amount of post-retirement consumption. The ideas above can be expressed by the following model:

Suppose that, after its most basic consumption has been satisfied, the household must first complete the various tasks within its life cycle, the first of which is housing. Let the speed at which income increases be $y(t)$, the rate of change of house prices be $p(t)$, and the increase in most basic consumption be $\delta$ times the increase in income ($0 < \delta < 1$). Suppose that at time $T$ the household is able to complete the house-purchase task; then the following equation (1) holds:

$$\int_0^T p(t)\,dt = \int_0^T \left[ y(t) - \delta y(t) \right] dt \tag{1}$$

Differentiating the above equation with respect to its upper limit of integration gives:

$$p(T) = (1 - \delta)\,y(T) \tag{2}$$$$\frac{p(T)}{y(T)} = (1 - \delta) \tag{3}$$

It can be seen from (3) that, as time passes, the faster house prices rise, the more the household must reduce the share of basic consumption and increase saving in order to complete the house-purchase task — that is, reduce the consumption rate and increase the saving rate. Because completion of the planned tasks is subject to the strong constraint of the life cycle, in the special circumstance in which the household foresees that, relying on income alone, it cannot complete the task within the first cycle, $\delta$ may take a negative value: that is, the household buys the house in advance by borrowing, locking in the total house price, and then repays over the remaining life cycle, or even leaves the next generation to continue repaying.

2. A Graphical Illustration of Single-Cycle, Multi-Stage Consumption

Figure 4: Single-cycle, multi-stage expenditure

The figure above, drawn by combining the assumption of stage-based expenditure set out earlier with the single-stage expenditure figure, depicts a household’s multi-stage expenditure. In the figure, the household must complete as many of its expenditure tasks as possible before the point of retirement. If, during the intervals $T_1 \sim T_2$ or $T_3 \sim T_4$, income growth cannot keep up with the speed at which expenditure prices grow, the lag in these two intervals will in effect “crowd out” the tasks of medical care and old-age support within the life cycle; but we assume that “reducing life in order to lower expenditure is unreasonable,” so the household can only reduce the other expenditures, on condition that the first task is completed, and complete the first task at the cost of the quality of the subsequent expenditures. At the same time, it must retain a certain amount of precautionary saving so that it can meet basic living expenditure when it has no income. In this situation, therefore, the household’s consumption rate is bound to fall.

3. Multi-Cycle, Multi-Stage Consumption Expenditure

Consider the perpetual, intergenerational character of the household. If the first generation withdraws before a certain task has been completed, then making up the expenditure that the previous generation did not complete becomes the primary task of the second generation — namely, the period before $T_{12}$. Only after this is completed does the second generation begin a new cycle. This undoubtedly further postpones the time at which the second generation completes all its tasks; at the same time, it also means that the second generation must compress consumption and save more from the very moment it begins to obtain income. This transfer of financial pressure between generations becomes a new and important reason for the suppression of the consumption rate.

IV. Brief Conclusions and Directions for Reform

China has a large population and deep-rooted family values; as the economy has developed through reform and opening up, many new problems have appeared. Such livelihood issues as housing, medical care, old-age support, and education have gradually become important factors affecting household stability, and have thereby also become major social problems. On the basis of the analysis above, the following several directions can be found for solving China’s problem of under-consumption:

1. Raise workers’ income, especially the income of workers in employment

Workers’ income is the foundation of all consumption and expenditure. Although China’s per capita income has grown very fast, it is still very insufficient compared with the other expenditures discussed in this paper. Raising the rate of income growth is therefore fundamental to solving China’s current problem of an insufficient consumption rate. After a long period of economic construction, China’s economic structure has begun to change: the era in which manufacturing held an absolute share has passed, and the vigorous development of the tertiary sector has provided broad scope for income growth. At the present stage, a considerable proportion of China’s employed workers are engaged in work in the tertiary sector, so stabilising the income of employed workers and raising the income of tertiary-sector workers can effectively shorten the time taken to complete the expenditure of each stage, and thereby effectively promote a rise in the consumption rate.

2. Postpone the retirement age, or enable retired persons to obtain income again in the market

With the improvement of living standards, China’s average life expectancy has lengthened from 41 years in 1949 to an expected 72 years in 2010. The lengthening of the life cycle means that workers can optimise their behaviour over a longer time, but China’s stipulated retirement age forcibly blocks the possibility of this optimising behaviour. This stipulation, of course, takes into account China’s severe employment pressure, but that cannot justify depriving retired workers of the right to continue obtaining income. It is therefore suggested that a suitable market of supply and demand be provided for workers willing to continue working after retirement, extending the time over which this group of workers obtains income. As to the concern that these workers will compete with younger workers for jobs, the author considers this not to be obvious. First, the knowledge structures mastered by these two groups differ, and the jobs created in the new economy may be ones that older workers cannot perform. Second, the two markets can be segmented through age limits, avoiding intergenerational competition. Finally, making use of the human capital that workers have accumulated, where they do so voluntarily, is itself the original intent of economics.

3. Stabilise asset prices and the prices of other items of large expenditure

The large expenditures of Chinese households at present are concentrated in the several items mentioned in this paper, housing expenditure above all being the most important of all. Take Beijing as an example: in 2008 the average wage of Beijing employees was 44,715 yuan, while the average price of second-hand housing had already reached 13,150 yuan per square metre by November 2009. Calculated at the 18 square metres of housing per person published by the Ministry of Construction, and supposing a household of three generations totalling seven people — four elderly persons, two working adults, and one child — purchasing a second-hand house would require the whole household to accumulate for nearly 20 years while undertaking no consumption at all. If the elderly need medical care and the child needs education, this period can only be lengthened — quite apart from the fact that the household’s basic expenditure is indispensable. Thus a 22-year-old university graduate who, on starting work, obtains a job with an annual salary of 44,000 yuan must reduce consumption if he wishes to complete several more tasks before he retires. Of course, this household’s income may grow step by step with economic growth, but the prices of other expenditures are also rising; if the risk of unemployment is also taken into account, saving more is the only road. To change the status quo, therefore, the prices of these items of large expenditure must be forcefully stabilised — at the very least, the rate at which prices rise must be curbed, so that households can see that the time needed to complete payment is not being postponed as expenditure costs rise.

4. Socialise expenditure within the life cycle, and change the order of expenditure

The key to what causes stage-based expenditure is that the order of these large expenditures cannot be changed. The various loan and financing instruments change only the ability of some people to complete a certain expenditure within the life cycle; they do not change the order of expenditure, and at the same time they add costs and affect the time the next generation takes to complete its expenditures. The only way to eliminate this order of expenditure is therefore to spread these expenditures across every household, using the time differences in the expenditure cycle to help households complete their expenditure tasks in turn: that is, to establish various forms of insurance directed at households’ large expenditures. Such insurance should be provided uniformly by the state; otherwise, households unable to pay the insurance premiums in full will still be crowded out of this process. Under the requirement of stability and harmony, the direction of state fiscal spending should first tend towards guaranteeing the people’s livelihood.

Chinese society has its own distinctive characteristics, and strong family values are one of them. If all kinds of economic and social problems are considered in detachment from this reality, the conclusions reached will be biased. This paper has taken this characteristic into account; its deeper implications remain to be further explored and to be confirmed with data.
