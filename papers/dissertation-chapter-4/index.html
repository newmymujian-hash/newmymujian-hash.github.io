Chapter 4 The Continuation of the Cash-Flow Consumption Model in the Early Stage of Marketization Reform

Under the planned economic system there were profound institutional reasons why residents’ consumption displayed a “cash-flow model” and a “dependence on saving”, but these institutional reasons began to loosen after 1978. As the economic system made its transition from plan to market, the economic behaviour of enterprises, of the government, and of households began to be marketized. In this period the residents’ consumption rate grew considerably, but the cash consumption model underwent no fundamental change: the behavioural characteristics of depending on saving, and of depressing everyday consumption in order to reach a saving target, persisted. The marketization reform of this stage therefore did not touch the sphere of consumption and consumers; it was merely a quantitative effect brought about by the increase in the economic aggregate and the quickening of its growth — that is, the increase in the aggregate did not change the conditions constraining consumers’ behaviour, so that the consumption model neither could nor needed to change, and it was on precisely this basis that the original consumption model was continued.

The year 1978 is the landmark year in which China formally began the transition from a planned economy to a socialist market economy, and this major change of economic system, from plan to market, brought vitality to the economy: prices gradually replaced mandatory plans as the signal allocating all kinds of economic resources, and the resulting rise in efficiency showed itself in every respect. In aggregate terms, gross domestic product rose steadily, enterprise profits and fiscal revenue grew markedly, and as personal incomes rose, income channels began at the same time to diversify. At the same time, the content of household consumption underwent its first upgrading, leaping from the original hundred-yuan class to the thousand-yuan class, and durable household appliances began to enter households on a large scale. In this period, the structure of China’s working population was also in its “dividend” phase, and for households education and medical reform had not yet begun, so expenditure in these respects did not constitute an important consideration in household expenditure. Despite the uncertain factors and risks of industrial restructuring, the development of an outward-oriented economy, and financial system reform, and despite the income risks brought by individual modes of employment, household consumption in this period depended less on saving and more on income growth; but none of this constituted a major shock to the “cash-flow” consumption model. The household consumption model still displayed the old characteristics of dependence on cash, dependence on saving, and stage-based expenditure.

4.1 The Influence of Marketization Reform on Residents’ Income

In the era of the planned economy, the greatest characteristic of urban residents’ income was fairness at a low level, and this was precisely the manifestation, in respect of both efficiency and equity, of the effects of the planned economic system. After China began to build a market economy, the laws of the market and their results also manifested themselves clearly and rapidly. As efficiency rose, GDP entered a channel of steady growth, and at the same time fiscal revenue and the aggregate of residents’ income began to increase. In structural terms, however, the distribution of national income among residents, the government, and enterprises also began to become unequal, and the income gap between urban and rural residents also began to appear.

According to the core theory of this dissertation, the reason is that the aggregate cash flow of rural residents’ income is lower than that of urban residents, while at the same time, faced with large rigid expenditures, rural residents are more inclined, under their constraints, to increase saving, so that lowering the consumption rate becomes an inevitable choice.

4.1.2 Fiscal Revenue Gradually Declines as a Share in the Primary Distribution of National Income

As the country’s economic strength grew, fiscal revenue also increased year by year: fiscal revenue in 1992 had grown by more than two times compared with 1978. Taking 1978 as the dividing point, we also discover a marked characteristic in the proportion of fiscal revenue to GDP. In the era of the planned economy this proportion was stable at around 30 per cent; apart from the two special periods of the natural disasters and the “Cultural Revolution”, in which it fluctuated considerably, it was fairly stable in other years. After the entry into marketization reform, however, the share of fiscal revenue in national income declined year by year, falling to 13 per cent by 1992. The fundamental reason for this phenomenon lies in the fact that before 1978 China adopted, in fiscal revenue and expenditure, the system of “unified revenue and unified expenditure” （统收统支）: although fiscal resources were limited, the share taken in the primary distribution of national income was nevertheless stable, and the state’s control over the economy was likewise stable; and it was precisely because it had such stable fiscal revenue that the state was able, so far as possible, to bear the various kinds of “welfare” under the planned system and the redistribution that guaranteed fairness. After the entry into the stage of marketization reform, however, in order to encourage the raising of efficiency the state “delegated powers and yielded profits” （放权让利） to enterprises, and in primary distribution it inclined towards enterprises, which gave rise to the phenomenon of the declining share of fiscal revenue in primary distribution. In addition, the growth of fiscal revenue after 1978 was also markedly lower than the growth of GDP, and this too was a result of the state’s delegation of powers and yielding of profits.

Because of the rise in the economic aggregate brought by the market economy and the improvement in enterprises’ operating efficiency, the fact that fiscal revenue’s share of gross national product declined year by year did not change the trend of fiscal revenue growing year by year: it can be seen that over these fifteen years fiscal revenue grew at an average rate of close to 8 per cent a year, and the government’s fiscal strength was not weakened by marketization reform but, on the contrary, was strengthened. The increase in fiscal revenue in this period was, however, mainly aggregate-driven, that is, fiscal revenue increased because the GDP aggregate grew. The three economic agents — government, enterprises, and consumers — participated jointly in market economic reform, and if the shares of the growth obtained by government and consumers diminished or changed little, it may reasonably be presumed that enterprises became the greatest beneficiaries of the marketization process in this period.

4.1.3 Enterprise Efficiency Improves

In this period the improvement in enterprise efficiency was marked in scale. First, the gross output value of industry increased more than sixfold, by more than 16 per cent a year on average, and it was precisely this increase that became the powerful driving force behind the growth of gross national product. Although this speed of industrial production began under conditions in which domestic demand, long suppressed, was released in large quantities after reform and opening, and although enterprise operation and management at that time were still far from the level of management of costs and product quality attained by enterprises under modern market-economy conditions, enterprises’ profits in this period nevertheless rose steadily, driven by the aggregate, also reaching an average increase of 5 per cent a year. Driven by enterprises’ vigorous investment and production, their capacity to absorb employment was also released, the average annual number of persons employed rising year by year.

The rise in enterprises’ output value and efficiency in this period shows from another side that marketization reform liberated the productive forces and profoundly changed the relations of production. For example, the expansion of enterprises’ autonomy enabled them to choose products, output, and prices according to the laws of the market and their own capacities. In the use of operating funds they no longer depended wholly on state allocations, but could raise finance through banks and other enterprises; this relation between the supply of and demand for capital caused China’s financial market to develop gradually and provided the foundation for the further reform that followed. In the use of labour, too, enterprises acquired more new forms: besides formal employees they could also offer short-term labour contracts according to actual need. These institutions, which could only take effect once the shackles of the planned economy were cast off, gave enterprises a profound degree of freedom; enterprises gradually grew into market agents rather than economic units on a planning table, and it was precisely this that formed the foundation of the rise in enterprises’ productive efficiency and of macroeconomic growth. In addition, the nature of enterprises also gradually changed from the single public ownership of the past to the co-existence of private and state ownership. The benefit brought by the recognition of the legitimacy of private enterprises is not only the affirmation of private property rights; more importantly, it initially constructed an institutional environment of competition. Institutional change of this kind is the most important thing in the marketization process, and is also the motive force driving the ceaseless growth of the economy.

4.1.4 Household Disposable Income Rises

While the market economy brought a rise in efficiency, it also affected equity. Not only in the sphere of primary distribution: marked gaps had already begun to appear in labour income alone. The major change of economic system also had a profound influence on consumers’ income and consumption behaviour: aggregate income, income gaps, consumption levels, and the consumption structure all began to differ markedly from those under the planned economic system. The result produced by this change of aggregate income and expenditure in the same direction is, on the one hand, an increase in aggregate consumption, on the other hand, an upgrading of consumption, and finally a strengthening of stage-based consumption behaviour. That is to say, the change that economic reform worked on enterprise behaviour did not occur in consumers: the consumption model still maintained the cash-flow model. This shows that the conditions under which the cash-flow consumption model was formed had not been “reformed”. 4.2 The Appearance of the First Upgrading of Consumption

4.2.1 A Qualitative Description of the Upgrading of Consumption

The absolute increase in the economic aggregate promoted the increase in the aggregate of individual income, and the relative inclination of income distribution towards enterprises and residents strengthened the capacity to consume; the consumption capacity suppressed under the planned economic system was released, its most obvious manifestation being the steady rise in households’ holdings of durable consumer goods. At this stage, colour televisions, refrigerators, and washing machines, as important consumer goods for raising the quality of life, became consumption hotspots, replacing the “old three big items” formed in the planned economy period and represented by watches, bicycles, and radios. From the data above, combined with households’ holdings of the old three big items in the same period, it can be found that in this period households had basically approached saturation in the former hotspot products, and since the consumer goods were all durable, there was no desire to replace them either. As the income level of each income stratum rose with economic development, the appearance of new consumption hotspots was natural. In addition, looking at the aggregate of holdings, the number of washing machines in the same period was comparatively large, the reason being that the price of washing machines was relatively low, and among these three appliances their substitution for labour — or, one may say, their raising of utility — was the most marked. Finally, the upgrading of consumption was a universal phenomenon across all income strata, which can be seen from the change over time in the aggregate holdings of consumer goods at different income strata. This very consistent consumption trend is the most powerful annotation of the upgrading of consumption.

At the same time, it should be noted that in this period Chinese households were initially entering the marketization process, while China’s systems of housing, medical care, education, and employment had not kept pace with this step from the planned economy period, so the “constraints” of the cash-flow consumption model discussed in this dissertation had not disappeared. The reason consumption could upgrade rapidly at this time is that households’ saving, after satisfying the basic requirements of life, had not been used up — that is, households had sufficient cash or saving to complete the payment. This upgrading of consumption thus reduced households’ aggregate saving, while at the same time further consolidating the core position of saving in household assets, and continued the cash consumption model. Under conditions of rising income, although the prices of these consumer goods were higher than those of watches and the like, households’ capacity to consume them was still strong. What is more important is that, relative to disposable income, the total price of these goods was still higher than households’ capacity to pay, yet households still financed them through personal saving rather than commercial loans. That is to say, in respect of the consumption model, the cash consumption model and the consumption model dependent on saving showed no improvement. The upgrading of consumption in China’s rural areas was also gradually proceeding; because the growth of aggregate income was slower than in the cities, the time at which the upgrading of consumption occurred, and its speed, also lagged behind the cities. For an analysis of this aspect, see Li Runliang (2010).

The upgrading of consumption is not only closely related to residents’ income and the level of social development; it also interacts with, and mutually promotes, the consumption model that is gradually forming. The constraints of the cash consumption model formed in the planned economy period had not been relaxed by the market economic reform that had only just begun, so whatever the object of consumption and however income grew, the essence of Chinese household consumption behaviour would not change. This does not mean, however, that marketization reform had not the slightest influence on the original consumption model. First, the development strategy of “efficiency first, equity later” caused the income gap gradually to appear, and income is the most important constraint determining the consumption model, so as this gap gradually deepened, China’s consumption model also gradually began to differentiate: for the high-income strata, the cash consumption model gradually weakened, while in low-income households it was retained and even strengthened. Second, marketization required enterprises to transform themselves from units executing plans into rational economic men, so the change in enterprise behaviour caused consumers who had originally depended on the “work unit” to lose their protection; and taken together with the changes in China’s population aggregate and structure, the state systems of security in medical care, old-age provision, housing, and employment could not operate — the institutional change in these constraints is what became the major influence of market reform on the consumption model. It may be said that the transformation of the consumption model is not a remoulding of the forms of consumption, but rather guides the conversion of the consumption model by remoulding the conditions constraining consumers.

4.3 The Upgrading of Consumption Is Incompatible with the Cash-Flow Consumption Model

The characteristics of the cash consumption model have been summarised above, and an important one of them is that it is adapted to a relatively low level of economic development, a characteristic manifested on the consumer’s side as a relatively low level of consumption structure. If in the era of the planned economy China’s labourers were basically content with adequate food and clothing and were clearly at a very low level of consumption, then as reform and opening and the marketization process proceeded, and as the economic aggregate rose ceaselessly, labourers also came to have higher demands of their own lives, hoping to pass from adequate food and clothing towards a moderately prosperous life, and further to reach a comfortable and enjoyment-oriented standard of living. But the consumer goods corresponding to a life of this kind have changed fundamentally from those of the lower stage, and this change leads to the incompatibility of the cash-flow consumption model. Analysed from the angle of the laws of consumption upgrading, the cash-flow consumption model is likewise not conducive to the appearance and maintenance of new stages of consumption. According to the explanation of consumption economics, consumption behaviour towards consumer goods passes gradually from lower-priced goods to higher-priced goods, from the subsistence type to the enjoyment type. This transition period displays the following three characteristics: (1) the long-run consumption curve is the envelope of the short-run consumption curves; (2) the time intervals of consumption upgrading grow longer and longer; (3) the consumption curve of durable consumer goods is “inverted-U” shaped. Then, from the macro angle: as the marketization process accelerates and Chinese households basically reach the level of adequate food and clothing, what is the influence upon, and the relation to, the consumption model of the development of the urban and rural consumption structure?

4.3.1 The Upgrading of Durable Consumer Goods Requires a Change of Consumption Model

This incompatibility is manifested first in respect of durable consumer goods. The quantity and variety of durable consumer goods are an important mark for measuring a household’s standard of living — just as trains and steamships symbolise the age of steam, televisions and refrigerators symbolise the electrical age, and computers and the internet symbolise the information age. Before China entered the track of marketization reform, the level of economic development and socialist public ownership meant that the durable consumer goods held by households were almost a blank, in quantity and variety alike. In the cities, housing belonged wholly to common property, administered by the work unit to which one belonged and allocated by grade; labourers, while using housing, needed to pay only a very small rent. This meant that housing, a large asset, did not enter households’ consumption planning catalogues at all. According to data in the China Statistical Yearbook, in 1985 China’s urban households owned only 152 bicycles per hundred households, and rural households only 80, while the number of complete sets of furniture was still smaller, at only 4.29; as for electrical appliances, this dissertation selects televisions and electric fans, of which urban households held 17 per hundred households, while rural households had none; electric fans, whose price is somewhat lower, numbered about 74 per hundred households in the cities and about 10 in the countryside. From this it may be inferred that in 1978, and still earlier, the quantities of these consumer goods possessed by Chinese households were lower still.

As the market economy developed in depth and enterprises’ manufacturing capacity rose, these consumer goods, in respect of both price and residents’ income, no longer required long-term saving in order to buy them; some products have even withdrawn completely from the field of vision of household consumption because they have been replaced by new products — for example, the replacement of electric fans by air conditioners, and of bicycles by motor cars — and this replacement is the upgrading of consumption, that is, the demand still exists but the product satisfying the demand has changed. The direct result of the upgrading of consumption is that utility is greatly satisfied while expenditure also rises substantially. So the problem placed before households’ consumption goals may not have changed in substance: that is, when income is insufficient to satisfy the upgrading of consumption, saving finance or other financing channels are still needed. This situation often occurs: the prices of new consumer goods rise with the process of consumption upgrading, possibly by more than income rises, with the result that, because the product’s price is too high, the upgrading of consumption cannot be completed. Under the planned economy, there were generally two channels of finance for such consumption upgrading: one was financing among household members and relatives, the other private financing among friends. But as population mobility accelerates, the financing capacity of both channels is being weakened by the market, while marketized financing channels are gradually growing. If consumers consume through commercial borrowing, then their balance sheets and cash-flow statements will undergo a qualitative change, thereby bringing about changes in the consumption model and in the model of managing the balance sheet. This is precisely the incompatibility of the cash-flow consumption model with the process of consumption upgrading under marketized conditions, and also the formal requirement for the appearance of the balance-sheet consumption model.

4.3.2 The Transformation of the Consumption Structure Requires a Change of Consumption Model

The consumption structure refers to the shares of the quantities and prices of the products consumed in a household’s total consumption plan; it is another important perspective from which to examine a household’s standard and mode of living. Generally speaking, when the degree of economic development is low or a household’s aggregate income level is low, food accounts for a comparatively high share of total expenditure; as income rises, the kinds of food consumed may change, new or better food items will be consumed, but their share of expenditure gradually declines while non-food consumption gradually appears; as income rises further, the share of food expenditure in total consumption declines further and tends to stabilise, while the shares of other kinds of consumption increase markedly and their content changes with the changes in society.

But when the consumption goal changes to “service-type products” such as medical care and education, the nature of the product itself determines that the consumption fund cannot possibly be completed by saving over a short time all at once; the quantity and quality of such products needed in a lifetime can only be determined according to the concrete stage of life. Second, consumption of such products has fairly great elasticity, so price can to a great extent determine the quantity and quality of consumption. Finally, the total price of such products is often comparatively large, and completing payment through simple saving under the original consumption model can no longer meet requirements; the change in the nature of the consumption object requires the means of one-off payment to be transformed into a means of stage-based payment, and this means of payment is precisely the core of the new consumption model.

4.3.3 The Transformation of Consumption Concepts Requires a Change of Consumption Model

Consumption concepts refer to the attitudes and beliefs held by consumers in the process of their consumption activity. Although consumption concepts have departed from the scope of economics research, subjectively formed consumption concepts do indeed have a close connection with objective consumption activity. Man lives in a society composed of other people, and at the same time man also lives among commodities, so the individual characteristics of the consumer, the mutual influence among consumer groups, and the influence of consumer goods themselves on consumers all sway consumption behaviour to different degrees. In China’s period of planned economy, household income was low, and the influence of the traditional Chinese consciousness of the “virtue of thrift” caused much saving and little consumption to be esteemed and practised; nor did there exist among consumers any obvious consumption emulation, imitation, or catching-up. So under the guidance of objective constraints and subjective conditions alike, the cash consumption model logically became the mainstream. But when the country’s door opened, and as its own economy developed, the consumers of the developed countries and their richer consumer goods formed a shock to the concepts of China’s consumers; gradually, consumerism not only influenced the generation that had originally esteemed thrift, but also caused the new generation to grow up within this consumption concept; borrowing to consume, once very hard to accept, is easily carried out among the younger generation and is even treated as a fashion and imitated competitively, and under conditions in which the functions of consumer goods are already sufficient, ceaselessly upgrading and replacing them is nothing surprising. For all that consumer concepts have indeed stimulated enterprises’ research, development, and innovation in products, the consumer good is, after all, a commodity, and needs to be bought and paid for. So fast a speed of consumption renewal means that the path of “save, buy, use, break down, buy again” fundamentally cannot satisfy consumers’ requirements, and so the path of “borrow, buy, use and repay, borrow again, buy the new model, use and repay” becomes possible. In this way, satisfying the demand for utility first and considering repayment afterwards becomes the new consumption concept. From a certain angle, the acceleration of the speed of consumption may cater to the acceleration of the speed of change in the market economy; although this new concept has brought risks never experienced before, its power of attraction to the new generation of consumers is still enormous. So this transformation of consumption concepts has, in a more profound sense, become a motive force for the transformation of the cash consumption model.

4.4 The Cash-Flow Consumption Model Cannot Effectively Withstand Market Risk

4.4.1 The Influence of the Marketization Process on Income and Expenditure

After the marketization process began, the original cash-flow consumption model had, besides the internal volatility and stage-based character discussed above, some further respects in which it was incompatible with the free market economic system. Market economic reform increased consumers’ explicit income and at the same time also made explicit the subsidies that were originally implicit, turning them into things one must pay for oneself in cash; the gradual rise in the level of material consumption made the value of consumption objects ever greater, and income once again displayed “shortage” relative to expenditure; under conditions in which private property rights are recognised, the expansion of the balance sheet brought returns and risk side by side. None of these incompatibilities improved as the marketization process gradually deepened; on the contrary, they once had a tendency to worsen, so the cash-flow consumption model did not change but was forcibly strengthened, and it is precisely this strengthening that has suppressed the rise in China’s consumption rate and caused consumption and domestic demand to be unable for a long time to become a motive force driving economic development.

Under the cash consumption model, the influence of changes in product prices on the consumption path is also very great. Limited income, together with target consumer goods whose value is high relative to income, inevitably makes consumption behaviour sensitive to changes in commodity prices; a small rise in price may very possibly cause most households to postpone their consumption plans. Concretely, in China’s period of planned economy, because the emphasis was on heavy industrialisation, the manufacturing level of light industrial products was not high, with the result that the prices of such products, which are closely connected with the people’s lives, were comparatively high; added to the “shortage” characteristic of the planned economy, which often caused product prices to rise, households facing the risk of price adjustment were often at a loss, with the result that the consumption path could not be smoothed.

The increase in unexpected expenditure is also one of the risks the cash consumption model cannot withstand. Unexpected expenditure includes expenditure arising from accidental damage to the bodies and property of household members; illness, injury, theft, and the like all belong to this kind of risk. In societies in which the insurance system is developed, such losses can be compensated through commercial insurance or social insurance; but households under the cash consumption model have no insurance of this kind, and when such accidents occur they can only withstand them partly through their own saving. The result and influence ultimately caused are similar to those of a rise in commodity prices.

Finally, the risk of changes in the macroeconomic environment, such as inflation, is also a blind spot in the defences of the cash model. In the early 1990s, because a large amount of consumption potential was released, a situation of demand exceeding supply was created, finally causing serious inflation in the monetary sphere. Faced with inflation, households seemed to have no better way of withstanding it than to continue buying more commodities; the returns brought by increasing saving were far from able to compensate the losses brought by inflation, with the result that commodity prices rose still more rapidly. Under the cash model there is a lack of financial instruments that can be used for hedging, and a lack of recognition of private property rights, which makes this model fundamentally unable to adapt to the needs of the existence and development of a market economy.

It is precisely this unchanged consumption model that has suppressed the rise in China’s consumption rate.

4.5 The Status and Role of Consumption at the Macroeconomic Level

The upgrading of the consumption structure is a macro phenomenon, formed by aggregating consumption objects at the micro level, while aggregate consumption is a macro aggregate in the value sense. The economic aggregate of an open economy is jointly composed of four parts; for China, the three sectors of investment, trade, and government procurement have always been the principal motive forces of economic growth. Arranging the development strategy in this way was reasonable and necessary in the initial stage of market economic development, because it could bring into play China’s advantage in labour costs and the state’s capacity to mobilise the economy. But as the market economy advances step by step, this kind of economic growth dependent on the non-consumption sectors increasingly displays its insufficiency. Especially after the shocks of the two financial crises of 1997 and 2008, the mode of growth and development driven by reliance on external demand and investment gradually became unable to continue maintaining high growth. Developing consumption and domestic demand has therefore become the focus of China’s macroeconomic development at present. For this we need first to analyse the relations between China’s consumption sector and the other economic sectors.

The aggregation of the consumption behaviour of households and individual consumers constitutes the kernel of changes in the macro consumption rate; at the same time, the interaction, and its results, between consumption in the macro sense and the other economic sectors will also influence the consumption rate and consumption behaviour at the micro level. The role of consumption in the macro sense in the economy is self-evident: it is not only the motive force of development but, still more, its ultimate purpose; progress in the economic aspect of society is to a great extent displayed through the “quantity” and the “quality” of consumption, and the raising of labourers’ standard of living is, still more, embodied in the upgrading of the content of their consumption. So the relations between the consumption sector and the other economic sectors, household consumption behaviour, and the consumption model formed by combining the two will inevitably become the focus of consumption research. What, then, are the actual relations between China’s consumption sector and its other economic sectors? And how are these particular relations connected with the formation of the consumption model of China’s households?

4.5.2 The Relationship between Government Revenue and Expenditure and Consumers

The government is an important economic sector in market economic activity; its behaviour is displayed in investment, government procurement, taxation, and the redistribution of income. If the government is taken as the object of analysis, under conditions in which the aggregate of national income is given, its behaviour in income and expenditure will certainly have a major influence on consumers.

China’s government fiscal revenue has always displayed a fairly stable trend of growth as economic strength has gradually increased; correspondingly, in aggregate and growth rate it has also maintained a very stable consistency of fluctuation with GDP.

Figure 4-13: The Growth Rates of GDP and Fiscal Revenue

[Figure image: image90.wmf] Line chart, c. 1950–2009, of the GDP growth rate and the growth rate of state fiscal revenue (final accounts), in per cent. The two series move closely together throughout; fiscal revenue growth is the more volatile of the two in the early decades, with swings between about −37 and +100 per cent, and both settle into a 10–35 per cent band after 1980.

At the same time, in the primary distribution of national income, we can also find in the data several key points in time explaining the real economic reasons for changes in fiscal revenue. First, taking 1978 as the dividing point: the proportion of fiscal revenue in GDP was stable at a fairly high level before this point, and after it declined year by year. We all know that 1978 was the year China formally began marketization reform; under the planned economic system before it, fiscal revenue’s maintenance of a high level of around 30 per cent was precisely the work of the system. Among these years, the abnormally high figure of 1959 and the abnormally low figure of 1969 were due to statistical data being distorted by natural disasters and to the serious interference of the Cultural Revolution with the economy. Apart from these, the proportion was very stable. In the marketization reform after 1978, and particularly in the 1994 reform of the tax-sharing fiscal system, more fiscal power was devolved to the level of the provinces, so although the central government’s fiscal revenue increased in aggregate, its share in GDP declined year by year. The intention of this reform was originally to delegate powers and yield profits, and to encourage the development of the market economy, but it caused the central government’s capacity to control the localities to decline. Thus after the Asian financial crisis of 1997, under conditions in which domestic consumption was weak and enterprises’ desire to invest was not strong, the centre began to raise its capacity to regulate and control the economy, and to enlarge the proportion of fiscal revenue in GDP.

Figure 4-14: The Trend of Change in the Proportion of Fiscal Revenue to GDP

[Figure image: image91.wmf] Line chart, 1950–2009, of the ratio of fiscal revenue to GDP. The ratio fluctuates around 0.27–0.32 before 1978, with a peak of about 0.40 in 1961 and a dip in 1969, then declines steadily to a trough of about 0.11 in the mid-1990s before recovering to about 0.20 by 2009.

The result of doing this was that China rapidly walked out of the Asian financial crisis and, under conditions in which the value of the renminbi remained stable, realised sustained economic growth. At the same time it is worth noting that at this time the motive forces of growth, besides government stimulus and the growth of the trade sector, did not include any visible contribution of consumption and domestic demand to the economy — that is, from this point in time onwards, central finance once again became an important force regulating and controlling China’s economic development. This gradually strengthening force’s “crowding-out effect” on enterprises and consumers was mainly displayed and completed through the behaviour of government expenditure.

After the Asian financial crisis, in order to guarantee the speed of economic growth, the government enlarged the force of its investment, in directions that were basically the construction of infrastructure, such as highways, railways, and docks; although the construction of such public infrastructure is indeed one of the government’s economic responsibilities, the deficiencies in the construction of public welfare systems in such respects concerning the people’s livelihood as the construction of indemnificatory housing, investment in education funds, and medical reform caused latent hidden dangers for the Chinese economy, and were especially very insufficient for the expansion of consumption and domestic demand. Thus, in respect of infrastructure construction, government investment crowded out part of the demand of enterprises to invest autonomously through market behaviour; on the other hand, consumers, whose cost of living was increasing, under conditions in which the supply of the public welfare security system was seriously insufficient, could only be forced to reduce consumption and increase saving in order to guard against the various uncertainties that might occur in the future. So the government’s influence on consumption and domestic demand in both respects, revenue and expenditure, was negative — that is, it produced a serious “crowding-out effect”.

4.5.3 The Relationship between External Demand and Domestic Demand: Weak Domestic Demand Made Up by External Demand

Today the global economy is integrated and the division of labour unfolds at the level of states; China naturally participates in it too. Since reform and opening, China, relying on its cheap labour costs, has attracted a large quantity of the world’s processing and manufacturing industry to settle in China; while raising the level of China’s manufacturing industry, this also caused more of the products produced in China to be exported abroad in exchange for a large trade surplus, and caused many of China’s coastal outward-oriented cities to develop at flying speed. This unbalanced domestic development demand and the foreign countries’ large demand for China’s cheap products became mutually stimulating forces, forming China’s present situation of attending to external demand and neglecting domestic demand.

[Figure image: image92.wmf] Line chart, 1978–2009, of the contribution rates (per cent) of final consumption expenditure, gross capital formation and net exports of goods and services. The consumption series is generally the largest in the earlier years and fluctuates between about 33 and 97 per cent; the capital-formation series rises over time; the net-exports series is volatile around or below zero and plunges at the end of the period.

The figure reflects the ratios of the contributions of consumption and domestic demand, capital formation, and the trade sector to the economic aggregate. Among these, the most obvious relation between domestic demand and external demand is that they “wax and wane against each other”: every high point of growth in external demand is a low point in domestic demand’s share, and every peak of domestic demand necessarily corresponds to a trough of external demand.

Besides the real economy, the value of the renminbi also changed as its trade status changed. From 1979 to 1984 China experienced the change from a single exchange rate to a dual exchange rate and back to a single exchange rate, while before 1993 a dual exchange rate system was practised; after the exchange rate reform of 1994 a managed floating exchange rate system has been practised to this day. In the period during which the exchange rate system was changing, the renminbi’s exchange rate experienced a “U”-shaped change from high to low and back to a high position: taking the rate against the US dollar as an example, it fell from 293.66 in 1985 to 861.87 in 1994, and then gradually appreciated, reaching 683.1 in 2009. This trajectory of the renminbi’s value, first depreciating and then appreciating, tallies with China’s strategy of developing an outward-oriented economy: the period of the renminbi’s depreciation was precisely the period in which China’s foreign trade was starting and flourishing, and when the products made in China had occupied foreign markets, the renminbi began, logically, to appreciate according to the rules of the market. Although ordinary domestic consumers cannot feel the benefits of the appreciation of their own currency, there is no doubt that such an exchange rate policy did indeed help China accumulate a large amount of foreign exchange wealth, and at the same time helped China realise upgrading more quickly in respect of industrial development.

Combining the consumption rate data, we can also sum up still more implications. Before 1992, when the consumption rate was comparatively high, the manufacturing level of domestic enterprises was not high, and most products basically had no competitiveness internationally, so developing the domestic market was the only path. Especially since domestic consumption, after being suppressed for a long time, was released in large quantities, a situation was created in which, even though product quality was not high, it was still a seller’s market, and because demand was excessive serious inflation was even caused in the monetary sphere. But as China’s manufacturing capacity increased and the growth of the domestic market became limited, enterprises began, under the guidance of the outward-oriented economic strategy, to “go out” and open up foreign markets, and the broad external market opened up through low costs made enterprises attend first to external demand rather than domestic demand. So the main cause of this relation of mutual waxing and waning between China’s domestic demand and external demand is the fluctuation of external demand — that is, domestic demand is developed to supplement it only after external demand has first fluctuated.

This situation of neglecting consumption and domestic demand was never improved, and in the financial crisis that broke out in 2008 the contradiction between domestic demand and external demand manifested itself once again. When the United States and Europe, China’s two principal trading partners, fell into crisis, causing China’s exports to fall substantially, domestic demand once again became the focus of attention. After the Asian financial crisis there was an opportunity to improve the environment for domestic demand, but the government did not shift the focus of development; this crisis has once again become an opportunity for transformation.

4.5.4 The Role of Consumption and Domestic Demand in China’s Economic Growth

Consumption is the ultimate purpose of economic development; the raising of consumers’ utility can only be attained through the consumption of products. Although for a sovereign state developing trade and expanding exports can indirectly increase welfare, these are still means. The significance of consumption for a country’s economic development is beyond doubt. In the initial stage of the developed countries’ development, the consumption rate also passed through a period of low level, but after passing through this stage the consumption rate basically stabilised at a comparatively high level.

Figure 4-16: The Consumption Rates of US Residents and Government

[Figure image: image93.wmf] Line chart, 1960–2009, of two United States series: the residents’ consumption rate rises slowly and steadily from about 64 per cent to about 73 per cent, while the government consumption rate declines gradually from a peak of about 31 per cent around 1970 to about 22–26 per cent.

Looking back at China’s consumption data, no such phenomenon is displayed: only before 1992 was a fairly high level maintained, basically higher than 60 per cent, with individual years (such as 1989) lower than 50 per cent. After 1992, as China’s marketization construction accelerated, this figure then declined year by year, maintaining an average of around 40 per cent. The residents’ consumption rate in the United States above, by contrast, was always maintained above 60 per cent, rising after 2000 to as high as above 70 per cent, while the government’s consumption rate never broke through 30 per cent; and after entering the 1980s, the residents’ consumption rate also displayed a trend of gradual rise while the government’s consumption rate slowly declined. Relative to China’s year-by-year declining consumption rate, consumption and domestic demand in the United States are indeed the most core motive force driving its economic growth: once domestic consumption is weak, the risk of a decline in the speed of US economic growth is great. For China, by contrast, consumption has never borne the role of driving growth; instead, economic growth is very sensitive to external demand and investment. This state of affairs has already in fact departed from the ultimate purpose of economic development, and at the same time it has become the realistic basis for China’s expansion of consumption and domestic demand and transformation of its mode of growth.

From the standpoint of the micro agents, China’s households have already walked out of the survival and subsistence stages and are gradually beginning to enter the stage of moderately prosperous life and the pursuit of quality of life. At this stage, households’ consumption demands are no longer the most basic articles of life, nor durable consumer goods such as televisions and refrigerators, but consumption at a higher level, such as housing, motor cars, and better education and medical care. The content of these consumption demands is rich and their prices also higher, so under conditions in which household income gradually rises it is necessary, by innovating the means and models of consumption, to satisfy households’ consumption demands, and at the same time to make economic growth and the consumption model match each other and form a virtuous cycle. The enrichment of consumption content first causes production enterprises to increase; the increase in supply induced by this growth in demand makes a very marked contribution to the economy; at the same time, on the supply side, because the market faced is enormous, the refinement of the division of labour and the increase in supply further increase the economic aggregate. On the other hand, the economic growth brought by technological innovation also cannot be neglected; in particular, the changes in consumption hotspots led by innovation in the information field have caused “Say’s law” to revive. These changes on both the supply and demand sides play a supremely important role in China’s economic growth, so the motive force on which China’s further growth is to rely still comes from both consumption and domestic demand and production and supply; especially when productive capacity is relatively excessive, how to increase consumption and domestic demand is the breakthrough point for the sustained and healthy growth of China’s economy.

4.6 The Cash Consumption Model Is Incompatible with China’s Marketization Process

After the importance of consumption has been clarified, how to increase consumption and domestic demand becomes the most core question. Of course, income and the employment questions related to income, and the questions of the primary and secondary distribution of national income, all need focused analysis, while the analytical target that can combine these factors and display them as a whole is the object of this dissertation’s analysis, the consumption model. The consumption model is an organic combination of various factors such as the macroeconomic structure and micro household income and expenditure, and there are many concrete ways of combining them, among which the cash consumption model and the balance-sheet consumption model are the two most basic models. The characteristics of the cash consumption model have already been discussed above; then why must this consumption model be changed — that is, in which respects and at what levels does the cash consumption model, after all, influence China’s economy? This dissertation holds that the incompatibility of the cash consumption model with the market economy is mainly manifested in the following respects: first, the cash model can only tally with a relatively low level of economic development; it cannot satisfy households’ high-level consumption demands, nor can it satisfy the requirements of economic growth; second, the cash consumption model itself displays instability and a “stage-based character” in respect of the consumption process; third, the cash consumption model is not conducive to the expansion of consumption and domestic demand; and finally, this model cannot effectively withstand some market risks.

For the cash consumption model to be called a “model”, it must necessarily be a system, formed by the combination of various related factors, that can be identified and distinguished from other organisational forms; it is because of this characteristic that the cash consumption model is adapted to a low-level mode of development.

First, under the cash consumption model, the aggregate income of residents or households is markedly on the low side, and this becomes the decisive factor in the formation of the consumption model at the undeveloped stage of the economy. Whether a market economy or a planned economy, the difference in organisational form does not exclude the fact that all economies must pass through an initial stage. The capitalist countries also experienced a stage of low income and low consumption in the initial period of economic take-off.

In the 1960s the hourly wage of an ordinary manufacturing worker in the United States was about 2 US dollars; by 2009 wages had reached 18 US dollars an hour, whereas in the initial period of the American industrial revolution, that is, in the mid-nineteenth century, 2 dollars was approximately a worker’s income for a day’s work. Under such income conditions, the consumption of products such as motor cars, televisions, and refrigerators still depended principally on saving. Only after consumer loans, this new form of consumer finance, appeared did loans become the principal means by which the majority of American households bought and replaced consumer goods. It can be imagined that before this new consumption instrument appeared, the financing means a household could adopt in order to buy a sewing machine or a Ford Model T were only saving and borrowing among acquaintances. In this way, a comparatively long time was needed for products, from being produced until they were transferred into consumers’ hands, because only households with higher income and sufficient saving could complete payment; enterprises’ capital turnover speed was therefore comparatively slow, and at the same time the speed at which households’ welfare level rose was also comparatively slow. Ultimately, slowness in both production and consumption caused enterprises’ capital accumulation to be slow and finally, at the macro level, made the level of economic development not high.

It may be said that this model, which relies principally on labour income and one’s own saving to complete consumption payments, and a low level of economy are mutually conditional. Because the level of economic development is low, investment needs to be increased, so the price of capital must be relatively high in order to attract capital to form productive forces; naturally, labour income relatively declines, thereby influencing the raising of the consumption level. On the other hand, in the initial stage of economic development, the consumption structure has as yet no requirement of further elevation, so the content of consumption is simple and its value relatively low, which also affirms, from the demand side, the reasonableness of the existence of the cash consumption model. The joint action of these two aspects, each the manifestation of the other, is an important characteristic of the cash consumption model.

But as the economy develops and the level of manufacturing rises, enterprises’ output growth rises steadily, so in quantitative terms how to digest finished products becomes the foundation of enterprises’ further development; in the pioneering of technological innovation, the appearance of new products becomes an important path by which enterprises obtain new space for development, and these products are often comparatively expensive, so how to enable ordinary households to pay for new products with limited income also becomes a new problem faced by enterprises; finally, for households, gradually raising the standard of living — the transition from adequate food and clothing towards moderate prosperity and the pursuit of quality of life — poses, as a practical problem in the sphere of consumption, the question of how to possess the consumer goods representing the corresponding standard of living; so the upgrading of the consumption structure is not only the demand of the product supply side but also the demand of the product demand side. Under such circumstances, income and means of payment and the like under the cash consumption model cannot match it, and instead become a force obstructing the further growth of the economy.
