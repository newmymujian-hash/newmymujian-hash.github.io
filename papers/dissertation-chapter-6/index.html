Chapter 6 The Risks and Obstacles in Developing the Balance-Sheet Consumption Model

As the economy develops and people’s living standards rise, and as the content of consumer goods grows richer and their quality improves, the transformation of the consumption model is in fact a process that occurs naturally as the market economy and free competition gradually deepen — especially once assets and large consumer goods have gradually become the principal objects of consumption. Yet this process of transformation, which ought to “occur naturally”, often fails to take shape because of various risks and obstacles. Since China is to establish a sound operating mechanism and environment for a market economy, realising the transformation of the consumption model in accordance with the laws of the market economy is the road that must be travelled; removing the various obstacles that impede the transformation of the consumption model and reducing the risks of the transformation thus become major problems that must be solved in setting out on this road. It has been demonstrated above that, under the cash-flow consumption model, consumption displays unstable volatility and consumers’ utility improves only slowly, and that these restrict the potential of economic development from both the supply side and the demand side. The balance-sheet consumption model is a correction of, and an evolution from, the cash-flow model; but having resolved the old contradictions it gives rise to new problems — namely, that matters such as the cash flow, asset returns and bankruptcy risk that an enterprise faces in its operations become important elements in consumers’ safe operation and even in macroeconomic security and stability. Analysing the types, magnitudes and movements of the risks internal to the balance-sheet consumption model is therefore a necessary condition for demonstrating that the transformation of the consumption model can succeed; and analysing the actual state of existence of these conditions is at the same time an important step in removing the obstacles to the transformation of the consumption model.

Under the cash-flow consumption model, consumers run almost no risk in maintaining the health of their balance sheets, because consumption behaviour that strictly “measures expenditure by income” and relies on saving determines that consumers cannot be exposed to the risk of bankruptcy, or of a shortage of cash flow that leaves them unable to pay debts as they fall due — although this “safety” is bought at the price of sacrificing the maximisation of utility. In the balance-sheet consumption model, there are liabilities of every kind under the heading of liabilities, and the debt structure and the term structure of debt are not uniform; a cash flow of a corresponding structure is therefore needed to service them, and this servicing must be well matched in both quantity and time if “bankruptcy” is to be avoided. This gives rise to risks that never existed under the old model: fluctuations in income, fluctuations in the securities market, fluctuations in real estate prices, fluctuations in expenditure on medical care, education and the like, fluctuations in the prices of ordinary consumer goods, changes in consumption policy, and so on, can all have a real effect on the health of the balance sheet. It may be said that the original consumption model exchanged lower utility for safety, whereas the new model appropriately raises risk while at the same time raising utility; whether the balance-sheet consumption model can evolve naturally therefore ultimately requires that the part by which utility is raised be greater than the negative utility generated by risk. Minimising risk, and controlling the level of risk, are thus at the core of the transformation of the consumption model.

6.1 Fluctuations in Cash Inflows

The significance of cash inflows for maintaining a healthy balance sheet needs no further emphasis. It may be said that, so long as cash inflows are sufficient, there is no essential difference between the balance-sheet consumption model and the cash-flow consumption model, because the consumer can immediately repay all debts in cash and thereby transform back into the cash-flow model; in that case, the reason for holding debt is rather that the cost of debt is “cheaper” relative to the “discount rate”. In reality, however, debt is held not because it is “cheap” but because cash flow is insufficient; and cash inflows, besides, fluctuate continually with changes in the external macroeconomic environment, which makes it possible for indebtedness to become the “normal state”. In these circumstances, maintaining stable cash inflows in accordance with one’s debt position becomes an important aspect of safe operation, and so stabilising those items that can bring in cash inflows is of the greatest importance. For the individual consumer these mainly include the following: labour income and the policy on the distribution of income, returns on financial assets, returns on real estate investment, and so on.

Looking at the overall level, the performance of the secondary industry is relatively stable; the primary industry was negative for several consecutive years in the initial period of marketization and only afterwards turned positive, with a fairly large amplitude of fluctuation in the elasticity; the employment elasticity of the tertiary industry has always been greater than zero, and its average value is relatively high. This shows that the tertiary industry is indeed the main part absorbing employment; but because elasticity cuts both ways, when the economy is in the doldrums the proportion of unemployment caused will also be relatively high. The stable development of the tertiary industry is therefore of great significance for employment in China. Combining this with data on economic growth, one also finds that the trends of economic growth and employment growth do not coincide; but it is nevertheless a fact that, as the process of marketization has accelerated, there have appeared a development of private enterprises inferior to that of state-owned enterprises, a decline in the capacity to absorb employment, and at the same time a level of wages of the staff and workers of private enterprises lower than that of state-owned enterprises.

The analysis above is the present state of China’s employment pattern; logically it belongs to results. Only by analysing the causes that produced these results and putting forward solutions can China’s employment situation be effectively improved and the problems of slow income growth and of unbalanced incomes between different industries and between enterprises of different natures be solved in essence. This dissertation divides these causes into the following aspects: the urban–rural relationship, the east–west relationship, and the relationship between government and enterprises — that is, between state-owned enterprises and private enterprises.

First, one prominent manifestation of China’s reform and opening up and of the process of marketization is urbanisation. The process of urbanisation is generally carried out, and accelerated, as industrialisation’s demand for labour grows; but China’s system of separate urban and rural household registration management artificially divides the labour force into different groups, and this directly leads to discrimination in income and welfare. For labour with urban household registration, the same work means not only income but also statutory welfare guarantees, whereas for those with rural household registration there are no corresponding welfare guarantees, and even the obtaining of lawful labour income is fraught with difficulties. At the same time, rural labour cannot enjoy the services provided by the city’s basic public sectors, such as education for their children. In particular, on the premise that the supply of these public goods is deficient, price discrimination of various forms exists universally, and expenditure in these respects markedly raises the cost of living of rural labourers in the city, becoming an obstacle that crowds rural labourers out of urban life, and at the same time an obstacle to China’s urbanisation and to the balanced development of town and countryside. The core of the contradiction in China’s urban–rural relationship at present is therefore the household registration system: the free movement of factors required by marketization and the artificial restrictions of the household registration system have become the cause of the unbalanced development of town and countryside, and also an important cause of the urban–rural income gap. China should therefore abolish the household registration system as early as possible, establish a welfare guarantee system covering the whole people, weaken the labourer’s status and strengthen the labourer’s ability, let the market become the mechanism that regulates the supply of and demand for labour, and let wages become the lever that prises labour mobility loose; only then can labourers’ incomes accord with the process of marketization and with the process of urbanisation, the urban–rural gap narrow, and labourers’ consumption model be transformed into the balance-sheet type suited to the market process. On the other hand, considering the angle of absorbing employment: as the level of agricultural mechanisation in China rises and science and technology are applied in agricultural production, the potentially unemployed population of the countryside grows ever larger, and for these people to pour into the cities becomes an inevitable choice; balancing urban and rural development is therefore to let these people be able to find employment and live in the cities.

Second, the unbalanced development of China’s east and west is constrained not only by natural conditions but also by policy and institutions. Because in the initial period of reform China emphasised the development of foreign trade, the eastern coastal region, relying on its geographical advantage, developed first and set out on the track of growth, and the average income of eastern labourers is also markedly higher than that of the west; it may be said that the rise in incomes is precisely the result of the market playing its role. Eliminating the obstacles between east and west therefore means letting the market play its role on a nationwide scale, especially in regulating the movement of resources between east and west. In addition, because the east holds the first-mover advantage in market development, the government should support the west in policy and raise the bargaining power of western cities in the market. As labour costs in the east rise and the cost of living increases, the west becomes an important direction for the industrial transfer of eastern manufacturing; this is beneficial for maintaining the cost advantage of China’s products, increasing the west’s capacity to absorb labour [see Translator’s Notes], and raising the incomes of western labourers. In facing the foreign market and the domestic west, therefore, the government should increase policy support and guidance and expand the domestic market.

Finally, the relationship between government and enterprises and the relationship between state-owned enterprises and private enterprises are identical in essence. Since the reform of the fiscal and taxation systems, the government’s fiscal revenue has grown by a large margin and at a speed exceeding the growth of the economy, while state-owned enterprises, after experiencing a short period of being in the doldrums, have once again become the most important component part of China’s economic pattern. State-owned enterprises, as an important carrier by which the state holds economic strength, have indeed contributed markedly to the economy; but as state-owned enterprises have developed, private enterprises have faced various obstacles in their development. This state of affairs, which does not accord with the process of marketization, is both the cause of the fall in the incomes of the labourers of private enterprises and the cause of the gradual decline in private enterprises’ capacity to absorb employment. The industries in which private enterprises are located are basically labour-intensive, so returning to the road of developing private enterprises is beneficial for raising employment and thereby raising the level of income. Income is the foundation of consumption and the precondition of the transformation of the consumption model, and employment is the foundation of income; stabilising the total volume of employment first is therefore an important step in guaranteeing the transformation of the consumption model. In order to raise the total volume of employment, the several pairs of relationships above must be handled well — continuing to encourage the development of private enterprises, breaking the artificial urban–rural division, and balancing the development of east and west — so that employment is promoted and the transformation of the consumption model and a rise in the consumption rate can truly be realised.

In employment, besides the macroeconomic risks above that affect employment and thereby affect the cash inflow of income, micro risks are also an aspect that needs to be analysed with emphasis. This dissertation divides the employment risks in this respect into two main aspects: first, the risk of knowledge mismatch; second, the risk of first employment. The employment mismatch risk refers to the risk of mismatch that may arise, because of a time lag, between the knowledge learned by labourers who are still in the period of receiving employment training and the future demands of the labour market. This risk is related to the degree of development and perfection of the education system and the labour market. The risk of first employment refers to the risk of unemployment that exists when one first enters the labour market.

Before entering the labour market, labourers need to receive education and training; besides the mastery of basic knowledge, the accumulation and investment of specialised knowledge become important factors in whether employment can be obtained smoothly. But the individual can only decide the direction of intellectual investment through the current employment situation, preferences and ability. It may be said that this investment is a “venture investment”, so that when the investment is completed there may exist a risk of unemployment arising from a mismatch of specialised knowledge. Although the government can give guidance in the development of industries and of specialisms, the individual’s manner of decision-making is still short-sighted — that is, the direction of investment in a specialism is decided according to current average incomes and one’s own ability. The industries with relatively high incomes in China at present are still concentrated in finance, securities and telecommunications, so, looking at the heat of the choice of specialisms, competition relating to these industries is fairly fierce, whereas specialisms relating to the future development of environmental protection, green energy and the like are not fierce. This illustrates the short-sightedness of labourers in investing in specialisms. Because this short-sightedness exists, it may well cause the knowledge mastered by labourers after they have completed their educational investment not to match the knowledge required by the market, creating a latent risk — for example, an intensification of competition causing incomes in the industry to fall, or even unemployment through failure in competition.

In order to resist the above risk, the method generally adopted by competitors is to mobilise more resources besides specialised knowledge to compete for the limited posts — that is, to create fierce, and even vicious, competition in the employment market. Vicious competition takes many forms; in China it is mainly more “back doors and connections”. What is more important is that the “Matthew effect” often occurs in this matter: that is, a certain job-seeker originally made an investment in human capital and strove to enter this industry precisely because he had no such resources available, but in the end it is rather those who originally already possessed many resources who enter this industry in greater numbers. The Matthew effect often occurs frequently between generations. This risk has already become a stubborn ailment of China’s employment market: on the one hand it affects China’s education system, and on the other it affects fairness, causing poverty to accumulate between generations. But in the final analysis, the cause of this phenomenon is still the imbalance of incomes between industries and the short-sightedness of educational investment. Thoroughly avoiding this employment risk and waste of talent requires balancing the development of industries and the levels of income; on the other hand, it requires educating labourers to examine the costs and benefits of investment in human capital and to treat educational investment more rationally.

Combining the investigation of employment and income at the macro and the micro level, China’s current employment problem, the problem of raising labourers’ incomes, and ultimately the contradictions existing in the sphere of consumption are, in the end, a manifestation of the contradictions in the spheres of China’s industrial development and income distribution. Labour resources, like capital, pursue high returns, and generate fierce competition in whichever field that is. Solving China’s low consumption rate and developing the balance-sheet consumption model therefore still, in the end, require reform in the sphere of distribution.

6.1.2 Fluctuations in the Securities Market and the Risky Income from Financial Assets

An important sign of the transformation from the cash-flow model to the balance-sheet model is that, under the heading of assets, consumers have added “short-term investment” assets besides saving, among which financial assets are included. Under market conditions, capital, like labour, can bring cash flow income; and according to the individual’s preference for risk, the proportion of this part of cash flow in total cash flow also differs, but looking at the whole, as China’s process of marketization gradually deepens, its coverage among consumers grows ever wider and the proportion it occupies in total assets also gradually rises. The development and fluctuation of the securities market thus gradually become an important aspect affecting consumers’ cash flow. At the same time, because the securities market is a market for risk capital, the prices and returns of securities change unceasingly; although their returns may be higher than those of a risk-free asset such as saving, if the risk factor is taken into account the final result of the return is not that high. Considering the problem of cash flow and consumption together, the risk of the securities market affects the cash flow of consumers holding securities, and thereby causes the volume of consumption to change. Analysing the fluctuation of the securities market and the magnitude of its risk is therefore an important aspect in researching the transformation of the consumption model.

Blanchard (Oliver J. Blanchard) holds that the relationship between fluctuation in the stock market and aggregate income and aggregate expenditure is determined by the following equations, in which $d$ is aggregate expenditure, $q$ the aggregate market value of the stock market, $y$ aggregate income, and $g$ an index of fiscal policy:

$$d = \alpha q + \beta y + g$$

in which

$\alpha > 0$, $\beta \in (0,1)$.

Blanchard points out that current and expected production are the main factors determining the value of assets; conversely, changes in the value of assets will also affect consumption expenditure and thereby affect output, and the government’s economic policy will also, through interest rates, the volume of money issued, aggregate demand and the like, cause income to change while at the same time affecting the value of assets — among which, of course, is the value of “short-term investment”. The above relationships among macroeconomic variables can be corroborated by the relevant data for the United States. Tong Jiandong once made a regression analysis of American share prices and GDP over the 100 years from 1889 to 1989, and found that the relationship between the two is close and the coefficient of determination large; in particular, there is also an association between fluctuation in the stock market and the economic cycle, fluctuation in the stock market preceding fluctuation in the industrial cycle by four months on average, so that the stock market can become a device giving early warning of macroeconomic fluctuation.

The above is a theoretical hypothesis concerning the macro whole; for the micro individual it is similar. The consumer’s consumption expenditure depends on labour income and investment income; if the government’s subsidies to the individual are not considered, the individual’s consumption equation can be written in the following form:

$$c = \alpha q + \beta y$$

in which

$\alpha > 0$, $\beta \in (0,1)$.

So a change in income, or fluctuation in the stock market, will both have a direct effect on consumption; and labour income fluctuates less than the returns of the stock market. For most households labour income is permanent income and the return on short-term investment is temporary income, so $\alpha$ should be smaller than $\beta$. This model is a fitting expression of the balance-sheet consumption model, because it includes not only the direct effect of income on consumption but also the effect of asset wealth on consumption. The stock market’s effect on consumption is produced mainly through the following channel: fluctuation in the stock market → fluctuation in the value and returns of financial assets → fluctuation in cash flow → bankruptcy risk → fluctuation in consumption. The data for the United States in this respect, likewise, can partly explain the rationality of the hypotheses concerning micro individual economic variables. From 1992 to 1998 the American economy grew continuously and the stock market performed well; during this period private investment was stably higher than saving, the reason being that private income entered the stock market directly or indirectly instead of being transformed into saving; and during this period a continuous increase in asset returns brought a continuous growth in consumption, which became an important source of American economic growth. It is worth noting that because the risk factor had not been accounted for in this, it also buried a hidden peril for the later American financial crisis.

For China, according to the conclusions of Li Zhenming’s (2001) research on the relationship between changes in China’s stock market and consumption:

considering financial assets only, consumption $c = 0.01707A_t + 0.6446Y_t$, and $R^2 = 0.9934$;

considering all assets, consumption $c = 0.02567A_t + 0.6285Y_t$, and $R^2 = 0.9936$.

Looking at the econometric conclusions, they first confirm the assertion that the coefficient of change in financial assets is smaller than the coefficient of change in labour income; second, looking at the size of the coefficients, the effect of assets on consumption seems not very obvious. There are several reasons for this. First, China’s market for short-term financial investment is dominated by the stock market, and there is a clear gap between the maturity of China’s stock market and that of the stock markets of developed market countries, which makes the stock market strongly speculative; investors entering the market all enter with a mentality of short-term speculation, so the funds entering the market are not large and the returns obtained are consequently limited. Second, China’s per capita economic aggregate is limited, individuals’ incomes and total assets are limited and unevenly distributed, and all of this means that the number of people in a position to enter the short-term investment market is limited.

Synthesising the above analysis: the entry of short-term financial assets such as shares into the individual’s balance sheet is a road that must be travelled in the transformation of the consumption model; and because of the positive effect of the asset wealth effect on consumption, China must further perfect the management and operating methods of its stock and securities markets, reduce the speculativeness of the stock market, and as far as possible moderate its fluctuation, so that the results of macroeconomic development can, through this market, become an important channel promoting an increase in household cash flow, and thereby become an important factor stimulating a rise in the consumption rate and the transformation of the consumption model.

6.1.3 The Risk to Income from Real Estate Investment

The importance of real property to the consumer is manifested not only in its being able directly to increase utility, but also in that, because real property has the dual attributes of an investment good and a consumer good, its character as an asset and its investment returns are also important aspects of real property’s influence on consumer behaviour. Analysis of real property therefore cannot be separated from its duality. The consumer character of real property has a simple origin: housing is the most basic requirement of life, like food and clothing; in this respect a house is of no great difference, as to the nature of the commodity, from food and clothing. But because consumers do not move house frequently within a fairly long period, housing as a consumer good is usually bought once and consumed for a long time, until it needs to be rebuilt. In addition, the consumer character of housing is manifested in its being a building on the ground: when it is rebuilt, only the construction cost of the house itself needs to be paid — that is, the cost of consuming the house itself — the land rent already being included in the price paid the first time. Finally, as a consumer good, one set of housing is usually all a household needs; only when the size of the household expands because of descendants is more housing needed. The capital and investment character of real property derives mainly from the natural attribute of the immovability of houses and the attribute of land ownership. We know that investment itself means giving up the utility of current consumption over a fairly long period in exchange for future returns, so the relative stability of the residential character of housing over a long period of existence makes it a product with good investment character. At the same time, the unchangeability of the geographical position of land or housing becomes the source of “land rent”, and also becomes an important reason why real property becomes an investment good. In China’s housing market, housing displays investment character more, and consumer character only second; analysing China’s housing market therefore requires at the same time analysing the level of supply and demand for the housing entity, and still more analysing the influence of the money market and of “rent” in the composition of house prices.

The entry of housing into the consumer’s balance sheet not only means a rise in the consumer’s utility, but also means the beginning of the transformation of the consumption model. Letting real property become an item under consumers’ assets and liabilities as quickly as possible is therefore an important content of building a harmonious society. But China’s house prices have long been in a rising channel, more and more consumers cannot afford to buy a house, and in some large cities consumers cannot even afford to rent one; how exactly was such a reality formed? How does it need to be corrected? How can the combination of housing consumption and the transformation of the consumption model be realised? As an investment good, what are housing’s investment risks, and its effects on household cash income and on the balance sheet?

This dissertation holds that the causes of China’s high house prices have both aggregate demand factors and structural factors. As the process of marketization has accelerated, and as the level of industrial upgrading and of urbanisation has risen, not only has the movement of population between cities accelerated, but the rural population has also flowed into the cities in large numbers. In the era of the planned economy this part of the population was institutionally confined to the countryside; but because of the needs of economic development and the loosening of institutions, this part of the population has gradually begun to merge into the cities, and this has generated demand for housing. On the other hand, because of the acceleration of the movement of population between cities and the need to raise living standards, the small family with a married couple at its core has become the most important mode of family life in China, and this mode of life has also directly generated demand for housing. Finally, because China’s population base is large and the average life expectancy of the population has lengthened markedly, the total population has for a long time still been increasing unceasingly, which slows the speed at which real property is transferred between generations and thereby increases the new generation’s demand for housing. These three aspects become the most principal aspects of housing demand in China; and considering the rigidity of housing demand, it may be said that lowering prices through the management of aggregate demand is neither realistic nor rational.

That the management of aggregate demand is hard to realise does not, however, mean that house prices are rigid, because the formation of house prices in China is more principally a matter of structural factors: it is jointly determined by demand, supply, liquidity, government behaviour and the external economic environment. These factors are the direct propellers of the formation of house prices in China; and because these factors have elasticity, they have room for adjustment. At the same time, adjustability means that the risk is large; so, in analysing the relationship between these factors and the causes of house prices below, emphasis will also be placed on analysing the effect that the adoption of adjustment measures has on the security of the balance sheets of consumers, enterprises and the government.

Looking at the total volume and structure of China’s population: from the founding of the country to the present, apart from individual years in which China’s birth rate fell, the rate of natural increase has been stable and slowly declining. The continuous increase in the total population is mainly caused by the base being too large. In the total population, those under 14 years of age decrease year by year, while those over 65 are stable with some increase; although the total volume of the labouring population is increasing, considering the proportions of dependency, in fact the old-age dependency ratio of the labouring population is increasing year by year while the child dependency coefficient is declining year by year. This trend of change in the population shows, on the one hand, that China has already begun to enter an ageing society, and on the other hand — combined with the fact that the movement of labour is accelerating — it can be inferred that this part of the labouring population has a real demand for housing. The statistical data on household size also show that Chinese households fell from 3.7 persons per household in 1995 to 3.16 persons per household in 2008. This data clearly shows that the situation of “a couple plus one child” living together and jointly using one set of housing has become a universal phenomenon in China, and especially so in the cities. This gradual departure from the large family, with several generations living together, has already been dissolved by the development of the modern market economy, so the great majority of China’s labourers are now rigid demanders of housing. This part of demand can no longer be solved by three generations sharing one set of housing; this is the key point of China’s housing problem.

Looking at the supply side, China’s system of housing supply and demand has experienced an institutional change from a welfare allocation system to commercialisation, but China’s commercial housing has nevertheless retained its particularity: housing is privately owned, but the land is still state-owned. Although this institutional arrangement is a manifestation of China’s socialist public ownership, it causes a defect in the transaction of commercial housing itself in China: a land use right of 70 years cannot fully guarantee that the building on the ground and the private commercial housing are completely privatised; if, when the use right expires [see Translator’s Notes], the land use right is taken back, even if compensation is given, it is the individual who suffers the loss. It is precisely this short-term character that determines that, in China’s current supply side of commercial housing, the government and the developers price jointly: the government sells the land use right and gives it to the developers to operate, and in the end the consumer obtains only a housing use contract and an options contract with a term attached. At the present stage, as China’s process of urbanisation accelerates and large amounts of labour pour into the cities needing housing, this lease and option are “bullish”; but after 70 years, as the not-yet-expired real property contracts left by the part of the labouring population that now already owns housing increase as those people pass away, as the labourers owning housing increase with the gradual perfection of the process of urbanisation, and as China’s total population and total labouring population stabilise and even decrease, all of these will become factors making this special housing commodity contract and option in China “bearish”. The risks constituted by these factors cannot yet constitute the core problem considered by house-buyers in the short term, but they cannot be ignored in theoretical analysis.

Because the land is state-owned and the government is the agent of the state, government behaviour also becomes an important factor in changes in China’s house prices, and the government’s attitude towards and policies on real estate are also one of the sources of risk in the fluctuation of China’s house prices and of housing asset prices. After the tax reform of 1994, the fiscal power of local governments was withdrawn; although the state’s fiscal strength has since strengthened rapidly year by year, apart from individual developed provinces the fiscal revenue of local governments decreased, and the more grass-roots the government, the more this was so.

Such a system gives the government no economic motive to lower land prices, so consumers must pay for the ownership of state-owned land that in law originally belongs to themselves, and must pay again after 70 years in order to continue to use it. In order to lower house prices and the risk of a first house purchase, reform should therefore be carried out in respect of land ownership, returning to consumers the rights that originally belong to each consumer; this is the core problem of lowering house prices. Awareness of this problem, and reform of it, are also an important component part of the risk of real estate investment: whether the present policy continues to be maintained or an attempt at land privatisation is made, both will produce fluctuation in the formation of house prices, and the amplitude of the fluctuation is closely related to the concrete policy. Besides the risk of land policy, the risks of a first house purchase include such things as the differential rent arising from differences in the degree of development of cities, and the differential rent within cities, which also gestate enormous risk. China’s unbalanced development of east and west is most obvious in house prices: eastern cities are economically developed and their populations gather, so house prices are high, and the west is the opposite; a similar situation also occurs within each city. Balancing the development of east and west and rationally planning the layout of cities are therefore important steps in reducing differential rent and promoting the rationalisation of house prices. The formation of differential rent in China is not a matter of the single factor of capital investment in urban construction; it includes comprehensive factors such as employment, education and the level of urban management services. That is why we can see the phenomenon of newly built high-grade buildings in many western cities that nobody buys or lives in, while housing in developed cities still rises in price unceasingly. Balancing the development of east and west, and balancing the levels of urban development, therefore requires not only capital but still more a balance in social resources, especially the equality of educational resources and the perfection of the social security system. The crowd buying houses in developed cities is in fact expecting the present system to undergo no change, and that in the future not only will housing rents still rise but option prices will also continue to rise; policy risk is contained within this. This risk is also a factor that first-time house-buyers need to consider.

For consumers who have already bought a house, risk is manifested mainly in two aspects: first, the risk of fluctuation in the total value of the asset; and second [see Translator’s Notes], the risk of fluctuation in the returns of the asset. The first kind of risk refers to a change in macroeconomic policy causing the differential rent already occupied when the house was bought to dissipate, thereby causing the total price of the house to fall — for example, if the citizen status, the convenience of children’s schooling, the convenience of medical treatment, and so on, that came attached to the purchase of a house change because a new policy is issued, this will certainly lead to the dissipation of rent and a fall in house prices. The second kind of risk refers to the risk arising from the rate of return of the housing asset gradually lowering because of changes in the external economic environment, or from the cost of holding it gradually rising — for example, because a rise in the rate of return on investment in the real economy causes capital to leave the real estate industry, a relative lowering of the rate of return on investment in housing will lead to an increase in consumers’ bankruptcy risk, and this is all the more so for consumers who buy houses with loans.

Synthesising the above analysis of the various risks of housing assets, it may be said that the various manifestations of China’s housing market at present are all rational reactions to China’s present state of economic development and to various policies including economic policy. In the final analysis, therefore, for the consumer, whether the risk is that of buying a house or that of holding housing, it is policy risk. What is more important is that the changes of policy do not have a strong regularity, and the risk contained in them is correspondingly very large. Now that the housing problem has become a great problem bearing on social stability, the state should therefore, proceeding from the angle of the people’s livelihood, formulate a long-term stable policy system, thereby stabilising the expectations of consumers of all kinds, and thereby making the real estate market develop in a direction favourable to economic development, favourable to social stability, and favourable to reducing uncertainty and risk. In the implementation of policy, on the one hand the government needs, by lowering land prices, to lower real property prices and lower the threshold of a first house purchase, so that households with the capacity to pay “have housing”; on the other hand it needs to raise fiscal expenditure on affordable housing and low-rent housing, and to develop new forms of housing, guaranteeing that low-income households “have a house to live in”. The asset item occupying the largest proportion of assets — namely real property — whose stable price and returns are the core of a healthy balance sheet, is also the core of the transformation of the consumption model. If China’s consumers and households can all smoothly purchase high-quality real property assets, then households’ consumption model will, as analysed in the theoretical part above, be transformed fairly quickly and fairly smoothly into the balance-sheet type, thereby casting off the unsustainable development model of a long-term low consumption rate.

6.2 Problems of Consumption Cash Flow

The obstacles to the transformation of the consumption model manifest themselves not only in the acquisition and holding of assets within the balance sheet, but also in the direction and volume of cash movements within the cash flow statement. As the theoretical discussion earlier in this dissertation argued, changes in cash flow are the direct cause of consumers increasing their saving and reducing their consumption, and are the foremost problem that the transformation of the consumption model needs to solve. For Chinese households and consumers, cash outflows consist mainly of everyday consumption, medical care, and education expenditure. The consumption and medical expenditure here include not only the married couple of the core household themselves, but also their children and their already retired parents; education expenditure is mainly on the children. The root of this pattern of payments is that the construction of China’s medical care and old-age support systems is very imperfect, with small coverage and very large disparities, so that the task of making up these gaps falls back upon mutual assistance between the generations. This dissertation has held throughout that the analysis of China’s consumers and consumption problems absolutely cannot be divorced from the framework of the household, and the analysis below of the cash flows of medical care and old-age expenditure therefore follows this same principle. In education expenditure the object is clear, but the total volume of the expenditure cash flow differs enormously; this is a problem with Chinese characteristics, yet its influence on household cash expenditure is profound, and for reasons of data sources this dissertation adopts a qualitative analysis of it.

In general terms, the explicit rise in China’s cost of living and medical prices is marked, and the implicit rise in education prices is marked; the rises in the prices of these products all ultimately lead to an increase in household cash outflows, which not only directly raises the cost of living but also increases the risk of maintaining a healthy balance sheet. Increasing the net cash inflow is therefore an important aspect of, and the most direct method for, the transformation of the consumption model. As regards the concrete methods and measures for increasing cash flow, apart from the policy measures already discussed earlier — raising the employment rate, narrowing the disparities in income distribution, balancing development between east and west, and the like — and taking into account the characteristic of the close ties between the generations in Chinese households, this dissertation holds that this characteristic will exert an unfavourable influence on the increase of cash flow, and an unfavourable influence on the process of transformation of the consumption model.

6.2.1 Expenditure on Medical Care, Old-Age Support, and the Like

Expenditure on medical care and old-age support, as this dissertation has said, is not directed only at oneself but exists between the generations; in particular, as the average life expectancy of China’s population lengthens, consumers spend more and more in these areas. In the last few years especially, Chinese residents’ outlays on medical care and health have increased markedly. According to data from Guotai Junan, in 2004 the cash expenditure of rural residents in this area was 1,010.3, and that of urban residents 4,139.9; by 2007 these figures had grown to 1,536.5 and 6,172.1, increases exceeding or close to 50 per cent, while over the same period the growth in Chinese residents’ incomes lagged far behind these increases. The pressure of cash expenditure on medical care and old-age support has therefore increased absolutely, for rural and urban households alike.

That China’s drug prices pass through too many links from the factory gate to the patient’s hands is the direct cause of their being falsely high; secondly, the collusion of doctors, through their right of prescription, with the sellers is the key; in the end, the falsely high drug prices fall upon the individual consumer and the social insurance system. An important aspect of old-age outlay is embodied in medical expenses, so lowering medical costs will also have a direct and effective effect in lowering the costs of old-age support. In particular, considering the problem that the increase in the average life expectancy of China’s population brings about a relative reduction in the total length of working life, this dissertation holds that changing the retirement age can also in part increase cash inflows and offset the unfavourable factors brought by the increase in cash outflows in other areas. At the individual level, the average life expectancy of the Chinese population has risen steadily along with economic development. This is of course a mark of social progress, but on the other hand we cannot but consider that increased longevity requires the individual to plan his consumption needs over a longer period of time. In the simple model of life-cycle theory, this change can be represented graphically as follows:

Figure 6-8, from the left panel to the right panel, describes this change. Holding other conditions unchanged, one can see without further calculation that the individual’s lifetime consumption level will certainly fall from $C$ to $C^*$. This is the most direct result of a lengthening of the life cycle. But failing to consider the “other” conditions cannot rigorously explain this problem, so we bring the rate of growth of income and the term structure of work into consideration and construct a comparatively rigorous model to analyse further the relationship between longevity and the consumption rate.

Suppose the real interest rate is zero, the rate of population growth is $n$, and the rate of growth of income is $g$; then over a period of time $t$ the new consumers are $n_0 e^{nt}$, the total income of consumers within this period is $y_0 e^{gt}$, the consumer’s whole life cycle is divided into the two stages of “working” ($W$) and “retirement” ($R$), and within the life cycle the consumer decides consumption by averaging over total income, so that in each period the consumer will consume a part of current income $\frac{W}{W+R}$ and save the remainder against the time after retirement when there is no income. The consumer’s total income and total consumption can therefore be expressed by the following formulae:

$$C = \int_{t-W-R}^{t} \frac{W}{W+R}\, y_0 e^{g\theta} n_0 e^{n\theta}\, d\theta, \qquad Y = \int_{t-W}^{t} y_0 e^{g\theta} n_0 e^{n\theta}\, d\theta$$

After evaluating the integrals, the expression for the proportion of consumption in income is obtained as:

$$\frac{C}{Y} = \frac{W}{W+R} \cdot \frac{1 - e^{-(g+n)(W+R)}}{1 - e^{-(g+n)W}}$$

From the expression above, the influence of the rate of economic growth and the rate of population growth on the consumption rate is not large; $W$ and $R$, as against growth rates of less than 1, are higher by at least two orders of magnitude, so that when $W$ and $R$ are comparatively large, the latter fraction approaches 1, and what influences consumption comparatively greatly is therefore the term structure of work. An important indicator influencing the term structure of work is the proportional relationship between the length of working life and the length of retirement. Imagine an extreme case: if $R = 0$, then the theoretically calculated consumption rate should approach 100 per cent; the reason for a low consumption rate is therefore that the length of retirement is too long, and the important cause of an excessively long retirement, besides the retirement age prescribed by the state, is, more importantly, the lengthening of expected life. Calculating from the rough data for China’s employed population — formally entering the labour market and obtaining income at 20 (taking the average of adulthood at 18 and graduation from university at 22), retiring at 57.5 (men retiring at 60 and women at 55, calculated as an average), and an expected life of 70 — the true length of working life is then 37.5 and the length of retirement 12.5, so the consumption rate is approximately equal to 75 per cent. If the expected life is 80, then the consumption rate is approximately 62.5 per cent.

Going further, if we consider the period from an individual’s birth to his participation in work — supposing the length of time needed from birth to employment is $A$, and the rate of growth of the numbers entering the labour market each year is $\gamma$ — then these people are pure consumers, so their influence on consumption is positive and their contribution to income negative, and the graph of the life cycle is as follows:

From the simple calculating analysis above, the lengthening of expected life certainly directly lowers the ratio of consumption in income. The influence that this natural growth of longevity brings to the individual consumer is not only that the individual needs further to raise the total volume of income within the life cycle, but also that he must plan the consumption within the cycle over a longer period of time. Under the assumed condition of the holism of the Chinese household (the household as an integrated unit), and under the evident requirement that the time spent in education grows ever longer, this mode of planning exists not only within a generation but even more markedly between the generations.

The traditional Chinese household idea is “the more sons, the more good fortune”, which, understood in the economic sense, means that under conditions of diminishing marginal cost of child-rearing, having many children can lower the risk of old-age support. As China’s households have evolved from “many children” to a situation in which “only-child” households form the absolute majority, the planning behaviour of households in rearing children has changed fundamentally.

First, it has brought an increase in risk. This risk exists at the stages of the child’s survival, healthy growth, schooling, employment, and finally forming a household of his own. The first generation will therefore be more attentive in these respects than a multi-child household. For the household as a whole, failure at one of these stages will seriously affect the welfare of the whole household. This is not only a profound and vivid embodiment of the characteristic of Chinese household holism, but also a realistic requirement in the economic sense.

Secondly, this change is manifested in the content of the first generation’s budget for the second generation, and in its costs, in every area. The method the first generation can adopt to resist this risk is to increase the strength of its investment in the second generation in these areas. We often see that the costs such an only-child household spends in the course of its child’s growth are no less than those of a multi-child household — that is, the marginal cost is very large. Not only is this behaviour not irrational emulation; it is the most rational optimising behaviour. We can easily discover that households’ expenditure today on their children’s growth and education has already reached a considerable proportion of total household income. According to a research report on the “Guoyan Wang” (China Development Gateway research network), “the proportion of Chinese households’ expenditure on their children’s education has already approached one third of total household income. In the year from October 2004 to October 2005, households with children at school or kindergarten spent on average 3,522.1 yuan in total on their children’s education, approximately 30.2 per cent of the household’s annual income.” At the stage of the children’s employment, the expenditure may not necessarily be expressed as monetary expenditure, but the resources the first generation can mobilise exert an influence that cannot be underestimated on the children’s employment and their future income. Finally, the cost of the children marrying and setting up a household remains a major item of outlay even in the modern Chinese household. All of these are contents of the first generation’s budget for the second generation.

At the same time as the household structure has changed and the content of the first generation’s budget for the second generation has also changed, the content of the second generation’s budget for the first generation is likewise changing, manifested mainly in medical care and old-age support. In the planned economy period, expenditure in these two areas was completely borne by the state through enterprises and public institutions, and the individual needed no additional payment — that is, the state provided complete insurance. But with the economic transition, this complete insurance provided by the state no longer exists, and the social security system at present still has many deficiencies, such as low premiums and a limited scope of protection, so that for many households old-age support still has to rely on provision by the second generation, and this is all the more so for the vast number of rural households. Contents that formerly did not enter the expenditure budget have now all clearly begun to enter every household’s statement of income and expenditure, and their importance grows ever stronger and their prices ever higher, so that, in analysing household consumption behaviour, the institutions and prices connected with these budget contents will directly influence the consumption rate — because whichever member of the household it is, he must deduct such outlays from his income in advance, and this deduction directly lowers the individual consumption rate.

6.2.2 Expenditure on Education

This dissertation defines education expenditure as the cost households pay for their children to receive education. China practises a system of nine years of compulsory education; from the standpoint of institutional arrangement, it is free for school-age children to receive education, and education beyond the stage of compulsory education requires the payment of part of the fees. From the standpoint of practical results, China’s education system, from kindergarten right through to university, requires the payment of high fees; otherwise the groups that in theory should receive a free education will be excluded from the education system by the allocation mechanism. Extra costs such as “school-choice fees”, “temporary-enrolment fees”, and “sponsorship fees”, although they cannot be counted in detail, are indeed large items of outlay in households’ education expenditure. In addition, in order to receive a good education, besides competition in money, children must compete in other skills, and the extra training and tutoring for this becomes a rigid demand, so that the rigid costs in this area become another large item of outlay for households besides normal education expenditure.

Although we cannot count in detail from the micro level data on expenditure connected with education, such as school-choice fees and tutoring classes, this does not prevent us from reaching the conclusion that education expenditure has increased markedly, nor does it prevent an economic analysis of these phenomena. Here we start from the core of the problem: why do parents give up the free local compulsory education and choose to select a school? In the final analysis, school selection must still be traced back to: competition. After more than thirty years of marketization reform, China’s economic structure and strength have both been markedly raised, but the present industrial structure, consisting mainly of labour-intensive enterprises, determines that the demand for human resources of higher educational attainment is limited. At the same time, the returns to mental labour are obviously higher than those to physical labour, and, together with the influence of traditional Chinese ideas, this makes the vast majority of households choose directly to invest in higher education without considering the future returns, which intensifies the degree of competition for employment among this group of people. Competition in employment becomes the source of all competition. In order to gain an advantage in employment competition, students must gain an advantage in the brand of the school from which they graduate and in the level of their educational attainment, and so admission to a famous university is the most effective method among them. Entering a city, a school district, and a school with a high rate of progression to higher education becomes the motive force of the deeper level of competition, and the reason for school selection. Because the numbers competing are so large, excellence in academic results alone can no longer distinguish between them, and so the “appurtenances” beyond academic results have arisen in response — Olympic mathematics results, English proficiency grades, artistic talents, among them also money. The acquisition of these appurtenances cannot be completed within the school, and so the various fee-charging tutoring classes run by society provide services of this kind, and run them in joint-stock cooperation with schools. These tutoring classes are commercial in nature, so it is only proper that a corresponding cost should be paid. For households, the fees spent in these areas are many times higher than the fees of normal education. At this point we see that households’ cash payments on education are not merely the ordinary fees of the normal stage of compulsory education, but include cash payments for all kinds of appurtenant products. As competition grows ever fiercer, these costs grow ever higher, and have already become a heavy economic and mental burden on households. In the final analysis, therefore, the causes of the high outlays on education lie not only in the field of education; the deeper cause is the insufficient and unbalanced input of the Chinese government into the public product of education, and unbalanced economic development.

6.2.3 Rises in the Prices of Consumer Goods

China’s consumer goods market has undergone the enormous transformation from quantitative rationing in the planned economy era to the monetary rationing of marketization today, and the influence on consumers’ cash expenditure has passed from reliance in the past on all kinds of coupons to reliance today solely on money. Although, as incomes and living standards have risen and a situation of aggregate oversupply has appeared, fluctuations in the prices of general consumer goods have no especially obvious influence on households’ daily life, the combination of the mode of consumption and the content of consumption — that is, the consumption structure — is a major problem that China’s consumption field at present needs to face and solve. Fluctuations in the prices of consumer goods at different levels therefore not only exert a certain influence on households’ cash expenditure; more importantly, they exert a major influence on the long-term problem of the upgrading of the consumption structure. On the other hand, commodity prices are closely related to liquidity. Under a given market structure, the prices of consumer goods are to a large extent governed by liquidity policy. Having experienced the financial crisis of 2008, China practised an easy monetary policy, and today this easing has already become manifest in the field of consumer goods, inflation passing from expectation to reality. Consumers with the ability choose housing, gold, and other means of hedging, while for the vast majority of consumers the higher risks and entry thresholds of other investment channels restrict the conversion of the form of their assets, and enduring inflation is more realistic than changing the structure of their assets.

Combining the changes in the three, we can discover that, because of the external shock, the production of China’s enterprises and the consumption of its residents were both greatly shocked, and production and consumption, trade included, fell simultaneously into a channel of deceleration; in order to maintain growth the state began to use an easy monetary policy and an extremely easy fiscal and credit policy to stimulate the economy, but it was precisely the use of this policy that caused the CPI and PPI to turn positive in the last quarter of 2009 and to begin re-entering a rising channel. This rise, however, was not generated by the growth of domestic or external demand; combining the data on the total retail sales of consumer goods, one can discover that it was because the easy monetary policy led to a low cost of capital, and the trend of change in the total retail sales of consumer goods tallies very closely with the trend of change in the volume of money in circulation. That is to say, if the inflation factor is deducted, consumption did not increase because of the proactive fiscal and monetary policies. From the standpoint of the ordinary consumer, however, under given income the increase in the expenditure of daily life really did raise the cost of living. Starting from the purpose of encouraging consumption, China’s economic policy on this occasion of stimulating the economy and transforming the development model of reliance on external demand did not receive a good effect; on the contrary, it caused inflation and a renewed rise in housing prices. If a genuine transformation of the mode of growth is wanted, therefore, raising residents’ consumption rate requires, besides starting from the monetary level, the more important step of starting from the real economy, changing the economic structure to absorb employment and raise incomes. Otherwise, excessive liquidity can bring only inflation and a tilt towards capital in income distribution, and what is ultimately harmed is still consumption and domestic demand.

Analysing from the aspect of cash flow, then, Chinese consumers’ net cash outflow increases under conditions of monetary easing, and this is manifested in medical care, old-age support, education, and the outlays of daily life. This economic pattern and these fiscal and monetary policies, unfavourable to an improvement in consumers’ cash flow statements, are the obstacles at present to changing China’s low consumption rate and transforming the consumption model. Analysing from this angle confirms once again the main view argued earlier in this dissertation, namely that the problems of the slow transformation of China’s consumption model and of its low consumption rate are not problems existing in the field of consumption itself, but the embodiment in the field of consumption of problems in other fields, and that the low consumption rate is consumers’ rational response under their constraints. Solving the insufficiency of consumption and domestic demand in the short term requires improving the cash flow statement, increasing cash inflows and reducing cash outflows — for example, increases in government subsidies for consumers’ welfare security, increases in input into education, and the like; while in the long term it requires improving consumers’ balance sheets, letting real property, negotiable securities, and other assets enter consumers’ balance sheets as soon as possible, and improving the situation of the asset markets, in which fluctuations are violent and returns unstable. In this way, when more consumers become a propertied middle class, it will exert a decisive effect in expanding consumption and domestic demand and in changing China’s industrial structure and consumption model.

6.3 Risks to the Preservation and Appreciation of Assets

Having analysed the relationships and mutual influences between cash flow on the one hand and consumers, the consumption rate, and the consumption model and behaviour on the other, this section will, from the standpoint of the balance sheet, analyse the influence of the varieties and quantities of assets on the balance sheet, and the influence of liabilities and credit and their risks on consumers. Bringing these problems into the framework of the transformation of the consumption model for analysis will help to clarify the relationships between the factors above, and will ultimately exert a positive effect on the transformation of the consumption model.

6.3.1 Housing Assets

For the ordinary consumer, the housing asset is generally the item with the largest share in the structure of household assets; because of the dual nature of the housing asset as consumption and as investment, fluctuations in housing prices are connected with the security of consumers’ assets and with the returns on their assets. If the consumer uses credit to purchase housing, fluctuations in housing prices will also affect the safety of the consumer’s balance sheet. Below, this dissertation starts from the accounting standpoint to analyse the influence of changes in the value of the housing asset on the balance sheet and the cash flow statement, and thereby on consumption and the consumption model.

The table below is a simplified balance sheet, which includes the housing asset, the housing asset not being purchased through credit.

Assets Liabilities

Housing: 1 million yuan (current value)
Total assets: 2 million yuan

As regards the natures of saving and real property, both belong to investment: the liquidity of saving is strong and its rate of return low, while the liquidity of real property is poor and its rate of return comparatively high; at the same time, under conditions in which inflation exists, the real rate of return on saving may be negative — for example, China’s current interest rate on demand deposits is 0.36 per cent and the interest rate on one-year time deposits 2.25 per cent, while in the second half of 2010 China’s level of inflation exceeded 3 per cent, so the real interest rate on deposits is negative and China is at present still in a period of negative interest rates; so long as the level of inflation does not change, the rate of return on the savings asset will remain negative. Considering the disputes and analyses at present directed at housing prices, I hold that the core problem is not housing prices themselves but the maladjustment between the rate of return on real property, the mode of purchasing housing, and interest rates. Below, a hypothetical balance sheet is again used for a simplified analysis.

Assets Liabilities

Housing: 1 million yuan

In the simplified balance sheet above, at the beginning of the year this consumer has 1 million yuan each of savings assets and real property; at the same time it is supposed that the consumer purchases real property through a loan, the total volume of the loan being 500,000 yuan, the contractual interest rate on the loan being $r$, to be repaid over five years, repaying 100,000 yuan at the end of each year together with the interest generated by the loan amount of the preceding year; at the same time it is supposed that the saving is a one-year time deposit with a nominal interest rate of 2.25 per cent and an inflation rate of 3 per cent, and that the rate of return on real property is 10 per cent each year. On this basis we analyse how changes in the interest rate on the loan influence the rational consumer’s allocation of assets.

Case (i):

① The real rate of return on saving: $\dot{i}_1 = r - p$; thus the real income each year is $100 \times \dot{i}_1$.

② The real interest rate on the loan: $\dot{i}_2 = r - p$; thus the interest on the liability generated each year is $50 \times \dot{i}_2$.

③ The income from real property each year: 100 × 10%.

④ At the end of the year, savings are used to repay 100,000 yuan of the principal of the loan.

The balance sheet at the end of the year is then:

Assets Liabilities
Savings: 1 million yuan − 100,000 yuan = 900,000 yuan Housing loan: 500,000 yuan − 100,000 yuan = 400,000 yuan
Housing: 1 million yuan
Investment income: −500 yuan + 100,000 yuan Total interest on liabilities: 500,000 yuan × ($r$ − 3%)
Total assets: 1,999,500 yuan Total liabilities: 400,000 yuan + 500,000 yuan × ($r$ − 3%)

Following the idea of financial risk management, supposing the consumer’s ratio of liabilities to assets cannot change, then the nominal contractual interest rate on the loan calculated from the new balance sheet must be as high as 22.975 per cent; if it is lower than this value, the ratio of liabilities to assets will fall. Although the real rate of return on housing may be unable to reach 10 per cent, an excessively low interest rate on housing loans is still the main motive force leading consumers to purchase housing on loan and improve the structure of their assets and liabilities. Such a rate of return on real property and interest rate on loans therefore cause consumers to purchase real property in large quantities by means of liabilities, improving their balance sheets. At the same time, the concentrated behaviour of purchasing housing further accelerates the rise in real property prices, bringing about in the short term the fact of high returns on investment in real property, although the generation of this situation is only short-term — that is, it may possibly be a bubble. Drawing together the brief analysis above, we can preliminarily reach the following conclusions. First, the reason consumers purchase real property is mainly that its rate of return is markedly higher than that of other assets, and its safety high. This is so for first-time purchasers of housing and for repeat or multiple purchasers alike. Secondly, the low interest rate on housing loans encourages consumers to purchase housing by means of liabilities, but an improper allocation of assets will generate a risk of bankruptcy. Finally, from the standpoint of market supply and demand, the pursuit of high-return assets is the ultimate cause of the vigorous demand for the housing asset, and also the ultimate cause of the rapid rise in housing prices. In order to purchase the housing asset, consumers even, by raising the quantity of the low-return savings asset, attain as soon as possible the purpose of using liabilities to purchase real property, so as to avoid still higher potential losses in the future.

Besides the risk of potential loss of income, for consumers who have already purchased housing the risk is also embodied in depreciation. Although the rise in China’s housing prices is at present the dominant trend, the risk is not excluded of a short-term fall in housing prices causing losses to purchasers, and especially under the present policy background of high-intensity regulation and control of the housing market, the risk of short-term loss is still very great. If saving and real property are each 1 million yuan, the housing loan 500,000 yuan, and the other conditions are unchanged, and if housing prices fall by 10 per cent, then although the saving is also depreciating, there is no risk in paying the debts falling due, and only the balance sheet worsens; if the consumer has used up his saving in order to purchase housing, then not only will the balance sheet worsen, but a risk of bankruptcy will also be generated. If at this time, in order to avoid bankruptcy, the housing asset is sold, then the loss on the assets will become a fact, and the loss will be not merely a worsening of the accounting statements but a reduction of cash.

The acquisition of the housing asset therefore requires consideration not only of interest rates, housing prices, and the rate of return on real estate, but also analysis of cash flow and the potential risk of bankruptcy. From the macro standpoint, China is still in a period of further acceleration of urbanisation; large numbers of people entering urban life need to acquire real property, and residents living in cities also need to replace or acquire real property, so the demand for housing will continue to remain vigorous. This is the core factor in the continued rise in China’s housing prices, and so acquiring real property and converting saving into real property as far as possible is consumers’ rational expectation, generated by China’s economy. If expectations are stable, housing prices rise steadily, the balance sheets of consumers who have purchased housing continue to improve, and consumers who have not purchased housing will reduce consumption and raise their saving rate, bringing the housing asset into their balance sheets as early as possible. In this situation, the risk is concentrated mainly on the side of the consumers who have not purchased housing.

6.3.2 Other Assets

Besides the housing asset, China’s consumers also have other modes of investment they can choose, such as securities, gold, and the like; these assets are incomplete substitutes for saving — incomplete because the rates of return on these assets are higher than on saving but their risks are also greater than saving’s.

Among the assets China’s consumers purchase, the most important besides real property are the various financial assets, including stocks, bonds, funds, insurance, and the like. The risk to the returns on these assets is high — or rather, the amplitude of their fluctuation is large. Because the amplitude of fluctuation is large and frequent, not every consumer can grasp the timing of profit, and so a risk of loss on assets exists; this risk can be embodied and identified by the market and by trading behaviour. On the other hand, the risk of negotiable securities assets also increases with financial virtualisation, and this risk often cannot be identified by consumers, so it becomes a potentially major risk factor in the balance sheet. But in view of the risk of the stock market and the knowledge needed to master stock trading, the number of people in China participating in stock trading is comparatively small; in particular, under conditions of an incomplete market, trading in stocks and other warrants displays a marked asymmetry of information, and this has affected the enthusiasm for stocks entering the individual balance sheet.

As with the stock market, similar situations also exist in other warrant markets: either the entry threshold is high, or the risk is great, and it is hard for the individual who wants to participate in them to make a profit, because the motive of the household in maintaining its balance sheet is dominated by utility rather than by capital gains; the assets items of the household balance sheet are therefore still concentrated in the two aspects of saving and real property, and so the fluctuations of the stock market and the level of interest rates exert no marked influence on the type and structure of Chinese household assets. Where, then, is the influence of the stock market and the other asset markets on consumers and their balance sheets embodied?

6.3.3 The Risk of Bankruptcy

This dissertation holds that after the consumption model is transformed from the cash-flow type to the balance-sheet type, the greatest threat to households and consumers is the risk of bankruptcy, including being unable to pay debts falling due and being insolvent. According to the discussion above, the largest part of the household’s liabilities item is the housing loan; there may also be a small volume of small-sum consumer loans. Because of the restrictions of the conditions for issuing housing loans, consumers will generally not fall into a situation of insolvency, so the risk of bankruptcy comes more from being unable to pay debts falling due. If, for example, unemployment, illness, or the occurrence of other accidents reduces the cash inflow of the current period, then under a situation of insufficient saving bankruptcy becomes possible. This therefore explains all the more, for China’s consumers, the importance of cash flow and of a certain volume of saving for the security of household assets, and at the same time the importance of reducing the occurrence of other accidents and of cash outflows for the security of consumers’ assets. This is precisely the influence of the changes in cash inflows and outflows on consumers discussed above, and it is also the risk. Combining it with this dissertation’s definition of the middle class, those consumers who own housing assets will attach still more importance to the management and maintenance of the cash flow statement. For the individual consumer, the activities that can generate cash inflows are very limited; besides labour income, the most important, investment in negotiable securities, the appreciation of real property, and other temporary income basically include all forms of income, so the stability of employment and of the rates of return on the securities market is of great significance in resisting risk. On the other hand, because China’s consumers have not yet completely established the balance-sheet consumption model, the appreciation of the housing asset can be embodied only in the total volume of assets and the optimisation of the asset structure. For the consumer, the primary purpose of purchasing housing, the first set of housing especially, is residence, so it is impossible frequently to trade real property in order to increase cash flow; if it is not traded on the market, this part of the assets contributes nothing to cash flow. Starting from the safety of the balance sheet, and starting from the purpose of increasing cash flow, therefore, enterprises and departments for the appraisal of individual housing could be established on the basis of consumers’ balance sheets, and financial instruments used to turn this part of the assets — the appreciation of the assets — into negotiable securities of stronger liquidity, or directly into cash; this does not affect the balance sheet and also increases liquidity, and is a beneficial move for directly raising the level of consumption. This method, however, can be used only in the situation in which the property rights in the housing belong completely to oneself; otherwise those who use this method are more often the banks, and the cash flow of course also belongs to the banks. Asset securitisation products issued by banks on the basis of housing loans are very commonly seen on the market; in a situation in which the macroeconomic situation is improving and consumers’ cash flow is unchanged or increasing, securities of this kind can generate cash flow income, but once the consumer becomes unemployed or other unexpected expenditure increases, securities of this kind will depreciate. Not only may the consumer stop paying the loan payable each month and entirely surrender the ownership of the real property; the spread of this risk to the banking system will also cause still greater harm. The American financial crisis broke out in just this way. The maintenance of consumers’ balance sheets is therefore connected not only with the security of individual assets and the increase of utility, but also with the security of the whole banking system and financial system. In this sense, employment, wage income, and the level of welfare security — these factors closely connected with the income and expenditure of consumers’ cash flow — are all the foundation and guarantee of the security of the individual balance sheet, the stability of the financial system, and social stability. Besides the risk of bankruptcy in housing loans, as the volume of credit cards issued and used in China increases year by year, the risk in payments on credit card loans comes in its train. Although the sum of each use of a credit card is not high, if it has not been carefully planned, or an unexpected situation arises, a risk of bankruptcy still exists.

For the security of micro individuals’ assets and liabilities, therefore, for the stable growth of the macroeconomy, and at the same time for the smooth progress of the transformation of the consumption model, the maintenance of assets and liabilities should consider not only the structure of assets and liabilities but should consider cash flow still more, especially in the aspect of increasing the net cash inflow.

6.3.4 The Consumption Environment

On the other hand, the transformation of the consumption model also needs the construction of a good consumption environment. The consumption environment refers to the given constraints and game relationships that the consumer faces when a purchasing interaction occurs between him and consumer goods, producers, and sellers. The consumption environment is a very complex system, involving contents and relationships in the economic, social, psychological, legal, and other aspects, so improving the consumption environment is likewise a long and complex process.

The consumption environment is closely related to the transformation of the consumption model. If economic development needs to rely on consumption to pull it, then the consumption generated by consumers merely purchasing the necessities of life certainly cannot satisfy the goal of macroeconomic development. Letting consumers, besides this, also continue to maintain vigorous purchasing power is then an important content of improving the consumption environment. First, because for many products the information of buyer and seller is asymmetric, “opportunistic behaviour” is easily generated, and this is the most important cause of the deterioration of the consumption environment. The most effective method of curbing opportunistic behaviour is to strive for symmetry of information between buyer and seller, and for this the existence of intermediary institutions of comparatively high reputation is necessary. In order to avoid the intermediary institutions colluding with the seller, the most effective method of curbing opportunism is to strengthen competition among sellers. Secondly, the generation of opportunistic behaviour also lies in the short-term nature of trading behaviour. Temporary, short-term trading of that kind is where the consumption environment most easily deteriorates, so changing the point in time at which the risk of the product is transferred, postponing it from “payment” until after the consumer has used the product for a period of time, can effectively solve this problem. Thirdly, because for a long time China’s economic development has relied mainly on investment and trade, the laws and policies the state has issued in these two aspects are relatively complete. Now that it needs to rely on consumption to pull the economy, the state should speed up the formulation of laws to protect consumers and to regulate the development of the consumer goods market.
