Chapter 2 The Development of Consumption Theory: A Review of the Literature

Compared with growth and growth theory, consumption was for a long time not the most important field of theoretical research in macroeconomics, because the sufficiency of consumption itself — which is closely connected with man’s natural needs — seemed, relative to the utilisation of scarce resources, to be self-evident. For this reason, in the classical economics that began with Adam Smith, consumption was only a variable lying far beyond productive capacity: how to satisfy “it” was the ultimate goal of economic theory, and even the comparatively well-developed theory of demand did not connect consumption with macroeconomic development. Only after Keynes (John Maynard Keynes) had constructed modern macroeconomics was this situation thoroughly reversed. The modern macroeconomics founded by Keynes not only opened up the research paradigm of analysing macroeconomic aggregates; against the background of the capitalist crisis of relative overproduction it also paid greater attention to the analysis of demand. From the time we entered the Keynesian era, demand and consumption became important links driving the healthy operation of the economy. Still more important was the introduction of the “consumption function”, and the series of policy practices conducted around “effective demand” achieved good results; from this point, research on consumption formally entered the field of systematic economic research. The policy practices conducted under the guidance of Keynesian theory placed government purchases, monetary policy, investment activity and the like all around demand, so that in this sense Keynesian economics is in fact an economics of demand management. Although demand includes more than consumption, from this moment consumption did indeed combine with demand to become an important field of research in modern macroeconomics. Looking at the process of development of consumption theory, on the one hand it developed as real productive forces gradually developed, material products gradually became abundant, the role played by the market in economic activity gradually grew stronger, and mankind gradually developed from “shortage” to the stage of “relative overproduction”; on the other hand it also developed as economic thought gradually evolved and improved, and research objectives, methods and angles gradually became richer.

From the time Keynes put forward the consumption function, most research on consumption unfolded around the consumption function. Such things as remodelling the consumption function to accord better with reality, introducing variables with greater explanatory power, and expounding the consumption function in depth became all the rage; and the theoretical basis of these studies was in every case Keynes’s macroeconomic standpoint — that is, analysing through macroeconomic data and through such variables in the macroeconomic consumption function as the marginal propensity to consume and the average propensity to consume, and then using the analysis in policy practice. As research became more refined, the “life-cycle theory (Life Cycle Hypothesis)”, the “permanent income theory (Permanent Income Hypothesis)” and the “precautionary saving theory (Precautionary Saving Hypothesis)”, based on the micro individual, appeared; they very well revealed the connections between individual income, saving, consumption, investment and other such consumer behaviours, and Modigliani and Milton Friedman received the Nobel Prize in Economics for them respectively. This “transformation” of consumption theory brought research on consumption into the micro field, and the precise explanation of micro consumption behaviour and the rationality of micro aggregation became the research emphases of consumption theory. By this point consumption theory had truly become an independent field of theoretical research in economics, and research from the macroeconomic aggregate to the micro individual had been well developed within the framework of certainty; the research that followed began to expand in the direction containing risk and uncertainty.

The life-cycle theory depicted micro consumer behaviour very well, so subsequent research basically followed this theory; even where it was remodelled, the remodelling did not injure its core content — using a limited income as evenly as possible over the life cycle and maintaining a certain amount of assets to withstand risk and uncertainty. However, because of the lack of tools and methods for describing uncertainty, consumption theory at this time did not involve itself much with uncertainty. Subsequently Hall’s random walk model, and the precautionary saving theory, buffer-stock theory and so on that arose with it, formally introduced into the model the uncertainty and risk factors facing the consumer; this change not only accorded with the trend of development of economic theory, it also embodied a more meticulous study of consumption and of the consumer. Research at this time also formally went deep, from macroeconomic consumption under conditions of certainty to micro consumption behaviour under conditions of uncertainty, and passed from the concept of income and consumption in the original sense of national income distribution and total consumption to the individual consumer’s current and future income and income expectations, and to the substitution relations between current consumption and future consumption. In addition, as the external financial environment matured day by day, the use of financial instruments increasingly became an important variable in consumption decisions, so that consumption decisions not only became an important field of theoretical research but also an important component of real economic life.

While purely theoretical model research advanced step by step as the main thread, econometric empirical research also gradually rose from the status of testing theoretical models to a field of research of equal status with the construction of theoretical models, to the point that whenever a new theoretical model of consumption appeared, econometrics could always test it by suitable methods and put forward useful paths of improvement. Especially after the variables of theoretical models had become more and more diverse and complex, this impelled econometric methods to determine econometric proxy variables continuously on the basis of existing statistical data, rationally processed, and ultimately to test the correctness of the original models. Besides empirical econometric research, another method of testing the correctness of models is to use computers to simulate and test complex models for which explicit solutions are hard to obtain. The use of these two empirical methods provided a basis for the reliability of theoretical models; but the sources of econometric data and the ways of processing them, the choice of econometric proxy variables, and the determination of model parameter values when using simulation methods all present problems that are not easy to reconcile, and this also caused the disputes over the consumption problem to evolve gradually into disputes over the rationality of these technical problems. However that may be, these disputes in the end all revolve around, or originate in, consumption itself, so an in-depth discussion of these problems is also beneficial to the scientific development of consumption theory.

In research on consumption, Chinese scholars likewise followed a two-stage road of first imitating and then researching independently. Research in the initial stage simply used China’s macroeconomic consumption data to verify the Keynesian consumption function; of course the results obtained were similar to those in the West: although this consumption function was partly confirmed, there also existed such unsurprising “problems” as the “Keynesian paradox” and the stability of the marginal propensity to consume. Chinese economists then began to look for characteristics peculiar to Chinese consumers, thereby pushing China’s research on consumption theory into its second stage. The present author holds that the scholar who truly researched consumption starting from China’s reality was Yu Yongding: he was the first to characterise the behaviour of Chinese consumers by its “stage-based” nature, and then confirmed this view econometrically. Later there were also people who opened up territory along this road, such as Song Zhenxue and Zang Xuheng, Jiang Yang, Mu Jian and others; all these studies made a certain amount of progress.

Reality tells us that consumption is not only a theoretical problem, but a real problem concerning the individual consumer and society as a whole. Research on consumption not only needs innovation in methodology; what is more important is that only by deeply dissecting the individuals and groups of the country in question can conclusions with explanatory power be drawn. For example, how exactly was the characteristic preference for saving of Chinese consumers formed? Do Chinese consumers really prefer to save more and consume less? There are many causes of China’s long-run low consumption rate — which is the most important aspect? What risk-avoiding behaviours do Chinese consumers have? Is the cause of high saving connected with these factors? Are the current policies of raising consumption and lowering saving effective? Answering these concrete questions requires studying the characteristics of Chinese consumers and studying the characteristics of China’s macroeconomy; combining these two aspects is precisely an important content of the research of this dissertation.

Of course, before that, sorting out all these threads of theoretical development is not only beneficial to our developing new theory — what is more important is that it can help us to understand China’s economy better and provide a more scientific and more effective basis for policy for its healthy development. Although the literature researching consumption is already voluminous, the angle from which each researcher cuts in is not the same, so the ways of sorting out literature of the same kind also differ. This dissertation focuses on proceeding from the angle of risk and uncertainty, and researches consumption models through macroeconomic aggregates combined with micro behaviour; accordingly, the way of reviewing the literature correspondingly adopts a division according to whether the analysis of uncertainty is the main concern, combined with the impetus given to modern consumption theory by econometric empirical research. The brief review of theory below unfolds along the threads above.

2.1 The Development of Consumption Theory under Conditions of Certainty

In the Smith era, at the birth of classical economics, the scarcity of products was the most serious obstacle to real economic development, so the focus of economists’ research was basically concentrated entirely on production: whether the doctrine of the division of labour or the doctrine of supply, whether the Physiocrats or the Mercantilists, in the end it all had to come down to an increase in products or wealth. Analysis of consumption was therefore either very slight, or concentrated on optimisation under given conditions — that is, the environmental constraints facing consumption were basically certain. In the Keynesian era a crisis of relative overproduction of products appeared in the capitalist economy, so research on demand began to flourish. Although research on consumption gradually became a focus at this time, Keynes’s consumption function, because it did not consider risk and uncertainty, must still be classified as consumption theory under conditions of certainty. As research on the Keynesian consumption function went deeper, the life-cycle theory, which proceeded from the micro angle to study consumer behaviour in a refined way, appeared. This research paradigm remains to this day the standard for researching consumption, although the original framework was also very deficient in its analysis of risk. This in fact also became the fundamental reason why research on consumption involving uncertainty and risk was able to make breakthroughs and gains.

2.1.1 The Classical Period of Consumption Theory

The basis of Adam Smith’s economic theory is the division of labour: he held that anyone must depend on the labour of others to survive. Because of the existence of the division of labour, individual labour necessarily becomes part of social labour, and each person can ultimately satisfy his own aim of maximisation only by exchanging in the market. In Smith’s thought we can discover that “consumption” and “production” possess equal status: they live and die together. Smith also held that the quantity of consumer goods a person possesses is one of the indications of wealth, and held that consumption in the current period cannot provide profit and income; products for current consumption cannot be used for capital accumulation, but are nevertheless part of society’s total capital; whereas products retained for future consumption can carry out the reproduction of capital. This shows that rationally dividing social products is of great significance for promoting economic development: through “forced frugality” wealth can be increased, and increasing productive consumption directly creates wealth.

Say was the first economist to introduce a comparatively complete theory of consumption into classical political economy; he held that the sole object of research of consumption is the degree of satisfaction generated by the act of consumption itself, and its sole purpose is to compare the loss caused by the consumer’s consumption with the satisfaction it provides. Say held that the most suitable consumption for an individual should be: consumption conducive to the satisfaction of actual needs; consumption based on income; and consumption conforming to moral standards. On the relation between production and consumption, he held that how to dispose of the “surplus value” remaining after deducting from the value of the product the value of the material already consumed “concerns the state of mankind more than any other thing”. Only by using this “surplus value” for productive consumption can profit and wealth be brought about. Say emphasised that production determines consumption, and put forward “Say’s Law”, that production automatically creates its own demand.

2.1.2 The Neoclassical Period of Consumption Theory

After the marginal revolution, the consumer evolved into the consumer of the “rational man paradigm”. Neoclassical economics discarded psychological factors and turned to rational choice. Under this assumption, the consumer takes the realisation of “utility maximisation” as his goal, and consumption behaviour was simplified into finding the maximum of a utility function; the marginal utility theory of value became the foundation of orthodox Western consumption theory. Neoclassical economics’ consumption theory of utility maximisation completely abandoned the classical humanist tradition; on the basis of the marginal utility theory of value it established the neoclassical theory of consumption, regarding utility as the capacity of a commodity to satisfy wants. Jevons (William Stanley Jevons) held that the condition for “obtaining the greatest enjoyment in life” is: only when the marginal satisfaction obtained from the various goods consumed is equal can a person realise utility maximisation. Walras (Léon Walras) gave a mathematical proof of the consumer’s condition for utility maximisation: when a consumer purchases goods, when the ratios of the prices of the various goods purchased to their respective marginal utilities are equal to one another, the consumer’s satisfaction reaches its maximum. Subsequently a group of neoclassical economists — Pareto (Vilfredo Pareto), Hicks and Samuelson (Paul A. Samuelson) and others — re-stated the conditions for utility maximisation using indifference curves and budget lines.

Neoclassical economics takes the “rational man” as its basic assumption, and the individual’s consumption choice can be expressed as: the decision maker, under given constraints, maximises his target utility. For the consumer, this problem can be made concrete as: choosing a bundle of goods within the budget constraint set so that the utility function reaches its maximum; the equilibrium condition for reaching the maximum is: other things being equal, the ratios of the prices of the various goods purchased are equal to their marginal rates of substitution. At this point neoclassical economics had transformed the problem of utility maximisation into the problem of the rational consumer’s choice under constraints; in subjective form it abandoned the investigation of the motives of economic behaviour and confined attention completely to the purely economic logical analysis of the consumer. On the basis of a series of axioms of preference, the consumer was transformed into a synonym for rational behaviour. From the birth of neoclassical economics onwards, the object of economic research concentrated day by day on the level of the relation between man and things; it simplified complex and diverse economic phenomena and turned economics into a discipline about problems of maximisation calculation in the individual domain, so that economics, which had researched complex human behaviour, became a positive discipline pursuing “objectivity” and “science”. The consumer as “rational man” thereby completely cast off the characteristics of the “social man”, and fundamentally had no need to consider questions of ethics and morality.

2.1.3 Macroeconomic Consumption Theory Beginning with the Keynesian Consumption Function

Keynes’s (J. M. Keynes) macroeconomic thought is a milestone for the development of consumption theory: in his famous work The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money he first mentioned the “consumption function” (Consumption Function). In his theory, the influence on national income of the several macroeconomic sectors — consumption, investment, government expenditure and trade — is of the same kind, and this made research on consumption no longer confined, as in neoclassical economics, to the micro field of utility; from this point research on consumption also began to become one of the core problems of research in economics, especially macroeconomics.

Keynesian macroeconomics holds consumption demand to be one of the important driving forces promoting economic growth, and also one of the ultimate purposes of economic development. The formation of the aggregate of consumption and the share it occupies in the macroeconomy are important criteria for judging a country’s economic state and degree of development. In Keynes’s macroeconomic theory, consumption demand is an important component of aggregate demand; if consumption demand is insufficient, it needs to be supplemented by other sectors such as investment demand and external demand — in particular, the government should expand public expenditure to increase demand, otherwise the economy will fall into stagnation. On the other hand, saving, the opposite of consumption, is the source of capital and at the same time one of the determining factors of economic growth and development. How the individuals who constitute the macroeconomy decide the division between consumption and saving thus became the core problem of Keynesian theory.

Keynes, by assuming that the “man” at the micro level manifestly has several clearly identifiable psychologies of consumption, summed them up in the famous “consumption function”, in which he held that an individual’s consumption can be divided into two parts: one part is relatively fixed, called “autonomous consumption”, determined by the level of social development at the time; the other part varies with current disposable income, called “induced consumption” — income increasing by one unit, induced consumption does not increase by one unit but by less than one unit. That is, the consumption function is:

$$C = \alpha + \beta Y_d$$

where $\alpha > 0$, $\beta \in (0,1)$; $\beta$ is called the “marginal propensity to consume (MPC)”. The income remaining after consumption is transformed into saving, and the interest rate becomes the important mechanism by which saving is transformed into investment. It can be seen that this consumption function regards current consumption as a function of current disposable income, determined only by current income; and, secondly, that disposable income appears as “absolute income”.

Analysing this theory, one can conclude: first, the form of Keynes’s consumption function is comparatively stable; and, secondly, while the marginal propensity to consume is stable, the average propensity to consume tends to decline. However, early empirical research did a great deal of verification of Keynes’s consumption function, but the conclusions drawn did not support the conclusions above. First, the form of the consumption function based on the “absolute income hypothesis” is not stable, the evidence coming from the American economy after the Second World War; secondly, through research on long time-series data, economists discovered that the “average propensity to consume (APC)” is comparatively stable, while the “marginal propensity to consume” (MPC) displays a declining trend. From this point, the contradiction between theoretical prediction and empirical conclusion produced the “consumption function puzzle” in the field of consumption theory, to the extent that subsequent in-depth research on consumption theory all unfolded as attempts to answer this puzzle from different sides and different levels.

To solve this puzzle, under conditions of “certainty” and “backward-lookingness” two methods arose that attempted to reconcile these contradictions. One was Duesenberry’s (1949) “relative income hypothesis”; in the same year, in the article “Fluctuations in the Saving–Income Ratio: A Problem in Economic Forecasting”, Modigliani also independently put forward a similar view. The other was Arthur Smithies’s simple distinction, in the consumption function, between the long run and the short run.

The “relative income hypothesis” assumes that consumption is determined not only by current disposable income but also by the relative level of consumption compared with other people — that is, consumption has a “demonstration effect” and a “position effect”, so the utility function is not determined by current consumption alone. In this way, utility increases only when consumption is above the average level; and once the level of consumption has risen, circumstances that might lead to a reduction in the level of consumption will seriously lower utility, thus producing the “ratchet effect”. According to this new assumption about consumption behaviour, Duesenberry’s consumption function can be written in the following form:

$$\frac{C_t}{Y_t} = (1-a) - b\left(\frac{Y_t}{Y_{\max}}\right)$$

where $Y_t$ is current income and $Y_{\max}$ is the maximum income previously attained. Under the long-run conditions in which income increases over time, one can obtain the conclusion that “in the long run the average propensity to consume and the marginal propensity to consume tend to become equal, while in the short run the marginal propensity to consume is smaller than the average propensity to consume”. This reconciliation of Keynesian consumption theory had already broken away from the doctrine that current income determines consumption; it had already taken the consumption behaviour and income of other people into account, and the consumer also possessed “backward-looking behaviour”; but in essence it had not broken away from the result of the consumer passively adapting to the relation between consumption and income, nor had it considered the role in consumption behaviour of the consumer’s individual optimising behaviour, expectational behaviour and uncertainty.

Arthur Smithies’s method reconciled this contradiction by simply distinguishing the long-run consumption function from the short-run consumption function. He held that consumption is indeed determined by the level of absolute income, but that the long-run consumption function has the form

$$C = aY$$

while the short-run consumption function has the form

$$C = a + bY$$

Through empirical research on the relevant American data for 1923–1940, he found that under the assumption of long-run and short-run consumption functions, the relations between consumption, income and the time factor could explain the “consumption function puzzle” rather well; but the defects of this theory are the same as those of the relative income hypothesis, and it also cannot explain the fact that the transformation between consumption and investment is also subject to the major influence of other factors.

2.1.4 Microeconomic Consumption Theory Beginning with the “Life-Cycle Theory”

The life-cycle hypothesis (Life Cycle Hypothesis), also called the “life-cycle consumption function model” (below, LCH), has mainly two theoretical sources: one is the individual optimisation thought of neoclassical economics, and the second is Fisher’s (I. Fisher, 1930) intertemporal decision-making. This theory was first jointly put forward by Modigliani, Brumberg and Ando (Franco Modigliani, R. Brumberg, Alberto Ando, 1952, 1954, 1957, 1963); its main content is: when consumers consider maximising their own utility they consider not only current income but also total income over the life cycle; over the life cycle the total amount of their assets fluctuates, and this fluctuation is smoothed by the consumer by means of saving, so that a result of comparatively stable consumption appears. Modigliani (Franco Modigliani, 1986) expressed this thought with the following diagram:

[Figure image: image10.png]

[Figure 2-1: Modigliani’s life-cycle diagram, with age $T$ on the horizontal axis and $Y$, $C$, $A$ on the vertical axis: income is constant at $\bar{Y}$ through the working years up to retirement at age $N$ and consumption is constant at $C(T) = \frac{N}{L}\bar{Y}$ throughout life of length $L$, while wealth $A(T)$ rises steadily during working life, peaks at retirement and falls back to zero at $L$; the hatched areas between the income and consumption lines are marked “Saving” before retirement and “Dissaving” after it.]

(Figure 2-1)

From it one sees that saving, consumption and assets are all functions of time (of the life cycle): the consumer saves when young and dissaves when old, but the level of consumption within the life cycle is stable and unchanging, so that the consumer attains utility maximisation within the life cycle. The general form of this thought can be expressed mathematically as follows:

$$U = \sum_{t=1}^{T} u(C_t)$$

where the utility function satisfies

$$u'(\cdot) > 0, \quad u''(\cdot) < 0$$

The consumer’s initial wealth is $A$ and the income in each period of life is $Y_t$; the budget constraint the consumer faces over his whole life is then that lifetime total consumption cannot exceed the total of lifetime resources, that is:

$$\sum_{t=1}^{T} (C_t) \leq A_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{T} Y_t$$

The Lagrangian function for utility maximisation is then:

$$U = \sum_{t=1}^{T} u(C_t) + \lambda\left(A_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{T} Y_t - \sum_{t=1}^{T} C_t\right)$$

The first-order condition of this expression is:

$$u'(C_t) = \lambda$$

Thus, because the marginal utility of consumption is unchanging, consumption in each period is equal, and consumption in period $T$ can be found to be: (1)

$$C_T = \frac{1}{T}\left(A_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{T} Y_t\right)$$

From the expression above one can see that the quantity consumed in each period is the average value of the total resources over the life cycle.

If it is assumed that the utility function satisfies a time-additively separable form, $U = \ln(C)$, the interest rate is $r$ and the individual’s subjective rate of time discount is $\delta$, then the optimisation problem faced by the individual consumer is

$$\mathrm{Max}_{C_t}: \sum_{0}^{T} \frac{\ln C_t}{(1+\delta)^t} \qquad \text{s.t.} \quad \sum_{0}^{T} \frac{C}{(1+r)^t} = \sum_{0}^{T} \frac{Y_t}{(1+r)^t}$$

Solving by the Lagrangian method gives: $\frac{C_t}{C_0} = \left(\frac{1+r}{1+\delta}\right)^t$. From this simplified conclusion one can see that the less patient the consumer, the more he tends towards current consumption.

In the static case Modigliani further drew the following conclusions: (1) the size of the macroeconomic saving rate cannot be explained simply by a “preference for thrift”, but is closely related to concrete individual optimising behaviour; (2) saving grows as the national economy grows, and when the growth rate is zero saving will also fall to zero; (3) the age of retirement is a very crucial variable for the total amount of wealth.

Roughly contemporaneously with the life-cycle theory, Friedman (M. Friedman, 1957) also made a major contribution in “forward-looking” consumption theory, putting forward the “permanent income theory” (Permanent Income Hypothesis) (below, PIH). In the PIH, “permanent income” and “transitory income” are two important concepts: he held that permanent income includes forward-looking expectations of future income, so there is no direct method that can estimate it reasonably, and it can only be inferred through econometric research; transitory income is income the consumer did not expect — it can be positive or negative; in other words, transitory income is the deviation of current income from permanent income. Corresponding to this, consumption can also be divided into permanent consumption and transitory consumption: permanent consumption is related to permanent income and is unrelated to transitory income. In the long run, there is a stable relation between permanent consumption and permanent income:

$$C_p = KY_p$$

()

The current total income function is determined by the following expression:

$$Y = Y_p + Y_t$$

where $Y_p$ is permanent income and $Y_t$ is transitory income.

From the review above it can be discovered that the content of the LCH and the PIH is the same: both are consumption theories in which the consumer carries out individual optimisation on the basis of long-run income, so they are collectively called “forward-looking” consumption theory, and are also jointly called the “life-cycle–permanent income hypothesis” (LCH-PIH). However, compared with the life-cycle theory, the permanent income theory lacks the influence of assets on the consumption path: the latter explicitly incorporates assets into the consumption decision while the former leaves them implicit in “permanent income”, and this affects its persuasiveness.

However, this forward-looking microeconomic consumption theory of the LCH-PIH kind has substantive differences from the backward-looking macroeconomic consumption theory of Keynes and Duesenberry. First, forward-looking consumption theory requires the consumer to carry out intertemporal optimisation rather than pursuing only current optimisation; secondly, because the theory requires forward-lookingness, the consumer’s behaviour of allocating resources between the “present” and the “future” according to the individual’s preference for time becomes individual rather than aggregate behaviour, but through appropriate aggregation the aggregate can again be derived. This kind of theory can therefore serve as a macroeconomic theory possessing a micro foundation, and is a comparatively complete theory. In fact, researching consumption at the large macroeconomic scale is no longer the mainstream paradigm; the more important research results have all appeared in the further refinement of consumption theory possessing a micro foundation, and in work done with the support of high-frequency data.

Just when economists thought that consumption theory only needed to continue doing some repair work on the basis of the LCH-PIH, a large quantity of contrary evidence from empirical research reminded economists once again that consumption theory is far from perfect.

For the LCH, in estimating future labour income it assumes that the ratio of income to current income is fixed, and hence has permanence; however, changes in current income are very likely to be transitory, and in many cases an increase in current income is at the cost of a reduction in future income. Secondly, the life-cycle theory holds that the old are dissavers, but in fact the old often save positively. Criticism of the PIH comes mainly from its vague explanation of the relation between assets and consumption. Finally, the smooth consumption path predicted by LCH-PIH theory also did not appear in empirical tests.

Another major impact on forward-looking consumption theory originated in the “Lucas Critique” (Lucas Critique, 1976) and the research on rational expectations (Rational Expectation) that began from it. Although the LCH-PIH model considers the individual’s predictions of the future, the relation between the present and expectations is certain and entirely without risk, unaffected by any other random factors, and this obviously does not accord with reality. For not only will the individual’s future income change, even current income is not a stationary variable; on the other hand, we also cannot predict with certainty that the consumption and saving decisions made now will definitely be optimal within the life cycle in the future. The first kind of defect is mainly to be solved by models containing risk and uncertainty, while the second kind of defect originates precisely in Lucas’s dissatisfaction with models of this kind, including econometric models, because they may lead to the problem of dynamic time inconsistency (Dynamic Time Inconsistency); solving this problem requires considering the formation of the individual’s rational expectations. The further development of consumption theory thus abandoned the certainty of expectations and expanded in the directions of dynamisation and the consideration of risk, opening up an entirely new path for the perfection of consumption theory.

2.2 The Development of Consumption Theory under Conditions of Uncertainty

2.2.1 The Random Walk Theory

Hall’s (Robert E. Hall) research on consumption theory took as its starting point the negation of the mode of expectation of forward-looking theory. He held that although forward-looking theory considers the future, this mode of expectation is “adaptive expectations” (Adaptive Expectations), which combines only the information of the past and the present and does not include the whole information of the future. In reality, when people make predictions about the future they use all the information that can be used — that is, they carry out “rational expectations” (Rational Expectations). Hall (Hall, 1978) formally and rigorously demonstrated his idea, and obtained the famous “random walk” (Random Walking) model of consumption. He held that the individual’s maximisation objective function is the expected utility function, and that the utility function is quadratic, that is:

$$E[U] = E\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(C_t - \frac{a}{2}C_t^2\right)\right]$$

The budget constraint is:

$$\sum_{t=1}^{T} (C_t) \leq A_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{T} Y_t$$

Calculation gives: $1 - aC_1 = E_1[1 - aC_t] = 1 - aE_1[C_t]$, where $t = 2, 3, \ldots, T$;

thus: $C_1 = E_1[C_t]$, where $t = 2, 3, \ldots, T$;

this conclusion shows that expected consumption in each subsequent period is equal to consumption in the first period. In general, consumption in each subsequent period is unknowable — that is, changes in consumption are unpredictable. According to the definition of expectations, $C_t = E_{t-1}[C_t] + e_t$; and $E_{t-1}[C_t] = C_{t-1}$,

so one obtains:

$$C_t = C_{t-1} + e_t$$

This is Hall’s conclusion:

(1) The life-cycle hypothesis and the permanent income hypothesis in fact imply that consumption follows a random walk; consumption in the first period is

$$C_1 = \frac{1}{T}\left(A_0 + \sum_{t-1}^{T} E_1[Y_1]\right)$$

;

consumption is related to expected income, not to certain income;

(2) consumption in the second period is

$$C_2 = C_1 + \frac{1}{T-1}\left(\sum_{t=2}^{T} E_2[Y_t] - \sum_{t-2}^{T} E_1[Y_t]\right)$$

;

concretely, it is consumption in the first period plus the change in expected resources over the remaining life cycle divided by the number of periods of the remaining life cycle.

Hall’s random walk model advanced a great step in research method, strictly incorporating expectations into the framework of consumption theory; and the conclusion of his random walk model holds that, because of their rationality, consumers not only optimise within their own life cycle but also optimise across time and even across generations, so that the consumption path is in essence difficult to describe. Its strict form cannot conceal the disappointment that the conclusions of Hall’s model brought to researchers; however, economists did not give up, but found another way, reconsidering the consumption problem from the angle of risk.

2.2.2 The Theory of Precautionary Saving

The starting point of the theory of precautionary saving (Precautionary Saving Theory) is likewise the consideration of uncertainty and risk: it holds that fluctuations in income, and any fluctuation that potentially affects total assets, cause risk to increase, so that, expecting this risk, the rational consumer will raise saving to a level higher than that predicted by the LCH-PIH, so as to be able to withstand the influence on consumption of all kinds of future uncertainty.

Research on precautionary saving began very early: Fisher (Fisher, 1956) and Friedman (Friedman, 1957) discovered a great deal of empirical evidence supporting a positive correlation between individual occupational risk and saving; at the same time Skinner (Skinner, 1988) discovered that the two classes of people with the highest occupational risk (the self-employed and businessmen) on the contrary had lower saving. Although this use of occupation as a proxy variable for risk is comparatively crude, the contradiction in the real evidence nevertheless shows that the theory of precautionary saving needs further research.

The main reason impelling the development of this theory is that the original theories could not explain consumption behaviour and the distribution of household property very well; this shows itself mainly in three aspects: (1) consumption is sensitive to current income; (2) according to the certainty model, when the interest rate is smaller than the rate of time preference, the growth rate of the consumption rate should be negative; however, Deaton (Deaton, 1986) and Singleton (Singleton, 1985) discovered from American data that, although the real interest rate was very low and at the same time the rate of time preference was assumed to be positive, the growth rate of aggregate consumption in the United States was positive for a long time; (3) as to whether the old after retirement really are dissavers, Mirer (Mirer, 1979) and Danziger (Danziger, 1983) proved that the old are positive savers whose wealth continually rises, while Hurd (Hurd, 1987) found contrary evidence.

These in-depth studies gave consumption research new enlightenment — namely, that consumers may not be rational, and that borrowing constraints may be very crucial. Regrettably, neither of these two implications can be deduced from the LCH-PIH, while the theory of precautionary saving gives these contradictions a formal explanation. Leland (H. E. Leland, 1968), through rigorous model analysis, using a utility function whose third derivative is greater than zero, obtained a consumption path containing a variance; he held that the greater the “variance” used to measure risk, the less the consumer will consume and the more he will save; the part of saving here in excess of that in the life-cycle theory is the “precautionary saving” the consumer carries out in order to guard against future uncertainty. Caballero (Caballero, 1990b), using an exponential utility function with a constant coefficient of absolute risk aversion (CARA), expressed this thought explicitly. Suppose the consumer’s target utility function is:

$$\mathrm{Max}: E_0\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(-\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\exp(-\gamma C_t)\right]$$

The budget constraint is:

$$A_{t+1} = A_t + Y_t - C_t$$

where $Y_t = Y_{t-1} + e_t$, and $e_t \sim N(0, \sigma^2)$.

After optimisation, the optimal consumption path satisfies

$$C_{t+1} = C_t + \frac{\gamma\sigma^2}{2} + e_t$$

;

and the level of consumption is:

$$C_t = \left(\frac{1}{T-1}\right)A_t + Y_t - \frac{\gamma(T-t-1)\sigma^2}{4}$$

. It can be seen that the level of consumption includes $\sigma^2$ used to measure the size of risk: with income unchanged, the greater $\sigma^2$ is, the less the consumption and the more the saving.

Taking this theory as a basis, economists developed many models along different directions; the more famous among them are those of Zeldes (Zeldes, 1989), Dynan (Dynan, 1993), Wilson (Bradley K. Wilson, 1998), Carroll (Carroll, 1993), Guiso (Gusio, 1992) and others. These models each have their own characteristics while maintaining the basic properties and conclusions of the underlying theory: Zeldes’s model can calculate the optimal value of precautionary saving; Dynan’s model, after adding liquidity constraints, has a very strong capacity to explain reality; Wilson’s model advanced a great step in measuring income risk; and Carroll, a resolute supporter of the theory of precautionary saving, further put forward the “buffer-stock model”, giving a new and further explanation of consumption behaviour. All these theories are new branches extending from the great tree of the theory of precautionary saving; each branch approaches and simulates actual consumption behaviour and saving behaviour from a different direction and a different level.

2.3 The Impetus Given to Consumption Theory by Econometric and Simulation Methods

While theoretical and normative models gradually developed, the contradictions between models still existed, and the “refutations” of models by real economic data also appeared continually. If the differences between theoretical models may still be hoped to be unified through more careful specification of functional forms, variables and conditions of assumption, the conflict between models and real data is very difficult to resolve. The development of consumption theory has advanced continually not only in normative economic assumptions and modelling; new progress from econometric data and econometric methods has also impelled the continual progress of consumption theory, and the power of these indisputable facts from the real world is stronger still.

2.3.1 The Choice of Econometric Proxy Variables and the Development of Consumption Theory

Proving theoretical models by proxy variables is the method most commonly used by econometrics; its basic thought is: (1) construct a theoretical model under given conditions; (2) find proxy variables in actually obtainable economic data that match the definitions of the model, then re-derive conclusions through concrete econometric methods and compare them with the theoretical model; (3) judge the degree of applicability of the theory. For consumption theory, this seemingly simple process became more and more complex as the nature of the models changed.

When testing macroeconomic consumption theory, the consumption variable is only a macroeconomic aggregate variable, standing alongside aggregate investment, government expenditure, net trade and gross domestic product, and the statistical data are relatively easily obtained large-scale aggregate data. Simon Kuznets did his preliminary empirical research on Keynes’s consumption function by just such a choice of macroeconomic proxy variables: he overlapped and re-organised the relevant American data for 1896–1938, and then, through these processed data, calculated the final conclusion that the average propensity to consume is stable.

Starting from Keynes’s consumption function, aggregate data should be able to prove his inferences about the propensities to consume, but Kuznets falsified them. It was precisely the conclusion of this empirical research that aroused the doubts of theoretical circles about Keynes’s consumption function, and the point of entry of many economists’ in-depth research was econometrics through re-organising macroeconomic data. Arthur Smithies re-organised the American data for 1923–1940, estimating the long-run consumption function and the short-run consumption function from the observed values of income levels in different years and the corresponding observed values of consumption levels. There is a great deal more empirical research of this large-scale kind; but there are only two kinds of conclusion: either falsifying Keynes’s consumption function, or fitting it by changing the approximate shape of the consumption function. So although this kind of empirical research is very convenient in the use of proxy variables, its conclusions are very unsatisfactory.

After consumption theory turned to forward-looking research, the logicality and rationality of the models strengthened markedly, and at the same time the difficulty of econometric testing also increased with it. The basis of forward-looking theory is micro individual optimisation, and the differences produced by the behaviour of different regions and different strata are difficult to express with a uniform standard proxy variable. This is what produced the contradictions in empirical conclusions for the same theory in different countries, at different ages, facing different consumer goods, and so on. For example, “income” and “consumption”, data series very easily obtained at the macroeconomic scale, are almost unobtainable at the micro level; and “risk”, again, can be very well defined in theoretical models, but how it should actually be measured in empirical work became a difficult problem. Nevertheless, economists still tried as far as possible to find seemingly reasonable methods of research, and the contradictions likewise still exist. Fisher (Fisher, 1956) and Friedman (Friedman, 1957) used “occupation” as the proxy variable measuring “risk” and confirmed the theory of precautionary saving, while the research done by Guiso, Jappelli and Terlizzese (Gusio, Jappelli, Terlizzese, 1992) using the same proxy variable falsified this theory. Skinner (Jonathan Skinner, 1988) used the “mode of receiving salary” as the proxy variable for “risk”, and the result was that he discovered that businessmen and the self-employed who receive an annual salary save less than employees who receive a monthly salary, obtaining a conclusion contrary to “precautionary saving”. Some economists also proceeded from the pattern of consumption expenditure to look for proxy variables for “risk”. Dardanoni (Dardanoni, 1991) found, from the aspect of occupation, evidence that average consumption decreases as the variance of income increases. Dynan (Dynan, 1993) discovered that when consumption behaviour is considered in combination with education, occupation and industry, changes in consumption become not obvious. Carroll (Carroll, 1994) also discovered that the coefficient of variation of future income has no statistical significance for current consumption. A great deal of research also shows that expenditure on health insurance, medical insurance and old-age insurance, and the state of the household’s asset portfolio, have a certain persuasive capacity regarding the consumption model and precautionary saving. There is a great deal of empirical research of this kind; in particular, the ultimate purpose of empirical research that takes the verification of micro models as its goal is in every case to find a proxy variable that can both grasp “income uncertainty” and be unrelated to the other factors affecting saving, so as to prove that “income fluctuation” or “risk” directly causes “precautionary saving” and thereby affects consumption behaviour. However, this attempt to find micro proxy variables to prove the models has great difficulty in finding suitable micro data, to the point that some economists directly adopt the method of household surveys, having individual consumers report for themselves the subjective risk regarding future income, and measure by this means the uncertainty facing consumers. Guiso (Gusio, 1992) used the data of the Italian “Survey of Household Income and Wealth” for 1989–1990, utilising the uncertainty about future income subjectively estimated by people to research its influence on consumption. This method avoids the drawbacks created by using proxy variables, but is constrained by the degree of understanding of the questionnaire by the respondents.

In general, methods of this kind that use proxy variables and survey data to verify theoretical models directly are still in a state of confusion; but the situation of contradictions coexisting nevertheless developed the depth of research in rich directions and impelled the development of consumption theory.

2.3.2 The Impetus Given to Consumption Theory by Simulation Methods

The method of data simulation means: first constructing a purely theoretical model under given conditions of assumption, then through processing and calculation of the model obtaining equations that are easier to measure in form, and then testing the model through easily obtained data. There is another kind of simulation method: directly using computer software, on the basis of set parameter values, to depict the characteristics of the relations between the variables in the objective function, then handing the whole calculation and proof process over to the computer to complete, and finally proving the model by comparing the conclusions output by the computer with actual data. The main difficulties and divergences of the first kind of simulation method appear in two aspects: one is the specification of the utility function, the other is the gulf between micro behaviour and macroeconomic data; the difficulty of the second kind of simulation method lies in the writing and debugging of programmes and the obtainability of data.

In the first kind of simulation method, the individual’s most commonly used utility function is assumed to be of the following form:

$$U = V(c_1, c_2, c_3, \ldots, c_T)$$

Because in practice it is impossible for the individual to begin calculating optimisation only after his life has ended, this utility function is often specified as “intertemporally separable”, that is: $U = v_1(c_1) + v_2(c_2) + v_3(c_3) + \ldots + v_T(c_T)$; where each individual period’s “sub-utility” is an increasing concave function of total utility. Heckman (Heckman, 1971, 1974) and Ghez and Becker (Ghez, Becker, 1975) first researched this characteristic; the first-order condition finally obtained is:

$$\lambda_t(c_t) = v_t'(c_t) = \mu(1+r)^{-t}$$

where $\mu$ is the Lagrangian multiplier and $r$ is the real interest rate.

On this basis, for convenience of calculation, the utility function is often given the following two most commonly used forms:

(a)

$$U(c) = \frac{C^{1-\rho}}{1-\rho}$$

(b)

$$U(c) = -\frac{1}{\theta}e^{(-\theta c)}$$

Dynan (Dynan, 1993), using the utility function of form (b), calculated that the consumer’s optimisation condition is:

$$\left(\frac{1+r_i}{1+\delta}\right)E_t\left[U'(C_{i,i+1})\right] = U'(C_{it})$$

After “Taylor” expansion one can obtain the behavioural condition of the consumer containing uncertainty:

$$E\left[\frac{C_{i,t+1} - C_{it}}{C_{it}}\right] = \frac{1}{\xi}\left(\frac{r_i - \delta}{1+r_i}\right) + \frac{\rho}{2}E_t\left[\left(\frac{C_{i,t+1} - C_{it}}{C_{it}}\right)^2\right]$$

The purpose of calculating in this way is that the data on the variables of the expression above can be measured through grouped surveys. Using the data of the 1985 “Consumer Expenditure Survey”, Dynan’s conclusion was: precautionary saving is indeed closely related to occupational choice, but the results in the aspect of the “prudence motive” contradict the generally acknowledged risk aversion.

Wilson (Bradley K. Wilson, 1998), on the basis of the type (b) utility function, found a new econometric model by distinguishing durable and non-durable goods. He assumed the utility function to be of the following form:

$$U(CN_t, K_t) = -\frac{1}{\gamma}e^{-\gamma CN_t} - \frac{1}{\rho}e^{-\rho K_t}$$

The first-order condition is:

$$U_{CN}(CN_t, K_t) = \left(\frac{1+r}{1+\beta}\right)\frac{1}{P_D}U_K(CN_t, K_t)$$

Using the method of “Taylor” expansion in the same way, one obtains respectively the changes in durable and non-durable goods:

$$\Delta CN_t = \frac{\gamma - \beta}{\gamma} + \frac{\gamma w^2}{2}\mathrm{var}_{t-1}(Y_{t-1}) + \varepsilon_{CN_t}$$$$\Delta CD_t = \frac{(\gamma - \beta)\delta}{\rho} + \frac{\rho\theta^2}{2}\mathrm{var}_{t-1}(Y_t) - \frac{\rho\theta^2}{2}(1-\delta)\mathrm{var}_{t-2}(Y_{t-1}) - (1-\delta)\varepsilon_{CD,t-1} + \varepsilon_{CD,t}$$

Wilson, using the relevant American data on durable and non-durable consumer goods for 1954–1993, measured econometrically and discovered: if 15 per cent of quarterly income is risky income, precautionary saving will account for 42–55 per cent of total saving; if 30 per cent is risky income, then all saving will be precautionary saving.

Carroll (Carroll, 1993), using the type (a) utility function, obtained the “buffer-stock model” under the conditions of individual optimality, in which he used the concept of the “Equivalent Precautionary Premium” put forward by Kimball (Kimball, 1990) to re-measure the value of precautionary saving, and with an empirical survey by the US Federal Reserve (The US Federal Reserve, 1983) confirmed that coping with contingencies is the main reason for the existence of precautionary saving. His research also confirmed (1) that consumption and income growth grow contemporaneously over a fairly long time; (2) that in the short run, consumption and income often deviate; and (3) the fact that households always tend to hold wealth of relatively high liquidity. These results, which follow naturally in Carroll’s model, are precisely what the standard LCH-PIH and the Keynesian macroeconomic consumption function cannot reasonably explain.

The research above not only found all kinds of empirical evidence agreeing with the life-cycle theory and the theory of precautionary saving; the technical means it used also constantly reminded theoretical researchers of the direction of research. Although consumption theory, after several decades of rapid development, can already explain a great many consumption phenomena, economists still raise all kinds of questions, such as: (1) to what degree does the additivity of the utility function hold? (2) how should time and preferences be treated? (3) can replacing “utility” with “expected utility” completely express the meaning of uncertainty? (4) are the individual’s risk-avoiding and intertemporal substitution behaviours related? (5) if there is no uncertainty, should consumption be determined by income? (6) how exactly are the age structure of the group and the individual life cycle reflected in models and econometric data? (7) whom exactly does the representative consumer represent? (8) how close is the relation between the interest rate and consumption? (10) if consumers do not discount lifetime wealth at the interest rate, can abandoning the consumption theory that takes the interest rate as its starting point resolve the contradictions of the original theories? and so on. These questions are also the guide that leads us to continue researching consumption theory.

2.4 Consumption and Financial Markets

Consumption and financial markets are closely linked together through “liquidity constraints” and the “consumption-based capital asset pricing theory” (C-CAPM). In the LCH-PIH model, free borrowing is regarded as existing by assumption beforehand, which enables the individual, when income deviates from the target consumption path, to resolve this deviation through financial borrowing. In fact, free borrowing is not feasible at every time, in every place and for every individual, and this impelled economists to question the life-cycle theory from the aspect of constraints on borrowing. Stiglitz and Weiss (Stiglitz and Weiss, 1981) first rigorously proved, from the angle of asymmetric information, that “credit rationing” does indeed exist, and Hayashi (Hayashi, 1987) also reviewed the mechanisms involved. Since borrowing constraints do indeed exist, what influence will this result have on consumption? Hall and Mishkin (Hall and Mishkin, 1982), under the condition of assuming that “consumers merely spend their income”, reconstructed the life-cycle model to test the influence of borrowing constraints on consumption. Zeldes (Zeldes, 1989a) used data on consumers who cannot borrow and discovered that borrowing constraints are an important reason for the failure of the life-cycle model. Schechtman (Schechtman, 1976) and Bewley (Bewley, 1977) established a permanent income model with borrowing constraints; the result was: even if borrowing constraints exist, the consumer’s behaviour will in the end still converge to the average value of static income. Pischke (Pischke, 1991), using the micro data of consumers with liquidity constraints, constructed an econometric model and calculated and analysed aggregate consumption behaviour, making an expansion in the aspect of micro aggregation. These in-depth studies of borrowing behaviour are closely connected with financial markets. Under conditions of a perfect capital market, the existence of consumption credit will certainly change the external environment and the constraints of consumption, and thereby influence consumption behaviour. At the same time, this borrowing behaviour allows the consumer to use future income in advance at a certain cost, which determines that the budget constraint does not exist only temporarily but exists within the life cycle. If in addition the financial market permits it, changes in a resident’s lifetime assets and wealth will all have an influence on current consumption, and this is precisely an important reason why economists attach importance to borrowing constraints and financial markets. Deaton (Angus Deaton, 1992) researched this problem in depth; in it he assumed: borrowing is infeasible and at the same time the level of assets is always non-negative; the consumer has additive intertemporal preferences; and the rate of time preference and the rate of return on assets are both constant. The Euler equation of intertemporal optimisation derived from this is:

$$\lambda(c_t) = \max\left[\lambda(x_t), \frac{1+r}{1+\delta}E_t\lambda(c_{t+1})\right]$$

That is: when borrowing constraints exist, the marginal utility of consumption will not be smaller than the marginal utility of money. Even so, whether borrowing constraints exist is still an important reason distinguishing consumer behaviour; comparing the margin of money with the margin of consumption will become the former’s daily work, even if over his whole life borrowing constraints exist only at some point in time. Correspondingly, those consumers who pay attention only to the marginal utility of consumption being greater than the discounted value of its expected marginal utility will underestimate the importance of liquidity constraints for consumer behaviour.

On the empirical side, Murray (Murray, 1997) and Murphy (Murphy, 1998) researched the relation between household debt and household credit, and the relation between debt and future consumption. They held that high household debt means high financial risk, which will lead the household to reduce consumption. Garner (Garner, 1996) also discovered that variables measuring debt burden and credit rating have no significant influence on the growth of consumption. Dean M. Maki proceeded directly from the economic meaning of the various concepts. He put forward that there are many measuring instruments for measuring the debt burden, while the concept of debt burden itself is unclear, and different debt structures display different characteristics of economic growth.

Credit has already become an important route for alleviating the consumer’s income constraints, and consumption credit in developed countries has also already become an important indicator for testing the degree of macroeconomic development. In China, credit directed at consumption is undeveloped: not only is the individual credit system backward, there are also no supporting laws such as a corresponding “personal bankruptcy law”, and this leads to borrowing constraints continuing to exist. Research on the individual balance sheet, and research on the consumption financial market, should therefore be one of the directions of theoretical research.

Another theoretical route connecting consumption and asset prices is the “consumption-based capital asset pricing (CCAPM) theory”. The core of this theory is: because per capita consumption is related to the consumption flow of the representative consumer assumed by the theory, and the consumer’s consumption flow is closely related to income and assets, the risk of the assets held by the consumer can be defined according to the covariance of per capita consumption and returns, so that the systematic risk of the expected rate of return on assets can be explained by the rate of growth of consumption. Lucas (Robert Lucas, 1978) and Douglas Breeden (Douglas Breeden, 1979) first described this theory, and Jagannathan (Jagannathan, 1985), Cooper (Cooper, 1992) and Merton (Merton, 1973) also demonstrated the same view respectively. From the core of the CCAPM one can discover the difference of this theoretical direction: it uses consumption to price assets in reverse; its ultimate goal is not consumption but asset prices. This does not affect our incorporating this theory into consumption theory, for asset prices and returns do indeed influence consumer behaviour. Today a great many Chinese households hold shares, houses or other types of assets, and fluctuations in the prices of these assets have indeed been discovered to influence people’s consumption behaviour; although such problems in this field as the “equity premium puzzle” are not the most important problems for this dissertation to explain, we still need to examine the influence of asset markets on consumption.

2.5 Research on Consumption Theory by Chinese Scholars

Research on consumption theory by Chinese scholars is mainly concentrated on research using Chinese data to confirm or falsify consumption theory; individual scholars made new revisions in the utility function and in the consumption behaviour of Chinese households. Zang Xuheng (2001) carried out a comparatively comprehensive research on consumption theory, doing careful research in all aspects: the consumption behaviour of urban and rural residents (2007), the influence of income distribution on consumption (2005), consumption and asset choice (2003), the empirical testing of precautionary saving in China (2001), institutional change and consumption behaviour (1995), the relation between consumption content and life-cycle theory (1995), and so on. His basic conclusions can be summed up as: (1) the interest rate does not have much influence on residents’ consumption behaviour; (2) risk in such aspects as employment and income has an obvious influence on consumption; (3) asset prices, especially house prices, have a very great influence on consumption behaviour; (4) the economic system and the monetary environment have a certain influence on consumption behaviour. From the results of Zang Xuheng’s research, classical Western consumption theory fitted the Chinese economy before economic transition rather well, but divergences arose for consumption behaviour thereafter. Domestic economists therefore began to focus on researching, under the conditions of economic transition, the relation between the market economic system and the consumption behaviour of Chinese residents and the classical Western models, and their applicability. Zhang Jie (1997), Jin Xiaotong and Yang Xiaodong (2004) and others held that the life-cycle model does not accord with the consumption characteristics of Chinese residents, and that its explanatory power is therefore limited. The views of Jin Xiaotong and Yang Xiaodong in particular grasped the substance of the consumption characteristics of Chinese households; they summed them up in four hypotheses: (1) usually, within a given period, a consumer’s level of consumption is directly proportional to the level of his income; (2) Chinese households do not seek utility maximisation within the life cycle, but pursue utility maximisation at different stages; (3) household expenditure “determines expenditure according to income”, avoiding borrowing as far as possible; (4) under the conditions of income constraints and credit constraints, consumption is divided into basic consumption and additional consumption: only after basic consumption has been satisfied is additional consumption considered, rather than the two being considered at the same time. The two authors supported their views by citing data; although they did not adopt the methods of normative econometrics and simulation, their train of thought is still an important foundation of this dissertation.

Yu Yongding and Li Jun (2000) completely negated the validity of the life-cycle model for China. They held that “the uncertainty of long-term future goals; the lack of information; and institutional constraining factors, such as the absence of a personal loan market and a rental market” are all important causes of the “myopia” of Chinese consumers. They also held that the expenditure of Chinese residents has a marked stage-based character: “the typical division is into four stages: before marriage, after marriage, supporting children, and retirement”. Reconstructing the individual utility maximisation model and the budget constraint, the main conclusions they drew show once again that, for Chinese households, income and prices, and expectations of prices, are the key to consumption, while the interest rate is not the emphasis.

Sun Feng (2001) researched the precautionary saving behaviour of Chinese residents. On the basis of life-cycle theory, she constructed an econometric model:

$$\Delta C_t = \alpha_1'\Delta Y_t + \alpha_2'(Y_{t-1} - C_{t-1}) + \alpha_3'\delta_t$$

Using the data for 1991–1998, after a quantitative test she obtained the following conclusions: income has a significant positive influence on consumption; uncertainty has a negative influence on consumption; and at the same time the previous period’s income and the long-run equilibrium have a significant influence on current consumption. The first two conclusions are basically the same as those of other research, but the third conclusion shows that Chinese residents have already begun to possess a life-cycle consciousness and are changing their myopic (Myopia) behaviour.

He Juhuang (1998) used the normative method of data simulation to research the applicability of the life-cycle model in China. Assuming that people start work at the age of 20 and that the rate of growth of wages is fixed at $g$, a lifetime’s income, assets and consumption of the individual can be given by the following equations:

$$Y(x) = \begin{cases} W(20)(1+g)^{x-20} & 20 \leq x \leq L - 1 - 20 \\ 0 & L + 20 \leq x \leq N - 1 + 20 \end{cases}$$

(1)

$$B(x) = \begin{cases} Y(x) + Y(x+1) + Y(x+2) + \ldots + Y(L - 1 + 20) & 20 \leq x \leq L - 1 - 20 \\ 0 & L + 20 \leq x \leq N - 1 + 20 \end{cases}$$

(2)

$$B(x) = \begin{cases} Y(x)(L + 20 - x) & 20 \leq x \leq L - 1 - 20 \\ 0 & L + 20 \leq x \leq N - 1 + 20 \end{cases}$$

(1) is [the case in which] the consumer has anticipated future income growth; (2) is [the case in which] the consumer has not anticipated future income growth.

$$C(x) = [A(x) + B(x)]/(N + 20 - x)$$

(without liquidity constraints)

$$C(x) = \mathrm{Min}\left[A(x) + B(x)/(N + 20 - x), Y(x) + A(x)\right]$$$$A(x) = A(x - 1) + Y(x - 1) - C(x - 1), \quad A(20) = 0$$

(with liquidity constraints)

After calculating the functional expressions for consumption and saving and then carrying out numerical simulation, the basic conclusions obtained were: (1) if the consumer anticipates future income growth and there are no liquidity constraints, the change in the social saving rate is contrary to the standard life-cycle theory; (2) when income growth exceeds 4 per cent and there are no liquidity constraints, the relation between income and saving also does not accord with life-cycle theory; (3) only under the conditions in which the consumer cannot anticipate future income growth, and the mode of expectation is “adaptive expectations”, is life-cycle theory confirmed. He Juhuang’s research conclusions are thus closely related to the conditions for the existence of liquidity and to the formation of income expectations.

Pei Chunxia (2002, 2004) researched liquidity constraints and residents’ saving behaviour by empirical methods. She held that liquidity constraints do indeed exist in China, and that an important cause of their production is institutional change. Among these, the distortion of the interest-rate structure, high transaction costs, monopoly high prices and the polarisation between rich and poor are the core inducements creating this situation. She therefore put forward that improving the efficiency of financing, launching private investment and eliminating liquidity constraints are routes to changing China’s low consumption rate.

Song Zhenxue and Zang Xuheng (2007) reconstructed a new “segmentally increasing utility function” to replace the utility function of the standard model, and used this utility function to re-analyse the consumption behaviour of Chinese households. They also agreed that Chinese households have a certain “stage-based character” in consumption, and that each stage has a different utility for the household. On the other hand, consumers also differ from one another, and summarising aggregate behaviour with a uniform utility function definitely produces all kinds of incoherence, such as attitudes towards risk. He therefore put forward lowering expectations of income uncertainty and improving the credit market, giving residents the choice of smoothing the consumption path. Finally, importance must also be attached to the population structure, to avoid violent fluctuations of consumption over a larger cycle.

[Figure 2-4: a block flow diagram of the non-linear consumption-optimisation model: macroeconomic conditions, employment status, social-security status, and the interest rate and money supply feed into residents’ labour income, social-insurance income and financial-asset income, and hence into total income, disposable funds and initial financial assets; these drive the consumption–saving decision module, bank deposits and saving, and the investment-decision module and stock holdings, with feedback from investment returns to financial-asset income.]

They expressed the investment decision objective function, the consumption constraint, the income constraint, financial assets, and each of the macroeconomic modules separately by functions. Finally, after setting parameters such as the consumer’s age and structure of working duration, the rate of growth of labour income, the inflation rate and the interest rate, they carried out simulation optimisation by computer and compared various different schemes. The general conclusions of the model were: (1) consumers who pay attention to the whole life cycle, because of the stage-based character of income, the stability of the growth of regular consumption demand and the variability of irregular consumption, must increase saving in the middle-aged period of the life cycle; (2) for those consumers who pay attention to the later period of the life cycle, their consumption will change together with inflation, and individual saving and assets will definitely present a “hump” (Hump) shape.

There is a great deal of literature by Chinese scholars in the field of research on consumption theory, and the directions of development are not entirely the same either; but in general the path of evolution followed is basically similar to the path of development of Western consumption theory — namely: from certainty to uncertainty; from backward-looking theory to forward-looking theory; from macroeconomic analysis to a micro foundation. China is experiencing marketization and will continue to perfect the function of the market in allocating resources; both micro behaviour and macroeconomic data are experiencing unique changes, and constructing a suitable theory and explaining these unique phenomena is an important task for Chinese economists. It is gratifying that some scholars have already begun to attach importance to the differences between Chinese residents and households and those of the West, attempting to proceed from these differences to look for a theory of consumption that accords better with China’s reality. In methods, we also see that, besides traditional econometric methods, normative numerical simulation has also gradually begun to be used by economists. The use of all kinds of advanced methods has also caused Chinese scholars’ research on consumption theory to begin to enter a stage of refinement, and the stage of large-scale use of macroeconomic data is gradually being replaced by refined high-frequency data and precise econometrics and simulation.

2.6 The Direction of Development of Consumption Theory

Through the summary and review of consumption theory above, one can discover that, from its appearance to its further in-depth development, consumption theory at every stage accorded with the overall macroeconomic background, and at the same time embodied the requirements of the universal path of the scientific development of economic theory: from macroeconomic extensive research to micro refined research, and from simple logical exposition to the use of mathematical tools to carry out empirical testing. Every advance of consumption theory was the result of contradictions existing between theory and reality and between theories, and the resolution of contradictions at the same time impelled new advances in consumption theory.

After sixty years of socialist economic construction, thirty years of reform and opening, and several decades of integration with the world economy, China has gradually formed an economic system and institutions with Chinese characteristics. It was precisely this special character that helped China smoothly ride out the Asian financial crisis of 1997 and the world financial crisis of 2008; and it is precisely this special character that brings new problems and challenges to China’s economy. For China, a populous country, consumption and domestic demand that are always insufficient, and even decline year by year, have become a major problem that China’s economy must face if it is to grow in a sustained and healthy way. After all, the ultimate purpose of economic development is to raise the welfare of its own people, and the attainment and raising of this welfare must be consummated through consumption, that final link. The development of consumption theory must therefore make a contribution to explaining and solving this problem. Although China’s economy has its special character, after the standard theories have unravelled it thread by thread, the special must in the end merge into the flood of the general — and this transformation is also the effort this dissertation wishes to make.
