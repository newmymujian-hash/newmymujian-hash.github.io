Building China's Social Welfare System During Economic Adjustment

Full English translation. On building a social welfare system as China faces its first market-based cyclical adjustment.

✦ 24 min read

Jian Mu

School of Economics, Renmin University of China, Beijing 100872

Originally published in Chinese in Theory Horizon (Lilun Jie), 2009, No. 12, pp. 6–8. Translated from the author’s manuscript.

Abstract: A financial crisis originating in the United States is profoundly affecting China’s economy. For the world, this is the second great economic recession since 1929; but for China, this financial crisis, which has already spread to it, is the first cyclical adjustment it has faced under marketization. China has established a socialist market economy system, but the building of the related welfare system remains incomplete. Against this background, China will face risks different from those of the past; these risks will affect future economic development and the improvement of welfare levels. Avoiding these risks will ultimately depend on appropriate economic instruments and on the building of democratic institutions.

Keywords: marketization; economic adjustment; welfare system

By 2008, China’s reform and opening up had been under way for a full thirty years. Over that period China’s economy, politics, science and technology, and culture all achieved considerable development, and the transitions from centralization to democracy, from plan to market, and from a traditional society to a law-based and civilized society were all highly striking — in the economic sphere above all. The thirty years of reform and opening up not only enabled China’s gross national product to grow continuously at high speed; they also increased the share of technology and capital in the economic structure. This shows that China’s economy has gradually been shifting from an extensive to an intensive pattern, from a planned economy to a market economy, and from an economy of shortage to an economy of surplus. At the same time, however, over these thirty years the market’s natural orientation towards efficiency first destroyed the former “big pot” system of wages and welfare, and workers were transformed from “unit persons” into “social persons.” Although the people’s welfare has increased in absolute terms, as economic efficiency and aggregate output increase further, the problem of fairness has unavoidably and gradually come to the fore. To mitigate this deficiency of the market economy, which emphasizes efficiency and neglects fairness, China has established various welfare systems and social security systems to complement and make up for the defects of the market economy. The welfare system that has gradually developed and been perfected over the thirty years has played an important role in safeguarding fairness and maintaining stability. But under the impact of the 2008 financial crisis, China’s economy will also face a major adjustment. This places new demands on China’s economic development and on the building of its welfare system, and it is at the same time an opportunity and a challenge for building a harmonious society.

I. China Faces Its First Market-Based Cyclical Economic Adjustment

The financial crisis of 2008 had a grave impact on the world economy, and China’s economy has likewise been hit. First, the sharp fall in exports led many export enterprises to go bankrupt, and workers’ unemployment seriously affected their income levels — and these workers are important sources of income within their households. In addition, turmoil in the capital market shrank residents’ wealth. The great adjustment of the Chinese economy brought about by the financial crisis will therefore affect the welfare of a great many Chinese households. This economic adjustment, however, differs markedly from the other adjustments of the thirty years of reform and opening up.

1. The First, Fundamental Adjustment of 1978

In 1978 China formally began to enter the period of reform and opening up. Politically, emancipating the mind and seeking truth from facts became the guiding principles, and the people’s actual livelihood became the fundamental starting point of all work. In the economic sphere, the traditional planned-economy system began to loosen; the most important reform in the economic sphere began in the countryside, in the form of the household responsibility system characterized by the contracting of land to households. This responsibility-system reform thoroughly solved the problem of food and clothing for farmers and laid a good foundation for the next stage of economic reform. It can be said that this economic adjustment in China was led by innovation in political reform, and that it opened the prelude to the sustained development of China’s economy.

2. The Second Adjustment of 1992

In 1992, Deng Xiaoping’s southern tour speeches completely freed people from the debates over capitalism and socialism, making clear, with a pragmatic and objective attitude, that whatever the social system, the people’s livelihood must be placed first and the importance of economic development must be placed first. In this period, therefore, state-owned enterprises began to be contracted out in various forms; the market economy formally entered the field of resource allocation in China as an instrument; price effects began to appear; and the economy showed marked growth. This major economic adjustment likewise took the form of political signals first and economic reform following after.

3. The Third Adjustment of 1998

By 1998, China had basically established the socialist market economy system, although it was not yet very mature. The market played an increasingly obvious role in allocating resources, but its capacity for self-recovery was still not strong. The return of Hong Kong in 1997 brought opportunities to China’s capital market, and both the product market and the capital market in China showed a good growth trend; but that year also saw the Asian financial crisis. Hong Kong, a world financial centre, was seriously affected, and against this background the mainland, too, experienced such conditions as insufficient domestic demand. With the market in crisis, the government promptly applied a proactive fiscal policy, a prudent monetary policy, and a series of related measures, reversing the economic situation on the mainland, including Hong Kong, and at the same time prompting China to begin perfecting a risk-prevention system in its capital market. Judging from this process, this adjustment of the Chinese economy likewise began with accompanying political measures.

4. The Financial Crisis of 2008

China’s socialist market economy system has now become increasingly perfected: market actors, the market environment, and the monetary system have gradually developed, and at the same time China has been a member of the World Trade Organization for seven years, with many commercial rules aligned with those of the world. This makes it inevitable that the Chinese economy has become part of the world economy as a whole. Within that system, China is mainly engaged in processing and export trade, which is typically labour-intensive. This characteristic, while absorbing a large amount of labour and giving play to China’s comparative advantage, also means that fluctuations in international markets will have a major impact on the employment, income, and welfare of the Chinese people — as this financial crisis fully demonstrates. Unlike the previous occasions, this adjustment is not obviously connected with political adjustment: although governments at all levels have introduced measures to stimulate the economy, those are simply the functions of government in a market-economy environment. What we see, rather, is market actors responding rationally to the crisis according to the laws of the market.

This is, therefore, the first market-based adjustment that China’s economy has faced, and this crisis has accordingly become an opportunity to test the degree of perfection of China’s market system and the capacity of the government, enterprises, and other market actors to cope with crisis.

II. The Lagging Reform and Building of China’s Social Welfare Brings Risks to the Fore

In neoclassical economic theory, the simplest enterprise produces by relying on labour and capital, and labour, in turn, depends on effective labour — that is, on the quantity of labour that actually produces output. The quality and capability of workers must therefore be included in the production function. From a macroeconomic perspective, the quantity and quality of labour also affect GDP, and thereby the welfare of the members of society. From the perspective of welfare economics, Amartya Sen founded the analytical framework of the “capability approach,” defining development as the expansion of human capability and the enhancement of freedom, and linking capability with freedom and welfare: he holds that “development” should ultimately be manifested as the expansion of people’s capability and the extension of their freedom, and not merely as the growth of GDP, but as the growth of human potential, capability, and degree of freedom — and that all of this growth is to be realized through appropriate institutional arrangements.

From whichever perspective, then, raising a country’s level of economic development and improving the welfare of its members of society should begin with raising the quality and capability of workers. For China in particular, now that the economy faces a major adjustment, the understanding of growth should all the more be transformed, and the view of development should be located in people’s capability, quality, and freedom. For a country to raise and enhance its level in these respects, it must start from the welfare of society as a whole, providing security in such areas as medical care, education, old-age support, and housing. In the era of the planned economy, the lives of China’s residents were mainly attached to the “unit”: wages, housing, old-age support, and medical care were all of this kind. It can be said that such security was fair, and that such a system was suited to the planned-economy system. But as the market economy replaced the planned economy, such a welfare security system appeared very ill-adapted. For enterprises, expenditures such as housing and old-age support increase enterprise costs and affect their competitiveness in the market. China therefore began to socialize welfare security, transforming “unit persons” into “social persons,” thereby liberating enterprises so that they become competitive actors in the market, and at the same time liberating labour so that it becomes a labour resource adapted to the market economy; in this way the advantages of the market can be brought into play to allocate resources with high efficiency. In the process by which the market took effect, many workers lost their jobs and fell into poverty, becoming people in need of relief. This means the loss of their former welfare, and, in Sen’s theory, it is also the loss of workers’ capability. To raise the welfare of the members of society in a market-economy environment, one must therefore follow the laws of the market: it is necessary not only to increase social wealth, create jobs, and make transfer payments, but, more importantly, to increase the capability of the members of society through institutional reform.

1. The Current State of China’s Social Security and Welfare Reform

First, in urban areas, the “basic old-age insurance system for urban workers combining social pooling with individual accounts” was established and implemented in 1995. From the end of 2002 to 2008, the number of participants in urban basic old-age insurance nationwide rose from 111.288 million to 210.29 million. Second, in rural areas, China has also established a rural minimum living security system. The Basic Scheme for Rural Social Old-Age Insurance at the County Level (Trial) began to be piloted in 1992. By the end of 1998, 30 provinces, municipalities, and autonomous regions nationwide had developed rural social old-age insurance work, with 80.25 million participants. By the end of 2006, the number of participants in rural old-age insurance nationwide had reached 53.74 million. In addition, in medical care, unemployment, work injury, and maternity, China has also established security systems, with the number of participants rising year by year. The “basic medical insurance system for urban residents,” centred on pooling for major illnesses, began to be piloted in 2007; by the end of December, the number of participants in urban residents’ basic medical insurance nationwide was 40.68 million, of whom 25.83 million were in the 88 pilot cities. In minimum living security, by the end of 2007, a total of 22.709 million urban residents nationwide enjoyed the urban minimum living security.

These data show that, while complementing the building of the market economy and emphasizing economic efficiency, China has not neglected social security and welfare building, and has not ignored the problem of fairness. It is only that this fairness has gradually shifted from reliance on the “unit” to reliance on “society.”

2. Problems in the Existing Welfare Security System

Building a socialized security system in China, like building the socialist market economy, is an experience without precedent. Within it, there exist mainly several structural problems of the following kinds.

2.1 The structure of persons covered

China’s total population is large, and at the same time the structure of persons under the former system and that under the market-economy system still differ in some fields. This means that those who participate in the social security system at the present stage include urban residents, rural residents, and the floating population, and the security that society provides to these three categories of people is uneven. In particular, the floating population, and the non-employed population in cities and towns, have not yet entered the coverage of social security — and it is precisely among these that the people most in need of security are to be found. Although relevant policies have recently been introduced requiring employing units to give these employees corresponding security, the present state of labour supply and demand ultimately determines the price of labour, and enterprises in reality do not necessarily act as required.

2.2 The regional structure

Because China’s social security system is unsound and local protectionism exists, the division of interests among regions is serious: there are obstacles to transferring social security welfare between regions, the pooled portion cannot be taken out, and the receiving locality is unwilling to bear the additional burden. This greatly constrains the free movement of labour and talent and the realization of the goal of fairness.

2.3 The legislative structure

At present there is still no basic law specifically regulating social security relations. Social insurance, the core content of the social security system, has not yet established a unified social insurance legal system of relatively broad applicability, and the collection, payment, operation, and pooled management of social insurance contributions are not standardized. Legislation on social relief, social welfare, and preferential placement is considerably lacking. National legislation lags behind, local legislation is fragmented, and social security work in many areas can only be carried forward by relying on policy provisions and administrative means.

3. The Problems of Risk in the Economic Adjustment

China’s social welfare security system is an important component of the socialist market economy. In a period of economic adjustment such as the present one, the various existing problems bring risks — in economic, social, and political respects — that cannot but be taken seriously.

First, social welfare security problems affect economic efficiency.

The influence of the social security system on economic efficiency is indirect, but it is comprehensive and profound — all the more so as the world financial crisis gradually deepens into the real economy. China’s economically developed regions are mainly concentrated in the coastal provinces, and the enterprises in those regions are mostly processing-type export enterprises, which determines that their production depends heavily on international markets. This crisis, which broke out in the United States and began in the financial industry, was transmitted within a short time not only to other countries of the world but also to the level of the real economy, and many enterprises went bankrupt or were on the verge of bankruptcy. The unemployment rate thus rose, which directly led to a sharp fall in per capita consumption in the United States. This in turn left China’s export enterprises facing products with no market, falling profits, and, ultimately, the unemployment of Chinese workers. The increase in the number of unemployed means that labour resources cannot be effectively used: workers can obtain no remuneration, and enterprises likewise have no profits. Moreover, these workers are almost all the main sources of income in their households; their unemployment leads to a wider-ranging fall in consumption, which further leaves still more enterprises facing the problem of insufficient demand. All of this seriously affects the realization of economic efficiency.

In such an economic environment, the social security system ought to display its effect, playing the role of regulating and alleviating individuals’ economic pressure. But because of the various deficiencies of China’s security system, China faces greater risks in this great economic adjustment than countries with sound welfare systems.

Second, social welfare security problems affect social stability.

Because it embodies fairness, the social security welfare system plays the role of stabilizing society and alleviating social conflict. In the present period of economic adjustment, problems concerning income and unemployment, and problems that would not appear in times of prosperity, will increase people’s pressure; if at this time there is no good relief system and method, then when these pressures are released they will cause harm to society. Because of its structural problems, China’s present security system has not completely solved the security problems at each level and in each region, so when crisis appears, society’s latent stability faces a test. Economic pressure may be released through other channels, and these factors of instability will certainly also affect economic development.

Finally, welfare security problems affect people’s expectations, and thereby long-term economic development.

Modern macroeconomic theory, beginning with Keynes, tells us that the influence of expectations on the economy is profound and lasting, and that expectations are largely subjectively determined. The outbreak of an economic crisis often originates in people’s unstable expectations about the future, while economic prosperity is likewise associated with people’s optimistic expectations. At the present stage, China’s insufficient resident consumption is likewise due to unoptimistic expectations. Many households, because their main worker is unemployed, suffer a fall in total income and pessimistic expectations; at the same time, the various necessary welfare measures have not provided security in time, which further produces still greater pessimism about the future. Under circumstances such as the present, therefore, rational consumers will “smooth” their income across periods, reducing current consumption in order to provide self-insurance for the future.

III. Seizing the Counter-Cyclical Opportunity to Accelerate the Building of China’s Social Welfare System

The problems and risks analysed above all really exist in a market-economy environment. Against the background of China’s still-incomplete welfare security system, the special period of cyclical market-economy adjustment at present brings us not only crisis; more importantly, it brings opportunity.

1. Considering Employment from the Perspective of Welfare: Increasing Employment Is the Most Effective Measure for Effectively Implementing Growth Protection and Countering Recession

Apart from security at the spiritual level, the most important means of solving the welfare security that workers need is still to solve the problem of workers’ income. Raising the absolute amount of income is the starting point for fundamentally solving the welfare problem, and to solve the income problem one must first solve workers’ employment, so as to relieve the pressure of the crisis on the welfare security system. The government should therefore avoid a surge in the unemployment rate through such methods as increasing workers’ skills, broadening employment channels, and subsidizing enterprises that absorb employment. In addition, the government should also increase investment in the welfare industries so as to promote employment. An increase in employment can powerfully promote a rise in the level of consumption, which creates the conditions for maintaining the sustained and stable growth of China’s economy. China’s labour resources are abundant; this is a comparative advantage that other welfare states do not possess. In addition, differences in labour costs between China’s east and west make it possible for coastal export enterprises to transfer inland and continue to maintain their cost advantage. The government should therefore use the opportunity of this economic adjustment to guide coastal labour-intensive enterprises to transfer to the interior, realizing the local absorption of labour, and at the same time promoting the upgrading of coastal industries. Among China’s unemployed population there are not only people of insufficient capability but also university graduates, so promoting employment should, besides starting from industrial policy, also consider the matching of the labour-force structure with the industrial structure. Not only should people of insufficient capability be given certain training opportunities; people with knowledge should also be guided towards pragmatic employment.

The use of these economic instruments substantively strengthens workers’ own capacity for security, raises welfare levels, and improves consumers’ expectations about the future. In this way, against the background of economic adjustment, it is possible both to improve the people’s welfare level and at the same time to promote economic growth and resist the deepening of the recession.

2. Combining Institution Building with Short-Term Measures to Promote the Raising of Welfare Levels

Looking at long-term and near-term goals, institution building should be combined with short-term measures. Raising the people’s welfare level and building a fair and harmonious society are the purposes of establishing a social security system, and the realization of these purposes depends on short-term measures for economic growth, tax adjustment, policy tilts, and the like. But with a long-term view, what is more crucial is to rely on establishing a long-term security system: regulating, at the level of law, the relations of interest among people at different economic levels in society, regulating a fair starting point, guaranteeing the efficiency of the process, and ensuring that members of society can obtain corresponding relief when they need it. Such an economic and welfare system that gives equal weight to efficiency and fairness is the goal we ultimately pursue; at the same time, the further standardization of institutions can also further “harden” people’s expectations, which is precisely the benefit that establishing long-term institutions brings to economic development.

In concrete measures, one may consider transforming the welfare system from a rights type into a responsibility type, not only reducing workers’ dependence on the government but also institutionally strengthening workers’ sense of social responsibility and reducing inefficient government welfare expenditure; transforming the welfare system from a mechanism type into a compensation type, treating welfare, too, as an economic resource that is provided to a portion of people and requires “competition”; and, in addition, diversifying welfare, because welfare itself is not unitary — family affection, a sense of security, national pride, and other psychological feelings are all components of welfare. When welfare policies are formulated, these factors should therefore also be taken into account, providing welfare comprehensively to workers rather than only through material security.

3. Maintaining Economic Competitiveness on the Basis of Promoting Harmony and Preserving Stability

Over the thirty years of reform and opening up, there are many experiences to draw on, and that maintaining social stability is what enables economic development to be promoted may be the most fundamental, and also the most valuable, of all of them. Economic development requires a stable social environment, and economic development likewise promotes social stability; the two complement each other. But the economy pursues efficiency first, while social stability needs fairness first. If economic development can be achieved by relying on the market, then fairness requires the building of democratic institutions. Precisely under such differing purposes and modes of action there arise the trade-off between efficiency and fairness, and the topic of economic competitiveness and social stability and harmony.

On the one hand, from the perspective of raising economic efficiency, the market automatically allocates resources to the market actor who bids the highest price, and rational actors will offer purchase prices according to their own productive efficiency. So in a market-economy system resources are automatically allocated to producers with higher efficiency, which at the same time means that in the market there will always be a portion of people who cannot obtain resources. This requires society to provide these people with the necessary means of life through the channel of welfare. An increase in economic efficiency will thus help increase society’s total output, which means that the resources of the welfare channel will also become more abundant. As welfare rises, the social environment will also become more relaxed and stable; economic contradictions ultimately still need to be solved through economic instruments, and efficiency can promote social harmony and stability. On the other hand, social stability and welfare security not only directly provide the basic material means of life, but also raise human capital through education, medical care, and other respects, which will further raise economic efficiency and raise overall economic competitiveness.

In essence, therefore, the two are dialectically unified rather than opposed. And building such a system in which efficiency and fairness coexist harmoniously should begin with the building of democratic institutions. A democratic society first allows members of society to express their own preferences freely, and this first lays the foundation of fairness and harmony. Second, individual preferences can always form collective preferences; although the “impossibility theorem” exists, such collective preferences can still promote the raising of economic efficiency. Looking across the countries of the world, those with sound democratic institutions all show ample dynamism in their economic development, and as their economies develop, those countries’ welfare levels are also higher. This, from another side, promotes social stability, and society further becomes more democratic and civilized, forming a benign feedback mechanism. In a socialist country such as China, perfecting democratic institutions should be still more advantageous — not only because of the democratic nature that our socialist country itself possesses and the considerable development of our economy, but also because democratic institutions have a broad mass foundation in China.

At this critical moment, as the macro-economy undergoes its first cyclical adjustment under marketization, such important aspects of China’s economy as employment, consumption, investment, and exports have all fluctuated to different degrees, and gross national product is also undergoing the most profound deep adjustment of the thirty years of reform. Against this background, social democratic construction, including the social welfare security system, is likewise undergoing a test. But because of the dialectical relationship between economic efficiency and social fairness, this economic adjustment also provides the latter with an opportunity for further deepening and perfection. Seizing this opportunity to carry out institutional change can not only directly improve the people’s livelihood; it can also, over the long term, promote sustained and stable economic development and the building of a harmonious society.
