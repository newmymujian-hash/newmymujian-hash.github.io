Chapter 7 International Experience of the Transformation of Consumption Models and Policies for Launching Consumption

Under the market economy model, the contribution of China’s household consumption to economic growth has long been lower than that of the investment and trade sectors, and, compared with other countries, the contribution of consumption to the economy also lags considerably. Although, as a matter of theoretical logic, we cannot simply deduce what level of consumption rate is appropriate, or which country’s consumption model is the reasonable one, by comparing the differing economic foundations, consumption models, consumption policies and consumption-related policies of various countries we can still arrive at an orientation for the consumption model relatively suited to the next stage of China’s development. In addition, analysing and comparing the consumption models of different countries at different points in time can provide an appropriate starting point and frame of reference for China’s strategy for the transformation of its consumption model.

7.1 A Comparison of Consumption Models in Different Countries

This dissertation selects four relatively representative countries — the United States, Japan, India and Sweden — and, by analysing the characteristics of these countries’ consumption models, seeks to find a consumption model suited to the present state of China’s economic development. China is comparable with these four countries: first, all of them are typical marketized countries, while China too practises a market economy with Chinese characteristics, so that their economic environments have something in common; second, the four countries each have their own distinctive features in consumption models and policies — the United States, for example, advocates the supremacy of consumption, Japan upholds frugal consumption, Sweden’s consumption model is built on a high-welfare foundation, and India’s characteristics resemble China’s but its consumption model displays a marked dual structure. It is precisely these marked individualities that, from another side, provide directions for the transformation of China’s consumption model, whether to be developed or discarded.

If the macroeconomic data above express the phenomenon and the result of America’s high consumption rate, what are its causes? We may analyse them from the aspects of income, expenditure structure and mode of consumption.

First, American household incomes are high, and this is the foundation of all consumption; second, the level of social security in the United States is high and rationally arranged, which guarantees that consumers dare to consume; finally, the United States is the country with the most developed credit consumption in the world, and this mode of consumption has also greatly promoted the consumption behaviour of American consumers.

As regards the construction of the social security system, on 14 August 1935 the United States signed into law the Social Security Act, which marked the formal establishment of the American social security system. Although at that time its objects of assistance were limited to the elderly over 65, as economic strength gradually increased, the coverage of the security system gradually extended to every group, every aspect and every stage of society. The gradually strengthening force of security and America’s flourishing consumption also displayed an increasingly stable association with each other, so that, setting out from this aspect, it may be held that the social security system is also a foundation of America’s gradual advance towards a consumption society, and that the existence of the American social security system is therefore favourable to the development of consumption. There are many other assistance programmes within the American social security system; although some have rather harsh conditions, in terms of coverage and level of security they remain very high.

It can be seen that the American social security system embodies security in medical care, housing and daily life alike; in medical care, for example, approximately 63 per cent of employers provide medical insurance, about 60 per cent of the non-elderly enjoy health insurance, and of this the portion paid by workers themselves is only 25 per cent of the total. In this sense, hospitals and clinics are almost public facilities serving the poor population.

Looking at the consumption structure and the industrial structure, the upgrading of America’s industrial structure and of its consumption structure appeared relatively early: consumer products such as automobiles and household electrical appliances entered households almost as soon as they appeared, and American households thus passed rapidly from subsistence consumption to the pursuit of quality consumption. What arose along with this was a variety of financing products aimed at consumer products with relatively high prices, such as the specialised finance companies and financing instruments that appeared for the purchase of cars. In addition, because of the overall stability of the American economy and the relatively mature development of its financial markets, personal credit consumption in the United States developed relatively thoroughly: according to data of the Federal Reserve, from 1981 the total scale of credit in the United States began to climb markedly, developing from less than 1.4 trillion dollars in 1959 to nearly 9 trillion in 1981 and nearly 23 trillion by 1999, within which the form of credit consumption grew nearly tenfold. Wu Jingmei (2002) plotted this process of explosive growth of credit in the United States, from which it can clearly be seen that from 1981 credit, including personal consumption credit, entered a new stage of development, and has remained in a stage of stable development from that time on. This state of development of credit shows the important role of consumption in American economic development, and personal credit consumption has gradually become the principal mode of consumption.

In short, that the United States was able to embark on promoting economic growth through consumption is the result of the combined influence of various factors, and this model of high income, high consumption, low saving and even consumption on credit is not all advantages either. From the standpoint of objective factors, the United States is vast and sparsely populated with abundant resources, and in realising industrialisation and a per capita GDP of 10,000 dollars it had cheap resources and abundant labour to guarantee it. A well-functioning social security system and the upgrading of the consumption structure created American households’ pursuit of a high quality of life, for the sake of which they do not hesitate to consume on credit. According to a report of the United States Department of Commerce, in 2006 American personal saving was negative in all four quarters, which shows that individuals not only consumed that year’s disposable income but also drew on earlier saving or overdrew future income. What is more important is that the negative saving rate was not due to insufficient income but to excessive consumption, as can be seen from the data on the credit card debt accumulated by American consumers and on loans for housing, automobiles and electrical appliances.

On the other hand, the cost of living in the United States is relatively low, the Engel coefficient having long been stable below 20 per cent, and in 2004 the Engel coefficient was a mere 9 per cent. At the same time, the indicators for the housing, electrical appliances, automobiles and furniture owned by American households are far in the lead. All of this is an embodiment of the overall strength of the country, and also an embodiment of the state’s encouragement of consumption.

But this consumption model clearly cannot be promoted in every country, not only because there are objective disparities in countries’ economic strength, but also because China’s present state of development simply cannot reach such a level. Nevertheless, the American consumption model still has a certain significance as a reference for China, especially as regards the raising and use of funds in the construction of its social security system, and the development of consumption credit, which deserve serious study by the Chinese government.

7.1.2 The Consumption Model of Sweden

Sweden is a typical Nordic high-welfare country and at the same time a high-consumption country, its national consumption level ranking in the forefront among developed countries. In the consumption of Swedish residents, real estate and high-grade consumer goods predominate. In the property of an ordinary household, real estate makes up more than half, financial assets such as deposits are about 40 per cent of total assets, and high-grade durable consumer goods such as automobiles and yachts make up about another 10 per cent. Although the Swedish household saving rate is not high, this consumption model of low saving and high consumption differs from the American model, the most marked difference being that Sweden practises a nationwide policy of high taxation and high welfare.

All of these data show that the greatest characteristic of the Swedish economy is stability. The stability of growth, and the stability of the relations among economic variables, are not only brought about by the growth of the economy itself; more importantly, the high-welfare policies practised within Sweden provided consumers with a stable economic environment and greatly stimulated consumption.

The starting point of the establishment of Sweden’s social security system was the adoption of the National Pension Plan in 1913. Thereafter, as the economy developed further, Sweden’s social security system gradually became complete; today it broadly comprises three parts — social insurance, social welfare and medical insurance — among which pensions, the national health service and family education allowances are the most prominent items. Its coverage extends to all Swedish citizens, and its period of coverage is “from the cradle to the grave”, the welfare subsidies enjoyed at each stage not being all the same: in Sweden all of the elderly over 65 can obtain a pension, and the amount of the pension is unrelated to the wage before retirement; all persons with a fixed income need only pay 12.8 per cent of their total wages in order to enjoy fully free medical services at designated medical institutions; for households with children, the various education subsidies it provides cover every stage of a child’s growth completely — from 1980 to 2000, the coverage rate of care for children under six rose from 31 per cent to 66 per cent. At the same time, the foundation on which this broad and comprehensive social security exists is high taxation: data show that in 1979 the marginal rate of personal tax levied by the Swedish central government reached 87 per cent. At the same time, Sweden’s social security expenditure as a share of GDP also consistently ranks in the forefront among OECD countries: in 1987 Sweden accounted for 40.7 per cent, while in the same year Britain accounted for 25.5 per cent and the United States only 16.2 per cent; in 1983 the average per capita social security expenditure of the OECD countries was 1,929 dollars, while Sweden’s figure was as high as 3,649 dollars.

Therefore, although Sweden’s consumption model is also of the “high income, high consumption” type similar to America’s, its fundamental difference from the American model is that Sweden relies on high taxation while providing high welfare to provide consumers with a stable consumption environment, whereas the United States stimulates consumption on credit under the support of high-speed economic growth and a relatively stable social security system. According to this dissertation’s classification of consumption models, Sweden’s consumption model should in fact belong to the cash-flow consumption model at a high level; the characteristic of this consumption model is a high consumption rate, but the foundation of its existence must be a broad, long-term and high-level system of social security. According to the theoretical analysis above, under this high-level cash-flow consumption model, saving and other financial assets account for a relatively large share of total assets, and consumers do not tend towards consumption on credit, because high income and high welfare have already guaranteed that consumers can carry on their consumption activities smoothly without needing to incur liabilities. The shortcomings of this consumption model are manifested mainly in a heavy financial burden on the state, a heavy tax burden on residents, and, under conditions of high welfare for all, an inability to mobilise consumers’ enthusiasm for labour fully, which is unfavourable to raising economic efficiency. That Sweden was able to form such a consumption model is due on the one hand to the influence of socialist thought in the country, and more importantly to the backing of Sweden’s strong economic strength and superior ratio of population to resources. It was argued earlier in this dissertation that in the planned economy era China too in fact had a cash-flow consumption model, but because its weak economic resources could not support it, China was forced to abandon the planned economy. According to the calculations made earlier, China’s consumption rate in that period, under the cash-flow consumption model, was also very high, although its level was very low.

7.1.3 The Consumption Model of India

The Indian economy has many points in common with China’s economy, such as a large population, limited resources, a low level of industrialisation, and a large overall economic aggregate accompanied by various structural imbalances.

The figure below gives the trends of transformation of the growth rate of India’s household final consumption rate and the growth rate of GDP, and it can be seen that the relation between the two is also very stable.

Combining these two figures and data, we can basically infer that fluctuations in Indian residents’ final consumption are basically synchronised with fluctuations in economic growth, and that the driving effect of economic growth on consumption exists; next, the structural relation between the final consumption of government and of residents is stable and relatively consistent with the situation in a typical market economy country. Why, then, can the consumption rate of a developing country with a large population such as India also reach a level close to that of the developed countries? This dissertation holds that in the final analysis it is still to be attributed to the imbalance of economic development.

From the standpoint of consumption models, the consumption rates of the cash-flow type are all relatively high; evidently India’s consumption model is neither a wealthy cash-flow consumption model such as Sweden’s, nor a completely impoverished cash-flow consumption model, but a cash-flow consumption model in which a marked dual consumption structure exists, within which one part of those with a relatively high consumption rate belongs to a wealthy stratum such as the middle class, while another part of those with a relatively high consumption rate is the poverty stratum.

India’s economic growth is swift; by 2009 its per capita GDP had already exceeded 1,000 dollars, and a research report issued by the Asian Development Bank on the 19th showed that India’s middle class (with daily purchasing power of 2–20 dollars) had already grown to 420 million, among whom there were approximately 26 million of a wealthy stratum with daily purchasing power of 20 dollars or more. Beyond these data, still more attention must be paid to the severity of the dual structure of the Indian economy, which is manifested in almost every aspect. In India the coexistence of modernised cities and impoverished countryside is widespread. After independence, in order to change its state of economic backwardness, India long gave priority to developing industry in order to accumulate capital, and agriculture and the countryside gradually became appendages of industry and the cities. The proportion of agricultural capital declined gradually from the 1970s onwards, reaching only 5.8 per cent by 2000. On the other hand, modern industry and services led by the communications and computer software industries flourished in the cities, pulling the economy to grow at an average speed of 8 per cent a year. Precisely because of this state of economic development, government investment also tended towards the cities, where the rate of return is higher, and neglected the construction of agricultural infrastructure. The preference in government investment behaviour further widened the urban–rural gap and intensified India’s dual economic development. In social security, India to this day has no social security law in nationwide force; even in cities where social security exists, its scope of coverage is very limited, concentrated mainly in three aspects — work injury, welfare for women workers, and retirement security. There are only two sources of social security funds: borne by the government and borne by employers. Because there is no generally applicable legal constraint, the social security system is also dual in character: the rich do not need social security in order to live very well, while the poor can merely maintain their livelihood. Finally, India’s caste system severely restricts the intergenerational mobility of social resources, which is manifested in educational opportunities, employment opportunities and other aspects, and causes the greater part of India’s resources to be held in the hands of a high-caste population of less than 10 per cent. Institutional and cultural inequality causes India’s social dual structure to be marked, and has a serious influence on the further development of the economy.

It is precisely on the basis of the facts above that this dissertation holds that India’s consumption rate is higher than China’s not because India’s economic strength is higher than China’s, but because of the coexistence of dual consumption models. This consumption model, in which a wealthy cash-flow consumption model and an impoverished cash-flow consumption model coexist, cannot in a complete market economy system exercise a driving effect on the economy over the long term, and this model is precisely the one that China needs to avoid with all its strength.

7.1.4 The Consumption Model of Japan

Japan, as a developed capitalist country, already had a per capita GDP exceeding 40,000 dollars in 2009, but the level of saving of Japanese residents has nevertheless always been relatively high, and their consumption level is not as high as that of residents of other developed countries. This already roughly indicates that the consumption model of Japanese residents differs from those of other capitalist countries. The table below gives the structure of the shares of GDP of Japanese household final consumption and government final consumption.

From the figure above it can be seen that Japan’s government final consumption rate is comparable to America’s, but its household final consumption ratio is lower than America’s and comparable to Sweden’s. Japan has not developed a nationwide welfare and social security system of the Swedish kind; why, then, do Japanese residents not have as high a consumption rate as American residents? Looking at the mode of consumption, Japanese nationals are greatly influenced by Eastern culture, and at the same time, because domestic resources are scarce, Japanese residents are unwilling to consume excessively and unwilling to consume on credit; more of their income is used mainly to increase saving or to purchase financial assets rather than to consume. According to statistics of the Bank of Japan, Japanese nationals hold approximately 13 trillion dollars of assets, of which more than half is cash and saving and only 9 per cent is financial assets and liabilities. This datum presents a marked contrast with Americans, who own 36.5 trillion dollars of assets, of which cash and saving account for 13 per cent while 34 per cent is financial assets and liabilities. At the same time, Japanese people are accustomed to consuming in cash and are unwilling to use cheques or credit cards.

Looking at the characteristics of consumer goods, Japan has advocated frugality and energy saving since the 1950s; the average engine displacement of its private automobiles is only 1.2 litres, and the displacement of household cars is mostly below 2.0 litres, exactly the opposite of the United States, although even so nearly one third of residents rely on public rail transport for travel; the average capacity of Japanese households’ refrigerators is 440 litres, 40 litres less than in the United States. Looking at the structure of consumption expenditure, food expenditure accounts for about 22 per cent of total expenditure, and medical and health care expenditure for less than 4 per cent, which conforms to the standards of a developed country.

Looking at the construction of the social security system, the various kinds of security Japan provides to its nationals are also relatively complete, greatly reducing precautionary saving. In 1961 Japan realised “insurance for all nationals, and a pension system for all nationals”; this system relatively well regulated the income distribution among the various strata and basically realised both efficiency and equity. Japan’s security system is mainly divided into four parts, namely income security, medical security, social welfare and livelihood protection. These four parts provide differing degrees of security for unemployment, illness, disability, poverty and even the care of the children of dual-income couples. Although its scale of social security expenditure is not large — only 18.9 per cent — which is much less than Sweden’s, because it established a universal security system relatively late, and at the same time because of high-speed economic development, the number of people actually needing relief is relatively small, so a relatively low scale of expenditure nevertheless maintains the operation of the universal security system very well. In social security expenditure, items are tilted mainly towards old-age provision and medical care, these two together accounting for 88 per cent of total expenditure. It is precisely because of these characteristics of Japan’s social security system that the consumption model of Japanese nationals has gradually been shaped.

From these facts it can be seen that Japanese residents, under resource constraints, have gradually formed a frugal consumption model, manifested mainly in avoiding consumption on credit as far as possible and practising strict frugality to increase saving. Although this consumption model has to a certain degree also led to insufficient domestic demand in Japan, it cannot be denied that against a background of gradually scarce resources, this consumption model should be one of the goals of the transformation of China’s consumption model.

7.1.5 Lessons from the Different Consumption Models for China

The formation and development of the several typical consumption models above have both commonalities and individualities: they have a favourable side and also an unfavourable side.

First, the formation of a consumption model cannot be established through the promulgation of a single law, important though law is. The establishment of a consumption model is based first of all upon natural resources, economic strength, the degree of social development, and population structure. America’s economic strength is formidable, its market system very maturely developed, its resources abundant and its population at the same time relatively sparse, so the United States has the foundation to develop a high-level social security system for all its people. By contrast, India has a large population and is the developing country second only to China in the world; although its speed of economic growth is very fast, the duality of its economy as a whole determines that its consumption model too must display duality. Sweden and Japan are likewise economically developed, and consumers command a great deal of economic resources. But because the two countries differ greatly in natural resources and culture, and their strategies of economic development differ, Sweden’s model of high welfare and high consumption for all cannot be realised in Japan, and Japan’s frugal consumption model will not work in Sweden either.

Second, the most important aspect of the development and cultivation of a consumption model is not to change the consumption habits of the nationals, but, on the basis of respecting the nationals’ consumption habits, to bring their favourable side into play while avoiding their unfavourable side as far as possible. American consumers, for example, are already accustomed to consumption on credit; in the minds of American consumers, liabilities are not only not a burden but are a symbol of credit. The positive significance of this fact is that consumption can indeed become the most important driving force pulling American economic growth, while its negative significance is that the risks contained in consumption on credit may erupt along with economic fluctuations, of which the subprime crisis of 2008 is the best proof. Swedish nationals have certainly grown accustomed to a life of high taxation, high welfare and at the same time high consumption; for the individual, such a living and consumption environment is necessarily comfortable, but high welfare certainly causes low efficiency in the social economy and a heavy financial burden on the government, to the point that the “Swedish disease” appears. The Swedish government is therefore also gradually improving this system, introducing efficiency mechanisms alongside security. Japanese consumers uphold a consumption model of frugality and reducing liabilities, which is the safest for the individual, but from the standpoint of economic growth, Japan’s prolonged sluggishness of consumption has meant that consumption has never been able to become a new point of economic growth, of which Japan’s prolonged economic weakness after the bursting of the real estate bubble is proof. In practice, Japan’s saving interest rate has long been zero or even negative, but the saving assets of Japanese consumers have not decreased, a point in which it resembles China. This shows that for ordinary Japanese consumers the decisions of saving and consumption are not closely related to the interest rate, and that their consumption rate, stable at about 50 per cent, is the positive result of a stable social security system. India resembles China in many aspects: a large population, a low level of economic development, a marked dual structure, a low level of social security, and so on. The relatively high consumption rate produced by the dual cash-flow consumption model existing in India therefore contains injustice and hence instability. The factors forming India’s unique consumption model include not only the caste system, but also the sacrifice of equity, and the sacrifice of the interests of the vast countryside and of farmers, for the sake of pursuing the speed of economic development. It is precisely this imbalance that has shaped India’s dual consumption structure.

China’s specific reality also differs from that of the countries above. In consumption habits, ordinary Chinese consumers are likewise accustomed to saving and do not tend towards consumption on credit, which is why consumer lending business in China has never been able to flourish as it has in the United States. For a Chinese household, housing is not an ordinary commodity, nor is it merely an asset in the economic sense; it is the starting point at which a household settles down and establishes its life, so Chinese consumers’ demand for housing is rigid, and they will not be satisfied with “having a place to live” but must “have housing of their own”; to reach this goal, Chinese people who are unwilling to incur liabilities even change their long-standing consumption habits and incur liabilities — but that Chinese consumers are willing to incur liabilities for housing does not mean they are willing to incur liabilities for other ordinary commodities. At the macroeconomic level, only thirty-three years have passed since China moved from a planned economy towards a market economy; China’s social security system has already emerged from the low level and differential treatment of the past but has not yet determined its future direction — though it can be affirmed that neither the Swedish model nor the Indian model is suited to China, the former requiring too high a level of economic development to be reached, and the latter being unsuited because of the instability it contains.

Combining all of the factors above, therefore, China should follow a path similar to Japan’s: respecting the nationals’ consumption habits, raising the level of social security on the basis of gradual economic development, expanding the coverage of social security, and raising the remuneration of middle- and low-income labourers, with particular care to avoid urban and rural consumers facing injustice within the social security system. In the process of cultivating a consumption model suited to China, the role of the government is beyond doubt, not only because of the authority it possesses in enacting laws and formulating policies, but also because the government has the capacity to use financial means to redistribute the incomes of labourers of different strata. In addition, the future strategy of economic development formulated by the government has an important guiding significance for enterprises, and the behaviour of enterprises and their products have an important significance for leading and opening up new fields of consumption and cultivating new hot spots of consumption.

7.2 Income Distribution Policy and the Transformation of the Consumption Model

7.2.1 The Two Episodes of Income Distribution in the United States

Income is the foundation of consumption, so the sources of income — whether current income or future income, whether explicit wage income or the implicit income of transfer payments — all have a marked influence on the construction and transformation of consumption models. In the macroeconomic sense, income distribution can be broadly divided into two classes: one is primary income distribution, of wages, profits and taxes; the second is the use of government taxation. The first income distribution is current explicit income, while the second distribution is, for consumers, a redistribution that is implicit and deferred in character. From the nature of the two distributions it can be seen that the primary income distribution mainly pursues efficiency, while the second income distribution pursues equity.

The most marked characteristic of the American consumption model is excessive consumption, and the formation of this model is closely related to the present state of America’s economic development. First, America’s political foundation is stable and its economic policy practises firm marketization and liberalisation; under such conditions of stable expectations, American consumers, enterprises and government are all able to make rational decisions adapted to the present political and economic environment. Second, against this stable background, the long-term profit indicators of American enterprises display a trend of decline year by year as the organic composition of capital rises year by year. This trend shows that although productive capacity has risen markedly, the income enterprises obtain directly through production is decreasing; at the same time, because the quantity of labour used gradually decreases as the organic composition of capital [rises], the share of labourers’ income in the primary distribution is also falling. The figure below gives the movement of the profit rates of five major non-financial core industries in the United States — durable goods manufacturing, non-durable goods manufacturing, retail, wholesale and services — from which it can be seen that the declining trend is stable and marked. At the same time, the profits of financial services have been rising year by year.

The trend of long-term decline in the profits of American manufacturing enterprises is representative; it reflects the inner law of socialised large-scale production in a market economy, a law that is also embodied in capitalist countries such as Japan and Sweden. Under the domination of this law, therefore, enterprises and labourers will gradually tend towards a weak position in the primary distribution. At this point the government’s tax regulation policy becomes an important means of changing labourers’ income and thereby shaping the consumption model. From the Kennedy period, the United States began an unprecedented plan of tax reduction, cutting personal income tax nationwide, reducing the highest marginal rate from 91 per cent to 70 per cent, at the same time cutting the income tax on enterprise profits and relaxing the policy of tax deduction for the depreciation of enterprise equipment. Tax policies of this kind increased the income of ordinary labourers somewhat while the tax burden of the high-income stratum rose correspondingly. By the time of the Reagan government, after he came to power he continued to practise a policy of tax reduction, lowering rates while broadening the tax base: the tax burden of the low-income population, 50 per cent of the population, fell from about 8 per cent to about 5 per cent, while the tax burden of the high-income population, about 1 per cent of the population, rose from about 20 per cent to about 25 per cent. In the Clinton period, similar tax plans were still being practised, their main content still being to increase taxes on the high-income stratum and enterprises and reduce taxes on the low-income population. Over such a long period, because of the redistributive effect of government tax policy, the United States maintained relatively high and relatively stable growth, and its consumption rate also remained at a relatively high level. By the period of George W. Bush, however, his tax policy turned to protecting the high-income stratum, which inevitably widened the income gap once again and laid a hidden danger beneath the subprime financial crisis that followed.

America’s primary income distribution determined the objective fact of the decrease in consumers’ income, and thereby determined that, in order to guarantee that the products produced could be sold, new financial instruments had to be created as instruments of payment; consumption on credit then appeared reasonably and flourished. In order to allow this consumption model to operate smoothly, America’s income distribution policy and consumer behaviour alike were aimed almost entirely at maintaining the model of consumption on credit. The greatest shortcoming of this consumption model is that when difficulties of payment appear the risk of bankruptcy is high, and once consumers go bankrupt it provokes a chain reaction, as in the financial crisis of 2007. This kind of income distribution policy, especially redistribution policy and tax policy, is therefore not a path for shaping a healthy consumption model.

7.2.2 China Should Develop an Income Distribution That Stresses Equity while Pursuing Efficiency

Since China entered the process of marketization, the productive capacity and efficiency of its enterprises have been raised by a wide margin compared with the planned economy era, and it is precisely this that has guaranteed the rapid development of China’s economic aggregate. However, the inner law of the development of a market economy is that as the organic composition of capital rises, enterprises use less and less labour capital and more and more financial capital and industrial capital; this law is also embodied in China. In the earlier parts of this dissertation there is a detailed data analysis of the changes in the proportions among enterprise profits, labourers’ wages and government fiscal revenue, whose trend likewise shows that enterprise profits are relatively stable with a slight rise, that labourers’ income has fallen markedly, and that the increase in government fiscal revenue is the most obvious. This shows that China’s primary distribution has not inclined towards labourers, which is the most essential cause of China’s insufficient consumption demand. At the same time, China’s tax policy, especially the tax policy aimed at ordinary consumers, has not changed along with economic development and changes in the income gap. At present China exempts personal wages and salaries below 2,000 yuan from tax; above 2,000 yuan they begin to be taxed according to a nine-grade progressive rate, and the lowest tax rate on remuneration for services is 20 per cent. Although the exemption threshold for wage and salary income has been adjusted several times and was raised to 2,000 yuan on 1 March 2008, relative to the magnitude of the rise in incomes and the income gap, this standard is still insufficient for shaping a healthy balance-sheet consumption model suited to China. In order to reach this goal, China’s income distribution policy should set to work from the following aspects.

First, we should firmly strengthen our confidence in following the road of the socialist market economy, and carry out deeper reform of the non-marketized parts. Among these, the positive effect of the efficiency factor in increasing the social economic aggregate should still first be affirmed, so that in primary distribution the development and growth of enterprises, especially private enterprises, should be encouraged. China’s private enterprises are distributed mainly in the tertiary sector, so encouraging their development can effectively absorb labour, broaden the channels of employment, and at the same time continue to bring into play China’s comparative advantage of abundant labour. This can directly increase labourers’ income and government taxation, providing a foundation for increased consumption by consumers while also providing a firmer foundation for the government’s transfer payments. On the other hand, firmly following the path of marketized development can provide a stable policy background for the macroeconomy, which will play an important role in stabilising the expectations of enterprises and consumers, and thereby also play a key role in the stability of the consumption model. At present, one of the important purposes for which Chinese consumers raise saving and purchase housing assets is to guard against uncertainties that may occur in the future, and this uncertainty arises to a great extent from changes in economic policy. The government’s reaffirmation and emphasis of the marketization road is therefore of strategic significance for the stable development of the economy.

Second, in the primary distribution the government should reduce taxation, especially taxation on ordinary consumers, and in the present circumstances of gradually marked polarisation between rich and poor, reduce the tax burden of the low-income stratum. Logically, the consumption rate of low-income consumers is certainly higher than that of high-income earners, because the expenditure needed to satisfy the necessities of daily life is basically similar; even if the content of high-income earners’ consumption is richer and more expensive than that of low-income earners, its share relative to income is lower. So if attention is paid only to the indicator of the consumption rate, the consumption rate of low-income households, under a consumption model similar to the cash-flow type, is certainly relatively high — but this is not a sustainable, healthy consumption model. This dissertation therefore holds that the government should reduce the taxation of the ordinary wage-earning stratum in the primary distribution and institutionalise this, raising their disposable income and raising the threshold at which wage income begins to be taxed. Such an adjustment of tax rates not only accords with the facts of China’s economic development, but also embodies the equity of income distribution policy.

Third, practise “storing wealth among the people”. From the data above it can be seen that the Chinese government’s financial strength is formidable, its capacity both for investment and for consumption being very strong, but the capacity of Chinese consumers for investment and consumption has been feeble throughout. This shows that in the process of China’s great economic development, the government occupies an absolutely advantageous position, which is an important cause of the decline in residents’ capacity to consume. The government should therefore practise storing wealth among the people, and encourage residents’ consumption and private investment. In formulating fiscal policy, more financial resources should be used in the production of public goods and in promoting social justice and equity, such as investment in education in backward regions, the construction of low-rent housing, and the appreciation of pension funds. The production of these public goods is not only the government’s responsibility; its positive effects will also continue to influence and change consumers’ consumption habits, and will ultimately have a positive effect in forming a healthy consumption model.

7.3 Social Security Policy and the Transformation of the Consumption Model

7.3.1 A Comparison of Several Models of Social Security

The typical consumption models of the United States, Sweden, Japan and India were analysed above; among them the consumption rates of the United States and Sweden are relatively high, while those of Japan and India are somewhat lower. That the consumption models of these countries each differ is in part due to the important role played by the differences in their social security systems.

Sweden is a typical Nordic high-welfare country, and the characteristics of its social security model are “high taxation, universal coverage, high security”. It is precisely because Sweden’s social security coverage is very broad and its degree of security very high that this has become the foundation on which Swedish consumers dare to consume. However, this high-welfare model needs to rely on relatively high tax revenue, and at the same time the raising of the level of security sets relatively high requirements for the government’s financial strength and for the implementation of transfer payment behaviour.

The United States is a completely marketized country, and its social security model likewise upholds the principle of “shared burdens”. Although the American model does not have as high a degree of security as the Swedish model, considering its large population its coverage and level of security are still relatively high. This depends not only on America’s exceedingly strong level of economic development; its stable policy environment is also the great background constituting American consumers’ daring to consume on credit.

India has a large population and is also a typical developing country; its social security model resembles the present state of its economic development, that is, it displays a very marked “dual structure”. That is, the social security system exists only in the large cities, with little or no coverage of the countryside; at the same time social security also exists only for the few rich and the middle-class stratum, and basically not at all for the low-income stratum that most needs security. This dual-structured social security system coexists with the duality of India’s economic development. In India, therefore, the consumption model of the low-income stratum belongs to the impoverished cash-flow type, while the middle-class stratum that has security is of the balance-sheet type, and for the wealthiest stratum it is manifested as the wealthy cash-flow type. India’s consumption rate is therefore also relatively high merely judging from the data, the reason being that a considerable number of consumers still adopt the cash-flow consumption model, with its relatively high consumption rate, whether of the impoverished or the wealthy type. This is precisely what China’s consumption model and social security model need to avoid with all their strength.

Japan’s economic strength developed swiftly after the war, and its economic aggregate is at present firmly in third place in the world. However, because Japanese nationals’ understanding of their own country’s lack of resources is very clear, their consumption behaviour does not blindly pursue luxury, but selects what suits their own needs at the corresponding level of income. From the aspect of the social security system, Japan too has developed a relatively complete social security system, embodied in illness, unemployment, old-age provision and even the care of the children of dual-income couples. From the aspect of the collection of security expenses, Japan’s enterprise pension system effectively makes up the gap in social security. Under the joint action of these two forces, Japanese nationals seldom use consumption on credit, and when they do use it, it is mainly limited to small temporary payments. Although the consumption rate of this frugal consumption model is relatively low, considering resources and population numbers, and considering environmental problems, this consumption model has already displayed its superiority.

Considering the typical social security models of these countries, India’s dual social security model already does not accord with the requirements of China’s social development, and the Swedish model is more suited to adoption in countries with small populations and a very high level of economic development; the American model is easier to operate but needs a marketized economic environment and a relatively democratic and transparent political system as its guarantee; the Japanese model, relatively speaking, possesses resource foresight, and is more feasible especially for a large country such as China with a low per capita possession of resources.

7.3.2 Building a Sustainable Model of Social Security to Promote the Transformation of the Consumption Model

The original meaning of “security” is to guarantee that citizens living in a country have the basic rights of survival, of livelihood and of development, of which food, clothing, housing, travel, unemployment security, medical care, old-age provision and education are the basic elements. In the planned economy era, China’s level of economic development was not high; in particular, under conditions of no private property rights, urban consumers, apart from the basic means of subsistence, had all other security provided by the state, while all the security of rural consumers came from the land. As China’s process of marketization has accelerated and its economic strength has risen, the level of security for China’s labourers has also risen, and its coverage has gradually enlarged. Today, whether in the cities or the countryside, the most basic security for survival can be satisfied, and so security for the rights of livelihood and development has gradually become the focus, of which housing, old-age provision and medical care are the core. The security problems in these three aspects have appeared together with China’s urbanisation, the ageing of its population and the disappearance of the demographic dividend. It is precisely because China’s security in these aspects concerning future survival and livelihood is insufficient that this has profoundly influenced consumers’ expectations of the future, causing them to revise their consumption path unceasingly, with the result that consumption demand has declined continually. A great deal of relevant research shows that the greater part of Chinese consumers’ saving is in order to guard against future risks in housing, old-age provision and illness. It can reasonably be inferred that if this part of saving were released under conditions of good social security, China’s household consumption rate would rise markedly. The social security system has the character of a public good; constructing a good social security system requires not only payments by enterprises and consumers, but also large inputs by the government — this is the government’s responsibility — yet China’s reality is that the government, in order to maintain high growth, has neglected its input into social security! In order to maintain healthy and sustainable economic growth and development, therefore, the government must bring the construction of a complete social security system into its planning.

In housing security, the characteristic of Chinese consumers is that they hope to “have housing of their own” rather than merely “have a place to live”, so it is only by conforming to this consumption habit that a basis for constructing a consumption model suited to China can be formed. China’s housing system has already passed from the past system of provision by the work unit under public ownership to market provision with seventy years of private property rights retained, so that the confirmation of rights over the original publicly owned housing, together with urbanisation’s demand for housing, has pushed housing prices up enormously. Especially under conditions of unbalanced regional development and of local governments relying on land to increase revenue, housing prices have already far exceeded the payment capacity of ordinary consumers. In housing security the government should therefore lower the price of commercial housing by lowering the cost of land, so that housing prices return to a rational level; in addition, the government should increase the construction of security housing, guaranteeing the residential needs of low-income consumers.

As the trend of ageing in China’s population develops further, old-age and medical security have already become prominent social problems. These problems contain uncertainty within them and relate to consumers’ survival and livelihood in the stage of the future when they have no income, so a large part of Chinese consumers’ saving is also used to resist insufficiency of pensions and the rise of medical costs. If the government applied its fiscal revenue to the construction of pension funds and the medical system, and expanded the coverage of old-age provision and of medical security, consumers’ precautionary saving would decrease substantially, and a part of it would be transformed into consumption.

China is a developing country with a large population and a low per capita possession of resources; in this sense China resembles Japan. In terms of level of development, therefore, China cannot establish a social security system at as high a level as Sweden’s, and at the same time should avoid a dual model of the Indian kind; combining the marketized model of the United States with the Japanese model is therefore the direction of development of China’s social security model. On this foundation, China’s consumption model will also gradually be transformed into America’s balance-sheet model or Japan’s frugal model. These two consumption models are closely related to the social security model, so, in order to let the fruits of economic development serve consumers, and in order to let consumption become the most important driving force pushing economic growth, constructing a consumption model favourable to the sustainable development of consumption and promoting the transformation of China’s present cash consumption model is a major measure of strategic significance.

7.4 Industrial Policy and the Transformation of the Consumption Model

The significance of industrial policy for the transformation of the consumption model is embodied first in absorbing employment and raising labourers’ income. The manifestation of socioeconomic development in the structure of the three industries is this: as the economy becomes more and more developed, agriculture absorbs less and less labour while industry and the tertiary sector become the principal arenas of employment, the tertiary sector especially being the most important industry for absorbing labour — this is the process of industrial upgrading. China’s actual situation is that labour is abundant; as the secondary sector develops, the organic composition of capital gradually rises, which directly reduces employment in the secondary sector, thereby directly reducing labourers’ income and increasing the risk of income fluctuation. In order to reduce the influence of these factors on income and thereby on consumption, policies favourable to the development of the tertiary sector should therefore be formulated. At the same time, the secondary sector should also be encouraged to carry out upgrading transformation, for example passing from producing traditional products to producing products of new energy and new materials. An innovative industrial upgrading policy of this kind will have a positive effect in creating employment and raising labourers’ income, and will thereby have a positive effect in forming a more rational consumption model.

It deserves attention that on 18 October 2010 the State Council already issued the Decision on Accelerating the Cultivation and Development of Strategic Emerging Industries, making clear that a package of policies will be introduced from the aspects of finance, taxation and banking to accelerate the cultivation and development of strategic emerging industries. By 2015, the added value of strategic emerging industries as a share of GDP is to strive to reach about 8 per cent. According to the characteristics of strategic emerging industries, and based on China’s national conditions and its technological and industrial foundations, at the present stage the emphasis will be on cultivating and developing industries such as energy conservation and environmental protection, new-generation information technology, biology, high-end equipment manufacturing, new energy, new materials, and new-energy vehicles. The Decision also pointed out that, in order to coordinate with this strategic plan, the state will also perfect the financial and tax support system, encourage private capital to enter the relevant industries, and at the same time further perfect the growth enterprise market, promote the establishment of over-the-counter securities trading markets, and satisfy the capital needs of enterprises at different stages of development. It will vigorously develop the bond market, expand the scale of issuance of collective bonds and collective notes of small and medium-sized enterprises, actively explore the development of financial products such as low-credit-rating high-yield bonds and private convertible bonds, steadily promote the development of enterprise bonds, corporate bonds, short-term financing bills and medium-term notes, and broaden the channels of enterprise debt financing. In finance and taxation, too, there will be inclinations and preferences towards these strategic emerging industries, encouraging innovation and guiding investment and consumption.

The comprehensive launching of the development plans of the seven strategic emerging industries in 2011 will bring the commencement of a large batch of industrial upgrading investment projects, which will further optimise the industrial structure. The central government’s new disposition for the coordinated development of regional economies is an important factor supporting economic growth. This will drive regional economies to realise growth and promote the optimisation of regional economic structures. The implementation of the Twelfth Five-Year Plan and the launching of income distribution reform will give residents’ incomes a relatively fast growth with an institutional guarantee, and at the same time the government’s continued increase of effort in the people’s livelihood, and its strengthening of input into social security, will fundamentally further strengthen residents’ capacity to consume. Finally, the development of the new industries will drive an increase in employment, thereby increasing residents’ income and ultimately promoting the transformation of the mode of economic growth. The improvement of income distribution will push the consumption of urban and rural residents to become further active, and the consumer goods market can be expected to continue to maintain a flourishing situation.

On the other hand, industrial policy should also incline towards non-state and private enterprises, relaxing the regulation and high thresholds on the entry of private capital into fields, so as to satisfy consumers’ requirements from the aspects of increasing supply and lowering prices, and to shape a new consumption model. China’s private capital is concentrated mainly in the tertiary sector, the industries it engages in being mainly service industries and small-scale manufacturing. The characteristics of these industries are that the capital required is relatively small and the technical requirements are not high, but they can create relatively much employment — and employment, and thereby income, is the foundation of consumption — so encouraging the development of private enterprises can fundamentally change labourers’ consumption constraints and expectations. In addition, the constraints of competition between private capital and state capital must be relaxed; through market competition the cost of products must be lowered, breaking the monopoly of state capital and state-owned enterprises in certain important industries. Only such competition can lower product costs and prices, raising consumer surplus from the angle of increasing supply and thereby attaining the purpose of raising consumer utility.

In short, a country’s economic foundation and economic policies determine that country’s economic performance and economic face. The problem of the low consumption rate existing in China’s household consumption sector is of course not isolated either, and is by no means related only to the characteristic of Chinese consumers’ preference for saving; more importantly, it is closely related to employment, income, household structure, the distribution of national income, the mode of economic growth, and the like. This dissertation holds that China’s present low consumption rate is precisely determined by these constraints in the non-consumption spheres, so that changing the low consumption rate and thereby promoting the sustained and healthy development of China’s economy cannot seek methods within the sphere of consumption alone; the conditions constraining residents’ consumption must be further relaxed, so as thoroughly to change the consumption model and raise the capacity of consumption to pull the economy.

From a broader perspective, the transformation of the consumption model is a characteristic fact of the Chinese-characteristic economy’s further advance towards a market economy. When consumers possess complete private property rights and at the same time dare to consume on their own initiative on credit, it means that they have positive and stable expectations of the economic environment in which they find themselves. On this foundation, enterprises too can provide output more efficiently and stably. It is hard to imagine that such an economy could not develop healthily and sustainably! On the basis of private property rights and the completely free flow of factors, the market can regulate the operation of the economy full of efficiency. Consumers labour and consume, enterprises produce and profit, and the government does well its work as “night watchman” — this is the end point of China’s process of marketization.

Swedish figures (§7.1.2) — the 1979 central-government marginal personal tax rate of 87 per cent; social security expenditure of 40.7 per cent of GDP in 1987 (Britain 25.5 per cent, the United States 16.2 per cent); and 1983 per capita social security expenditure of 3,649 dollars against an OECD average of 1,929 dollars — are reproduced exactly as written, as the author’s 2011 figures.
  7. India, Asian Development Bank report (§7.1.3). The original dates the report only to “the 19th” (19日）, with no month or year; translated as written. The middle-class figures (420 million people with daily purchasing power of 2–20 dollars; about 26 million at 20 dollars or more) are reproduced as written.
  8. Japan, exhibit numbering (§7.1.4). The text introduces the exhibit “下表” (“the table below”) as a table, but its caption is “图7-7” (Figure 7-7); it is treated as a figure here, following the caption and the consistent figure sequence 7-5, 7-6, 7-7, 7-8.
  9. Japanese asset figures (§7.1.4). “Only 9 per cent is financial assets and liabilities” (and the parallel American figure of 34 per cent) reproduces the original’s 金融资产和债务； the category as named combines assets and liabilities and is flagged as unclear. The Japanese refrigerator figure renders the original’s “统计率平均440升” as “average capacity of 440 litres”; the original phrase appears garbled （统计率 has no clear meaning here) and the rendering follows the evident sense. Food expenditure “月22%” is read as 约 (“about 22 per cent”).
  10. Japan’s social security expenditure of “only 18.9 per cent” (§7.1.4) is given in the original without a stated base (presumably a share of GDP or of national income); translated as written and flagged.
  11. India’s middle class classified differently in §7.3.1. In §7.1.3 India’s dual model is described as the coexistence of wealthy and impoverished cash-flow models, the middle class being placed on the wealthy cash-flow side; in §7.3.1 the middle-class stratum “that has security” is described as “of the balance-sheet type”. Both statements are translated as written; the difference in classification is flagged for the author.
  12. United States tax history (§7.2.1). Several statements are reproduced as written although they sit uneasily with the standard record: the Kennedy-era cuts are said to have increased the tax burden of the high-income stratum “correspondingly”, and under Reagan the burden of the top 1 per cent is said to have risen from about 20 per cent to about 25 per cent while rates were being lowered. The sentence on the organic composition of capital also repeats “逐渐减少” in a place where the sense requires “rises” (“because the quantity of labour used gradually decreases as the organic composition of capital [rises]”); the bracketed word restores the argument’s evident meaning. These are the author’s statements and have not been corrected beyond that bracket.
  13. China’s personal income tax (§7.2.2). The 2,000-yuan monthly exemption threshold (effective 1 March 2008), the nine-grade progressive scale on wages and salaries, and the 20 per cent minimum rate on remuneration for services are stated as of the dissertation’s writing in 2011 and are reproduced as written; the threshold was raised again later in 2011, after the dissertation was submitted.
  14. Japan’s world ranking (§7.3.1). Japan’s economic aggregate is described as “at present firmly in third place in the world”, consistent with China’s having overtaken Japan in 2010 as noted in Chapter 1; retained as written.
  15. Shortened model names. Where the original uses the short forms 现金消费模式 (§7.3.2) and the like, they are translated literally (“cash consumption model”), preserving the original’s variation, as in Package 1; all refer to the glossary concept cash-flow consumption model.
  16. Terms coined or applied beyond the glossary. “Consumption rate” = 消费率 (final consumption/GDP, as defined in Chapter 1); “supremacy of consumption” for 消费至上； “frugal consumption” for 节约型消费； “dual structure / dual consumption structure” for 二元结构/二元消费结构； “organic composition of capital” for 资本有机构成； “storing wealth among the people” for 藏富于民； “demographic dividend” for 人口红利； “security housing” for 保障性住房； “collective bonds and collective notes” for 集合债券和集合票据； “growth enterprise market” for 创业板 (the ChiNext board); “night watchman” for 守夜人 (the government’s role, in the original’s quotation marks). “CEInet Statistics Database” renders 中经网统计数据库 (China Economic Information Network). Yuan Guangliang’s 《美国的社保体系》 is cited as “The Social Security System of the United States”.
  17. The State Council Decision (§7.4). The Decision on Accelerating the Cultivation and Development of Strategic Emerging Industries (18 October 2010), its target of about 8 per cent of GDP by 2015, and the list of seven industries are reproduced as written in the dissertation, which quotes and paraphrases the Decision at length.
