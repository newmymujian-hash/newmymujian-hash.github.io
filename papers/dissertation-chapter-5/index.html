Chapter 5 The Foundations and Paths of the Transformation of the Consumption Model

The profound changes produced by the acceleration and deepening of China’s construction of a market economy are manifested not only in the various economic indicators, but have gradually also come to be manifested in the behaviour of the government, of enterprises, and of consumers. At the same time, because the process of transition of China’s economic system has been relatively rapid, various forms of maladaptation have accompanied the marked performance in economic efficiency. On the government side, the shift from the past comprehensive management of the economy to the present position, in which the economy can be regulated only through fiscal and monetary policy and a part of administrative power, has left the government, when macroeconomic regulation is needed, with scant means and insufficient experience; in a crisis, a government lacking experience of market management may therefore make wrong decisions, and thereby, in the process of the market as a multi-player game, exert a negative influence on economic efficiency and equity alike. On the enterprise side, marketization has brought enterprises freedom, but has at the same time imposed on their survival the hard constraint of profit. Enterprises, as economic units whose ultimate goal is the pursuit and realisation of efficiency, no longer bear, as the “work units” of the past did, an excessive obligation of “equity”, and this has made the mode of enterprise management approach more closely that of the “economic man”. The disappearance of the various implicit welfare benefits formerly paid by the “work unit” has accordingly had a very great influence on workers’ incomes, and bears on the raising of workers’ utility and welfare; this missing part of welfare is the greatest crux of China’s present market economy reform. Finally, the employment environment, the mode of income, the social security system, and so on faced by all consumers have likewise undergone substantive change, and these changes in the constraint conditions have caused consumption behaviour to change. None of these changes is isolated; they are profoundly connected with one another and possess corresponding logical links. To discuss the transformation of the consumption model, one must therefore discuss every aspect connected with the consumer and its logical links, including the distribution of labour income, the perfection of capital and financial markets, the perfection of the social security system, and the present-day problem of household housing.

This dissertation holds that the consumption model is precisely the result of the combined action of these constraint conditions, and that to discuss the transformation of the consumption model one should therefore focus on analysing the changes in these constraint conditions. From the planned economy to the primary stage of the market economy, the constraints on consumption behaviour changed a great deal; but, as argued above, the consumption model of Chinese residents has not undergone a fundamental change. If the household consumption rate is used as the indicator measuring the size of consumption’s contribution to the economy, the consumption rate was indeed very high in the early stage of reform and opening up, but as reform deepened this indicator declined year by year. The result of the development of the market economy — that household consumption has decreased and utility has risen only slowly — manifestly runs counter to the goal of reform, so that it is necessary to analyse what exactly the influence of the changes in China’s macroeconomic environment over the last ten-odd years on consumers has been, how great it has been, and how, in the reforms to come, the constraint conditions can be relaxed so as to raise and develop consumption and domestic demand. How can the cash consumption model suited to a low level of economy be transformed into the balance-sheet consumption model suited to a high level of economic development?

5.1 Raising Labour Income Is the Foundation of the Transformation of the Consumption Model

It cannot be denied that income is the basis of consumption: even in the case of borrowing or credit consumption, income is the primary condition. Resolving the consumption problem should therefore rationally begin from analysing the aggregate and structure of income and adjusting income distribution. In the planned economy era, workers’ income took a single form and its aggregate was small; but considering that the cost of living at that time was very low and that housing and social security were all borne in full by the state, the cash-flow type of consumption model was completely able to suit the macroeconomic environment of the time. As the marketization process has deepened, the changes workers face have become diverse, but their incomes appear less fixed and less stable than in the planned economy period; the cost of living has risen markedly, and many implicit costs of the planned economy era have now become explicit costs — education, medical care, and old-age provision, for example — and the changes in these costs bearing on everyday life have a very marked influence on consumer behaviour; yet the government’s primary distribution, which guarantees efficiency, and its redistribution of income, which guarantees equity, did not take into account the risks workers face, and this ultimately became an important reason for residents’ high saving and low consumption. Under such a situation, from which aspects can one proceed so as to resolve the problems of the link of workers’ income more rationally and effectively, and thereby promote the transformation of the consumption model? This dissertation holds that growth of the economic aggregate remains the primary link; second, that greater adjustments should be made in the link of the distribution of national income, which should be biased more towards labour rather than capital; and third, that guaranteeing and raising the employment rate is the key to raising workers’ incomes.

5.1.1 The Facts and Prospects of Sustained Growth of the Economic Aggregate

Sustained growth of the economic aggregate is the precondition for the incomes of all economic sectors to rise. Since 1978, China’s productive forces have been liberated along the track of developing a market economy, and the direct effect has been that the growth rate of gross domestic product has remained at a high level, growing at an annual average of 9.8 per cent, without taking inflation into account.

Figure 5-1: The growth rate of GDP and the growth rate of average wages

[Figure image: image94.wmf] Line chart, 1978–2009: the GDP growth rate moves comparatively smoothly, between about 4 and 15 per cent, while the growth rate of the average wage of staff and workers in post fluctuates much more widely, peaking at about 35 per cent in 1994 and falling close to zero in 2009.

At the same time, the wage incomes of staff and workers in post have also grown with fluctuations; their trend is similar to the fluctuation of GDP growth, but in general the increase is higher than the GDP growth rate, reaching an annual average of more than 13 per cent without taking inflation into account. If calculated by aggregate, without considering price factors, in 2008 the average wage of staff and workers in post reached 29,229 yuan, a growth of more than 47 times over the 615 yuan of 1978. It was precisely the substantial rise in wage income that became an important reason why the share of the consumption rate rose over a fairly long period, and also a reason for the increase in fiscal revenue and enterprise profits.

[Figure image: image95.wmf] Line chart, 1978–2009: the contribution rate of final consumption expenditure (per cent) is high and volatile in the late 1970s and 1980s, peaking at about 95 per cent in 1981, and trends downward thereafter, standing at roughly 40–55 per cent in the 2000s.

Comparing the fluctuations in the two figures above, one can discover that 1984 and 1993 were respectively years of relatively high wage growth rates, that 1990 was the low point of the growth rate, and that after 1997 wage growth was relatively even. At the same time, the contribution rate of final consumption also corresponds respectively to a relative high point in 1984 and 1993, while 1990 corresponds to the low point of the consumption rate; and likewise after 1997 this figure also fell back from its high point and remained at a stable ratio. Although, comparing international data, China’s consumption rate is relatively low, the positive correlation between income and the consumption rate is nevertheless clear, and the positive correlation between the GDP growth rate and wage income, and ultimately residents’ disposable income, is likewise clear.

It is precisely because such a relationship exists that continuing to maintain growth of the economic aggregate is the ultimate foundation and primary condition for raising the consumption rate; abandoning growth and talking only of distribution will not attain the ultimate purpose of raising consumption. China’s economic growth should also change the mercantilist dogma of the past, passing gradually from an emphasis only on exports and investment to an emphasis on domestic demand, and from extensive growth to a growth model of intensive and circular economy; this change is the guarantee of China’s economy continuing to grow in the future, and can thereby also construct the foundation for consumption to be sustained, stable, and even to grow.

The standpoint of the choice, in the early stage of marketization, to develop an export trade model was precisely China’s relatively cheap labour costs and relatively scarce capital; labour-intensive industries therefore opened for China the gate into the world economy, and the products manufactured by limited capital together with relatively abundant labour exchanged for a large quantity of foreign exchange for China and produced a high economic growth rate for many consecutive years. As domestic economic strength has gradually increased and the world economic environment has changed, this mode of development of China’s, which simply relies on a cost advantage and takes the external market as its focus, has come under constraint from many sides. First, the standard of living of domestic workers has risen steadily along with economic development, and enterprises’ employment costs have accordingly gradually risen; in particular, after the so-called “population dividend” disappears, the foundation for the existence of the advantage of cheap labour costs will have vanished entirely. Second, the time has come when the inestimable environmental damage that high-energy-consumption and high-pollution industries have brought to a China whose natural environment is in itself very fragile can no longer not be faced and resolved; upgrading of the industrial structure is one of the ways of resolving these two problems — on the one hand reducing the use of resources while raising the efficiency of resource utilisation, and on the other hand the effect of the advancement of industry in raising workers’ incomes is also marked. Third, China’s population is numerous, which for economic development means an enormous market. That international enterprises have poured into China in succession while Chinese enterprises have always kept their eyes on exports is manifestly illogical; at the same time, Chinese enterprises’ deficiency in manufacturing level and innovative capacity affects their ability to develop the domestic demand market, and export products still possess an enormous profit space; the combination of these several forces has caused Chinese enterprises not to attach importance to the domestic demand market.

As China’s foreign exchange reserves have increased and the pressure of gradually rising domestic labour costs has grown, this strategy of China’s, of seriously relying on cheap labour costs to occupy international markets, has begun to face challenges: on the one hand the pressure for appreciation of the renminbi is enormous, and on the other the pressure of low domestic labour incomes and sluggish consumption and domestic demand obstructing the further healthy growth of the economy is also enormous. China’s mode of economic growth therefore needs to undergo a profound change after the financial crisis of 2008: domestic demand needs to be boosted on the precondition of raising labour incomes, while at the same time the labour-intensive production capacity of the past is reformed and upgraded, moving finally onto a healthy growth road driven by domestic demand.

It may be said that economic growth is the most concentrated manifestation of all economic policy practice; but as the constraint conditions China faces change — as growth leads to higher incomes and ultimately to rising labour costs, as the upgrading of the consumption structure makes new demands on enterprises’ productive capacity, and as the profit space of export enterprises comes under the dual pressure of supply–demand contradictions and the exchange rate — these things make it necessary for China to abandon the extensive, external-demand-weighted growth model of the past and turn to a growth model with domestic demand as its focus. Under the requirements of this new growth model, new demands are inevitably placed on the behaviour of enterprises, consumers, and the government: the government needs to begin to attach importance to equity rather than to efficiency first; enterprises need more innovative capacity to match the implementation of the strategy of upgrading the industrial structure; and consumers need to renew their consumption model.

5.1.2 The Pattern of Primary Distribution

If growth of the economic aggregate may be called the foundation of the expansion of consumption and domestic demand and of the transformation of the consumption model, then income distribution, and primary distribution in particular, is the key to realising the transformation of the consumption model. In primary distribution, the respective shares of capital and labour, and the share of government fiscal revenue in national income, are important indicators determining a country’s basic macroeconomic pattern. Starting from pure free-market economic theory, fluctuations in the interest rate and in wages should correspond to the degree of change in the scarcity of capital and labour, while the benefit the government, as “night watchman”, obtains from the economy should be the smallest. However, because of the good effect of Keynes’s macroeconomic theory in the sphere of practice, the active role of the government in an economic crisis is still affirmed, and the concrete manifestation of this role is precisely the use of fiscal power to stimulate economic recovery; this accordingly became the basis on which governments strengthened the proportion of fiscal revenue. For a country such as China, with abundant labour and having only just emerged from the channel of a planned economy, the status of the government in income distribution cannot be neglected in its influence on the macroeconomic pattern.

At the founding of New China, China faced a situation of severe scarcity of capital and abundant labour. In order to resolve this prominent contradiction and at the same time to complete heavy industrialisation rapidly, China practised an urban–rural division running from the mobility of labour to market activity in general, and, through the agricultural system of “unified purchase and sale” (tonggou tongxiao), artificially depressed agricultural profits to subsidise industry, ultimately bringing about China’s realisation of heavy industrialisation at a relatively fast speed. After China began to step onto the track of the marketization process in 1978, the situation of capital scarcity was not relieved, while the trend of increasing labour gradually appeared. Zhang Jun (2002) used the change in the “capital–labour ratio” to measure the degree of capital deepening; this ratio can at the same time reflect the two factors’ contributions to the economy and their relative scarcity. Pu Yanping and Huang Yi (2008) hold that from 1978 to the early 1990s, the initial stage of reform and opening up, China’s “capital–output ratio” showed a clear declining trend; this means that the share of capital in output increased, and that the contribution of capital, suppressed in the planned economy period, increased after reform. At the same time the “capital–labour ratio” also began to rise steadily, which means that the contribution of capital, relative to labour, to economic growth also began to increase. This result of theoretical analysis coincides with the fact, in this period of China’s, of capital scarcity and relatively abundant labour, and such a fact also gave capital the advantage in primary distribution. Since the marketization reforms, therefore, no matter how capital’s contribution to the economic aggregate has changed, relative to labour it has always held the advantage.

Equally deserving of attention is that it was after this adjustment that China’s consumption rate began to move downwards; sluggish consumption and domestic demand replaced the financial crisis as the internal reason for the government to expand expenditure, and this has continued to the present day.

From the angle of the flow of funds, the conclusion is likewise similar and clear. In trend, the disposable income of Chinese residents has declined continuously since 1992, while the disposable income of enterprises has gradually risen, and the disposable income of the government is higher than that of enterprises and rises amid stability; in structure, the disposable income of Chinese residents is less than that of United States residents, while the disposable income of the government and of enterprises is higher than in the United States; at the same time the income distribution structure of the United States is very stable, while this structure of China’s develops over time in a direction unfavourable to residents. If the data structure of the United States is taken as the standard, one can discover that if 5 per cent were taken from each of the disposable incomes of the Chinese government and of enterprises and given to residents, China’s income distribution structure would be fairly similar to that of the United States. If the United States is therefore taken as the standard, China’s government and enterprises have crowded out residents’ disposable income.

From the analysis above, combining data with facts: in the primary distribution of national income, capital is the efficiency sector and has always occupied the advantageous position; this conforms to the laws of a market economy. The changes in the status of the government and of workers, however, were exactly opposite, changing from the initial “small government, large domestic demand” to “large government, small domestic demand”. Precisely for this reason, to transform the consumption model, raise workers’ incomes, strengthen the construction of the social security system, and ultimately raise the consumption rate, so that China steps onto a healthy and sustainable development road, the behaviour of government revenue and expenditure must be analysed. Analysed from the angle of the primary distribution of income, this means reducing the government’s share in primary distribution while at the same time raising labour income. Although this will cause labour costs to rise and affect the cost of export products, in the long run such a change in costs will impel manufacturing to carry out industrial upgrading and industrial transfer, while the significance of higher incomes for stepping onto the road of healthy development relying on domestic demand is far-reaching.

5.1.3 Promoting Employment and Raising Income

If the bias towards labour in primary distribution may be called the foundation for raising incomes, then promoting employment is the guarantee of realising income. Income consists of two parts: one part is obtained directly or indirectly through one’s own actual labour, and the other part is property income. The first kind of income comes from one’s own labour; and although property income ultimately also originates in labour income, this “labour” is not one’s own “labour” but the “labour” of other people. In the planned economy era there was no private property — that is, the second kind of income was negated and regarded as “exploitation” — so the first kind, labour income, was the whole of consumers’ income. As the marketization process has accelerated and its construction has gradually been perfected, the second kind of income has begun to appear in China, to be recognised, and to be encouraged; accordingly, income from assets such as stocks and housing has begun to appear and has gradually become an important source of income, apart from labour income, for a part of workers. We shall for the moment not consider the question of the source of property, and shall analyse only the quantity and nature of labour income and property income, and their relation to employment.

Precisely because employment is the sole route to obtaining the first kind, labour income, analysing the changes in China’s employment and the current forms of employment is of important significance for raising incomes and thereby promoting the transformation of the consumption model. Looking at the overall picture, the growth of employment posts in China is not synchronised with the growth of the economic aggregate — that is, the economy develops rapidly but its capacity to absorb employment does not increase correspondingly. According to the calculations of Liu Shejian (2005), from 1978 to 2002 the average value of the total employment elasticity for the whole country was only 0.22% (because of a change of statistical scope in 1990, 1990 is not included), that is, a growth of one percentage point in GDP could pull employment by only 0.22 percentage points. At the same time, before 1990 the average value of the total employment elasticity was relatively high, about 0.33, while from 1990 onwards this elasticity value was only 0.12. Even if one takes into account that in the early stage of the marketization process China mainly developed labour-intensive industries, whose capacity to absorb labour was relatively strong, while along with the transformation of the economic structure capital- and knowledge-intensive [industries] produced a crowding-out effect on ordinary labour and led to this result, the elasticity of 0.33 in the early stage of marketization still appears very low. Looking at the employment elasticity of the structure of the three industries, after 1990 the employment elasticity of the primary industry was negative for five consecutive years; this shows that a large quantity of labour transferred out of this industry. And from 1998 the average employment elasticity of the secondary industry also appeared negative continuously, which shows that labour also began to transfer out of the secondary industry; at the same time the tertiary industry maintained a relatively high employment elasticity throughout, and especially so in the years in which the employment elasticities of the primary and secondary industries appeared negative. This phenomenon, on the one hand, shows that China’s process of industrialisation and marketization has been accelerating as time passes, that the characteristics of industrial advancement are clear, and that the trend of labour transfer can explain this very well; on the other hand, the tertiary industry has throughout been an important sector for China in absorbing labour and creating employment posts. Accompanying high-speed economic growth there is not only the transfer of labour from lower-level industries to higher-level industries, but also the fact that newly created employment posts do not increase in step — that is, the absorptive capacity of China’s present industrial situation for new employment is becoming weaker and weaker, and this becomes a major obstacle to raising workers’ incomes and to the transformation of the consumption model.

The calculation results of Liu Shejian (2005) show that from 1980 to 1988 the quantity of employment grew rapidly; afterwards, apart from a clear growth appearing from 1992 to 1995, in other years the growth in the quantity of employment was not clear. The reason is, on the one hand, that from the early 1980s to the end of the 1990s was the period in which private enterprises such as China’s township and village enterprises and collective enterprises developed most vigorously; the capacity of this kind of enterprise to absorb labour was very marked. In this period state-owned enterprises had not yet begun to tackle the problems of enterprise efficiency and redundant personnel, so the problem of disguised unemployment had not yet appeared, until in the middle and late 1990s state-owned enterprises began “reducing staff to increase efficiency” (jianyuan zengxiao). In the same period, private enterprises also began to encounter bottlenecks in their development; their scale was all along unable to expand, causing their capacity to absorb labour to decline. These two forces together produced the fact of a falling employment rate. On the other hand, because enterprises in a market economy environment must pursue efficiency, intensive operation and raising the technical content of the enterprise become the rational choice of every enterprise. The extensive use of advanced technology has therefore, on the one hand, raised enterprises’ competitiveness within the industry and internationally and accelerated the speed at which enterprises move towards the high end of the industrial chain, while on the other hand it has also brought a demand for labour of high technology and high knowledge content; regrettably, the knowledge structure of China’s workers and its education system are still unable to adapt to so rapid a market adjustment, causing a large quantity of structural unemployment.

This means that after marketization reform, the forms of individual employment are not confined to state-owned enterprises; individual and private enterprises are also directions of employment. 5.2 The Improvement and Regulation of Financial Markets Are Necessary Conditions for the Transformation of the Consumption Model

As argued above, [consider] the sources of consumers’ income and their changes. As the marketization process has deepened, Chinese consumers, apart from labour income, also have non-labour income such as income from financial assets and income from housing. Although this income is not the main part of consumers’ income, because its existence changes consumers’ mode of cash income and their risk, and even changes consumers’ balance-sheet structure, it has a profound influence on consumer behaviour; the stock and kinds of assets and the income derived from them are therefore the guarantee of the transformation of the consumption model. At the same time, because the foundation on which the non-human-capital market exists is the financial market, the improvement and regulation of financial markets is a necessary condition for guaranteeing the stability of consumers’ income and its structure, and is thus also a necessary condition for the transformation of the consumption model; it may be said that without developed financial markets the transformation of the consumption model cannot be completed.

China’s financial markets have likewise experienced the transition from a planned economy to a market economy. The financial market that in the past existed only among Chinese enterprises, banks, and the government has gradually not only opened to the outside, but also opened to individuals. The instruments for the intermediation of funds include, apart from loans and bonds requiring mortgage or guarantee, also stocks, credit, and so on. The appearance of these instruments has greatly facilitated the efficient flow of funds and promoted the raising of economic efficiency, and has also provided individuals or legal persons possessing market vision with another channel of income. But China’s financial markets still have a very large gap compared with the developed capitalist countries of the West; this gap is manifested not only in the degree of consumer participation, but likewise in many aspects such as the use and innovation of concrete financial instruments, financial regulation, and the degree of perfection of the relevant laws. Perfecting China’s financial markets will therefore provide legal persons and individuals with a healthy financing environment, while the diversification of financing channels is also a necessary condition for consumers to transform their consumption concepts and ultimately complete the transformation of the consumption model.

The transformation of the consumption model discussed in this dissertation is concretely the transformation of the cash-flow model into the balance-sheet model: the transformation from a consumption model relying solely on labour income and personal saving for consumption to a consumption model in which income is diversified and which relies on various financial instruments to finance consumption. However, the basic precondition for using financial instruments is the system of private property rights; recognising and further perfecting the system of private property rights is therefore the foundation of the transformation of the consumption model. The cash-flow consumption model has already been expressed above by means of a balance sheet and a cash-flow statement; what is the typical form of the “balance-sheet model”, the goal of the transformation of the consumption model? 5.2.1 The System of Private Property Rights Is the Basis for Constructing the Individual Balance Sheet

The individual balance sheet discussed in this dissertation is similar to the ordinary enterprise balance sheet: it is a balance sheet, resembling an accounting statement, in which the assets and liabilities belonging completely to the individual are summarised according to the principle of balance — cash, bank deposits, housing, furniture and electrical appliances, and the like can be entered on the assets side, while housing loans and other types of borrowing belong on the liabilities side. Although individuals, unlike enterprises, do not pursue only the maximisation of profit, guaranteeing as far as possible that the balance-sheet structure is rational, and that the cash flow produced by the various incomes can pay the debts falling due, is also a main goal of household management. When income is low and there is no system of private property rights, apart from mere cash and bank deposits there are no other assets on the “assets” side of the household balance sheet capable of producing cash flow; at the same time, the dearth of financial instruments also leads to there being, under the liabilities side, apart from a very few private loans and the expenses payable for daily expenditure, no other payables; consumers therefore have no need at all to use a balance sheet to plan their finances, and a running account or cash-flow statement form is better suited to their needs. Under the cash-flow model, if a household needs to purchase a large-value commodity and its cash flow is insufficient to pay for it, the methods it can use are either to increase saving or to [borrow] through private lending; it cannot use mortgage or credit conditional on private property rights, although this [latter] method can complete the transaction payment very conveniently. In both form and substance, therefore, the cash-flow model obstructs the raising of the consumption rate to a certain degree. Under the balance-sheet model, by contrast, a household can mortgage any asset under the assets side and, at the cost of a certain price, obtain the commodity, recording it at the same time under the liabilities side. Although the household’s assets and liabilities both expand, so long as its cash flow can pay the debts falling due, the household’s balance-sheet structure is still healthy; more importantly, the consumption rate can be raised and the consumption path smoothed. In respect of raising the consumption rate, therefore, the balance-sheet model possesses the advantage, and constructing it requires first of all the recognition of private property rights.

Looking at the time and content of China’s recognition of private property rights, the main object is housing. Because of its great value and its great use value, housing is the most important fixed-asset investment and consumer good in the household balance sheet, so the recognition of the privatisation of housing undoubtedly has great significance for individuals in forming the balance-sheet concept. Not only this: the recognition of private property rights is also manifested in the recognition of financial assets and their returns, in the recognition of intellectual creations and their returns, in the recognition of labour capital and its future returns, and so on. It may be said that on the basis of private property rights numerous ownership rights and rights to returns can be defined, and this in turn provides a foundation for existence for financing instruments such as the discounting of future asset returns and securitisation, and the balance-sheet consumption model can also be constructed upon it.

The degree of China’s recognition of private property rights is limited; the most important asset, land, is not included within it. Whether in the countryside or in the city, the state is the owner of the land — it is an asset which, in the state’s balance sheet, has no clearly set price but an extremely high value — and the definition of land ownership also involves the question of the nature of the state. It may be said that flaws exist in China’s system of private property rights; this causes farmers, in the process of urbanisation, to become genuine proletarians, unable to exchange land and self-built houses for capital with which to enter the city and develop; the benefits of rising urban land prices cannot be shared by house owners, and the value of housing assets is easily underestimated, causing losses to individuals. If a healthy growth path is to be taken, therefore, a breakthrough must be made in the aspect of the definition of private property rights.

In accordance with the theory of modern property-rights economics, the rights attached to an article are not one but a “bundle”: as regards land, ownership, the right of use, and the right to returns are rights simultaneously attached to land but which can be shared by different persons. China’s land ownership belongs to the state, but the right of use and the right to returns take diverse forms. For urban residents, at the same time as purchasing housing they must also purchase the right to use the land, and for a certain term (70 years); for rural residents, a self-use house site likewise carries only the right of use and cannot be transferred. In the household balance sheet, therefore, there is a defect in the property rights over housing, this most important asset, and this becomes an institutional risk for the individual balance sheet and for the transformation of the consumption model. In particular, China is experiencing large-scale urbanisation, and the ownership of rural residents’ housing and land will become an important aspect of urbanisation and of boosting consumption. If the land use rights of rural residents and the housing farmers use themselves could formally be entered as assets under the assets side of their balance sheets — that is, if the private nature of this part of assets were determined through law — then, after a market for the circulation of ownership rights is established, China’s urbanisation can be smoothly advanced, while at the same time the time limit on the land use of urban commercial housing is abolished. Such “privatisation” neither destroys the state ownership of land, nor, on the one hand, gives resource-poor rural residents the ability to enter the city to live and consume, and, on the other hand, stabilises urban residents’ expectations of housing. This is the cornerstone of a stable and healthy balance sheet and the precondition for the transformation of the consumption model; otherwise, excessive risk will directly lead to a degeneration of the consumption model.

Apart from the questions of housing and land rights, the recognition of financial assets has greatly promoted the evolution of the content and nature of the balance-sheet structure. When consumers manage their own labour capital to obtain income, they also have the opportunity to manage financial assets to obtain income; this completely changes the mode in which cash flow is produced.

5.2.2 The Use and Trading of Financial Instruments Are Important Manifestations of the Transformation of the Consumption Model

On the precondition that private property rights are recognised, how the role of these assets is brought into play is realised through the use of various financial instruments. We know that a financial instrument, by calculating and measuring the potential total value of an asset within a certain time, and then charging a certain fee, delivers this potential value in advance to the original owner of the asset, while the person providing the financial instrument holds this asset or other assets and obtains the future returns of the asset. Within this, differences in the method of calculating the potential value of assets, in the assets included in the calculation, in the number of years calculated, in the mode of combination of assets, and so on, compose a rich and diverse basket of financial instruments; the use of these financial instruments is an important mark of the transformation of the consumption model.

First, consumption is the consumer’s subjective behaviour; although it may possibly be planned, it still carries a very strong randomness and arbitrariness, whereas income does not possess this nature; this causes income and expenditure frequently not to match. When income is greater than expenditure it is possible to save; when income is less than saving, the consumption behaviour will be unable to be completed. This presents a real demand for consumption financing, and is also the real foundation for the existence of financial instruments directed at consumption. In the planned economy era, the level of consumption was itself low, and its main purpose was to satisfy subsistence — food and clothing, for example. These consumer goods were in themselves not of high value, and in a low-level economic environment, in consuming them one could also lower the quality of these consumer goods so as to satisfy the demand for quantity; even if income was very low, therefore, consumers had no reason to finance consumption. In addition, quantitative rationing under the shortage economy meant that even if one had money one could not complete a purchase, so financing had still less necessity for existence. Second, because the system of private property rights was lacking, consumers had no possibility of financing through financial instruments. The part of income greater than expenditure had, apart from saving or purchasing government bonds, no other channel of appreciation, and this accordingly also became, when expenditure was greater than income, the most convenient and lowest-cost financing channel. The interaction and mutual reinforcement of these two aspects of force ultimately formed the cash-flow consumption model. In addition, this consumption model of financing through saving fits very consistently with the thought advocated thrift and practising economy in Confucian culture, so this consumption model was very easily realised in China and survived. In short, the system, the culture, and the real level of economic development acted together to produce the cash-flow consumption model; the transformation of the consumption model therefore requires these aspects to change together, and the use of financial instruments is required by the level of economic development.

Second, as China’s marketization process has gradually deepened and consumers’ incomes have risen steadily, the consumption structure has also been continuously upgraded, passing gradually from the initial “hundred-yuan level” consumption — wristwatches and bicycles, for example — to the “thousand-yuan level” of household electrical appliances, and up to the present level of “ten thousand yuan and above”, such as education, medical care, cars, and private housing. The upgrading of the content of consumption and its nature place very high demands on the aggregate of income; but for large-value consumption such as housing, even income that has already risen is insufficient to pay, and so the financial instrument directed at housing, the “housing loan”, resolved this problem. This form of commercial loan has also been applied to cars and education. Apart from loans, the appearance of credit consumption provided consumption with a still more convenient channel. The use of financial instruments directed at consumption is accordingly a requirement of real economic development, and is therefore also a manifestation of the transformation of the consumption model.

Third, financial instruments and the balance-sheet consumption model are naturally fused. Whatever the financial instrument, it is in fact a reconfiguration, within a period of time, of assets, returns, and risk; without assets there would be no financial instruments. When the level of economic development is low, individuals indeed have no assets; even if an individual balance sheet were constructed, there would not exist under its assets side assets of the nature of housing and land, so neither mortgage nor credit could exist. Under marketization conditions, an individual’s income, house, equipment, and even credit can all be used to guarantee or directly [obtain] financing; the consumption process simply evolves from the original decrease in cash assets accompanied by an increase in fixed assets into an increase in assets accompanied by an increase in liabilities, and financial instruments are the key medium playing the role of conversion and “lubrication” within this; financial instruments themselves are also recorded, as liabilities, under the assets or liabilities side of the individual balance sheet. In form, an increase in assets and liabilities at the same time may perhaps not have influenced the individual’s financial surplus; but this transformation makes the individual’s assets or consumer goods richer, which undoubtedly raises the consumer’s utility; from this angle, the balance-sheet model is also superior to the cash model. The use of financial instruments is therefore an important part and manifestation of the transformation of the consumption model; in a market economy, differences in individuals’ balance-sheet structures and modes of income also provide a broad space for financial instrument innovation, and provide space for raising consumers’ utility and for raising the profits of financial enterprises.

5.2.3 The Transformation of the Consumption Model Requires Effective Regulation

The transformation of the consumption model requires not only changes in policy and in economic entities to match it, but still more the support of perfection and innovation in the financial sphere; regulation therefore needs to be implemented not only in the sphere of the real economy, but still more deeply in the financial sphere. The original intention of financial instruments is precisely that they are instruments for reconfiguring assets, returns, and risk, so financial activity is a sphere in which risk gathers. More importantly, because of the development of the modern financial industry, financial instruments have become exceptionally complex: products such as options, stocks, bonds, swap contracts, and insurance combine and are proportioned with one another, and the financial derivatives produced have almost concealed the risk of the financial products; driven by a lack of motive to disclose risk and by the profit motive, the creators of financial products almost grasp all of the information about the financial derivatives. Severe information asymmetry is therefore the latent risk in using financial instruments, and financial regulation is intended to reduce this information asymmetry as far as possible, and to lower the risk of the financial products themselves that have been concealed, and the risk added by information asymmetry.

In the process of the transformation of the consumption model, the content of regulation should include two broad classes: regulation directed at the objects of transactions and regulation directed at the subjects of transactions. The former includes such things as the rights and articles entered in the balance sheet — housing, commodities, income, and other securities, for example. The latter mainly faces the credit and the assessment of the operation of individuals and enterprises. If divided according to the regulatory departments, banks, non-bank financial institutions, securities rating agencies, and the sellers of commodities should all participate in it; although their functions differ, the participation of these regulatory actors will effectively lower transaction risk, and thereby lower the risk of the deterioration of the individual balance sheet.

Since China began the reform of the housing system, housing has become the asset of the greatest value in the individual balance sheet; because of such factors as low income and excessively rapid rises in house prices, individuals purchasing houses basically all have to [do so] through bank loans. The individual mortgages the ownership of the purchased house to the bank, retaining only the right of use, and after repaying the loan with income within a certain term reacquires the ownership of the house. In this process, the individual’s income situation, the loan interest rate, and the term become the main content of regulation. We know that the outbreak of this financial crisis originated in the United States real-estate market: because of the long-term low-interest-rate policy, many households whose incomes were not high were able to obtain housing loans at very low cost, while in reality, once those households with a fragile financial foundation could not pay the debts falling due, the banks faced the risk of bad debts; in reality it was this excessively high proportion [of such households] that became the final cause of the outbreak of the financial crisis. Combining market interest rates with judging the income cash flow of house purchasers therefore becomes the core of the banks’ implementation of regulation. But because China has not yet established a comprehensive system of income regulation and credit investigation directed at individuals, the authenticity of individual income is not reliable, and in how many of all house purchasers [the purchasers] are sensitive to interest rates is also impossible to know; contained within this is a latent risk to the banks, which requires the establishment of an income regulatory department directed at individuals to be realised.

Securities are also an important part of the individual balance sheet; in particular, trading in the derivative financial products produced after [the formation of] basic securities portfolios is even more an object of pursuit, because of the enormous latent returns. But because the structure of financial derivatives is complex, the cash-flow structure is complex, and the cash-flow structure is far removed from the basic risk (the risk has not disappeared; it has merely been concealed), regulation of this kind of asset is also relatively difficult. Before the financial crisis, the United States adopted the method of “ex post filing”: after a new financial derivative had been manufactured, it was sufficient to file it with the Securities and Exchange Commission; after the financial crisis, “ex ante regulation” became the new method: before the Securities and Exchange Commission has approved its listing, trading in products of this kind is all illegal. This change in the mode of regulation institutionally suppressed the motive of investment banks to over-complicate products and conceal risk, and accordingly also suppressed latent financial risk. China’s financial derivatives market is of course still far from rivalling that of the United States, but this mode of regulation is worth China’s learning from.

As the times change, consumer behaviour is also changing: the thought of “living within one’s means” (liang ru wei chu) has begun to loosen, while “consuming in advance” has become popular among the young; this behaviour appears not only in the consumption of large-value commodities, but also in daily consumption, the most common being the use of individual credit cards. By the end of 2009, the quantity of credit cards issued in China had already reached 300 million, with nearly 100 million issued in 2007 alone; and in 2007, the amount of consumption by bank card reached 21.9 per cent as a share of the total retail sales of consumer goods in the same period. Credit cards indeed facilitate the act of payment, but because their application and grant rest entirely on “credit”, there is very great risk for both banks and individuals. In China, applying for a credit card is excessively convenient; almost no excessive financial examination, credit assessment, or mortgage is needed, and at most a guarantor need be provided. On the other hand, even if a bank needs to know more of an individual’s financial and credit situation, apart from the existing bank transaction records and the People’s Bank of China’s individual credit system there are no usable routes, so banks can only develop business under constraints. It can be seen that the credit assessment and financial assessment of individuals are likewise an indispensable part of banks’ lowering their operating risk. Regulation directed at individuals’ finances and credit is therefore also needed for banks’ development.

For individuals, in particular in the process in which individual financial management and modes of consumption change from the cash-flow type to the balance-sheet type, the importance of financial assessment, credit assessment, and regulation is still clearer. Individuals do not need, as enterprises do, to establish a set of standard financial systems, but when individuals carry out economic or other activities in the market they need to provide materials of this kind; introducing a third party outside the individual’s transactions to carry out assessment and intermediation can therefore effectively raise market efficiency and lower risk. At the same time, the examination, rating, and regulation of individuals’ economic behaviour by their transaction counterparties and by third parties are beneficial to the healthy degree of the balance sheets of both parties to a transaction. Effective regulation therefore needs to be executed not only by consumers themselves, but jointly by banks, commodity sellers, and newly established credit-investigation and regulatory departments directed at individuals; this has a positive effect in lowering market risk and raising the efficiency of market operation.

After the subjects of regulation have been made clear, changing the process of regulation is also an effective route to strengthening regulation. One of the shortcomings of the transformation of the consumption model from the cash-flow type to the balance-sheet type is precisely an increase in risk, because transactions under the cash model are always “paid for on the spot”, and no creditor–debtor relation exists between buyer and seller; under the balance-sheet model, by contrast, not only do debt relations exist, but the ways in which debts arise are many. Regulation of the individual consumer should begin from examining and verifying the consumer’s credit and situation of income and assets, and then, through regulating and controlling risk, [proceed] by the degree of performance of the repayment contract. But in the competition among financial institutions and other enterprises, such a sequence of regulation is not necessarily strictly executed — issuing credit cards too conveniently, a low cost of default, and an excessively long interest-free period, for example, all cause enterprises latent losses from bad debts, that is, risk. From the consumers’ side, because under their assets side there arise, apart from saving, other financial assets, and the structure and risk of these virtual financial assets are almost completely concealed by their manufacturers, they also bring individuals latent risk. [The mode of regulation] should therefore, as in the United States, change the sequence of regulation, turning from the filing type to the application type: the sellers of financial instruments first explain their detailed structure, returns, and risk situation to the regulator, and only after examination and approval can [the instruments] be traded in the market, rather than being traded first and filed afterwards — in that case the risk has already been transferred and regulation has already lost its meaning. Turning from ex post regulation to ex ante regulation is therefore a procedural change for effective regulation.

5.2.4 Speeding Up the Establishment of a Personal Credit Rating System Is Conducive to the Transformation of the Consumption Model

The characteristics of the cash consumption model determine that no credit risk exists between the two parties to a transaction, especially in spot transactions with individuals. Under the requirements of the transformation of the consumption model, when individuals take loans or consume on credit they need to make their own credit situation clear, and at the same time enterprises in transactions also need individuals’ credit materials. But the situations of numerous individuals differ in a thousand ways: the quantity and structure of the assets they possess differ, and because moral hazard and adverse selection exist, the authenticity of the materials they themselves provide differs, and the final results differ accordingly; between individuals and enterprises, therefore, suitable intermediary institutions are needed to carry out information verification and disclosure, reducing the risk brought by information asymmetry. This is the important content of the personal credit rating system.

The United States is the country with the most developed personal credit reporting system, which is closely related to credit consumption in the United States. The initial forms of credit consumption in the United States did not differ greatly from China’s: lending among acquaintances, pawnshops, sales on credit by shops, and usury were all once widely prevalent. But as population mobility became frequent, this form of credit among acquaintances gradually could not satisfy requirements, and privately operated consumer credit bureaus accordingly appeared in the United States. The “consumer credit bureaus” specially collected, and provided for a charge, personal consumer credit records, the number of defaults, and even private information beneficial for assessing personal credit such as marriage, migration, and employment. Because consumers were widely distributed, companies of this kind were numerous; in particular, in the initial stage of development, companies of this kind did not communicate data with one another, which made the relevance and continuity of personal credit records poor. With the gradual maturing of computer technology and the impetus of the United States federal government, these dispersed small enterprises began gradually to merge and reorganise and to share data; a “network effect” was accordingly gradually formed — the more numerous and the deeper the participation, the greater its own value. There now exist on the United States market several large institutions, forming a situation of monopolistic competition, whose information covers the whole country. When these institutions provide individuals’ credit situations, they basically score individuals’ consumption, loans, repayments, and so on in accordance with a procedural flow, ultimately forming the individual’s credit score, and enterprises then decide on the basis of this score whether to grant this person a credit loan. This credit rating directed at individuals greatly promoted the development of the United States consumer market: after the Second World War, the share of consumer credit in consumers’ disposable income rose from 4 per cent to 25 per cent, while the proportion of housing loans rose from 11 per cent to 80 per cent; what deserves noting is that this period is also precisely the period in which consumer credit rating began to move towards regularisation and scale.

The present degree of social development in China and the requirements of economic development are destroying consumers’ original modes of consumption and credit. First, as the marketization process deepens, the mobility of labour and capital gradually accelerates; this process has accelerated [the dissolution of] the basic structure of China’s “acquaintance society”, directly leading to a reduction in lending behaviour among acquaintances. Second, marketization has also pushed enterprises into competition; enterprises under the profit motive also wish to expand sales, expand markets, and thereby increase profits, but facing so large a latent market as individual consumer credit, because of the lack of credit rating the risk is very great, which forces enterprises to be very cautious in developing this part of the market, and makes it harder still for individuals to obtain credit loans from large financial institutions and enterprises. Third, because China’s credit assessment institutions directed at individuals are basically a blank, individuals who wish to obtain loans in a non-mortgage form lack channels; this has therefore seriously suppressed the consumption demands of a part of capable consumers, and also has an obstructive effect on the implementation of China’s measures for developing growth relying on domestic demand. It is therefore necessary to develop as quickly as possible credit recording and rating companies directed at individuals, of the kind found in the United States, which share information and form a network. What provides similar business in China is the People’s Bank of China: in November 2003 it established the “Credit Information Bureau of the People’s Bank of China” (Zhongguo Renmin Yinhang Zhengxin Guanli Ju), specially responsible for the work of collecting, preserving, and analysing credit [information] directed at Chinese natural persons; at present it already has the materials of about 600 million people. But this work in China started late, and its operating model is that commercial banks collect data through daily transactions and aggregate them to the People’s Bank, ultimately forming credit records. It is clear that this way of collecting credit records is not comprehensive: the records of many transactions between individuals and non-bank institutions cannot be included within it, and because this system has been established for only a short time, the effectiveness of its credit records still awaits improvement. The most effective method of making up for this system’s insufficiency is precisely, as in the United States, to encourage the establishment of private credit rating companies and, through computer technology, to achieve information sharing, ultimately building the personal credit system into a valuable network. In that case, no matter where consumers live or consume, and no matter when consumers apply for credit cards or loans, the information asymmetry between applicants and lenders can be effectively reduced, and credit can truly become the adhesive and lubricant of the market economy. In a deeper sense, the perfection of the personal credit rating system can also help consumers rationally plan their wealth and income at a higher level; this provides a route of improvement for the consumption model of the great majority of China’s consumers, who depress consumption and rely seriously on saving, and is beneficial for smoothing the consumption curve and for raising the consumption rate.

In addition, looking from the sphere in which credit arises, although it appears in the final form of “use first, pay afterwards”, the occasions of its arising are not necessarily in banks — education loans directed at individuals, small overdrafts of communications charges, and the like, for example. Precisely because of this, the spheres collecting and assessing personal credit information are by no means confined to bank transaction records, and that way of assessing credit relying solely on bank transaction and repayment records has clear deficiencies. A complete and comprehensive credit rating report should therefore gather together the various kinds of information from every sphere connected with the natural person’s everyday life, and this requires all kinds of enterprises and departments to communicate information with one another at a broader level. Even if this behaviour of communicating and exchanging information is mutually free of charge, for enterprises it is not necessarily “economic”, because the quantity and value of the information each enterprise itself possesses differ greatly, and “non-equivalent exchange” easily arises between them. Resolving this problem therefore also requires the coercive power of the government and the establishment of specialised credit rating enterprises to be realised. The government’s coercive power can impel data communication between enterprises, while the existence of specialised enterprises can guarantee the comprehensive comprehensiveness of their collection of credit information. The existing profit-making enterprises of this kind in the United States were also initially realised through state coercive power and the establishment of specialised private companies; by providing standardised personal credit scores, for a charge, to those needing to know personal credit, they make a profit, and ultimately, through market competition, formed the monopolistic-competition pattern of institutions of this kind in the United States today. The development of credit rating companies is closely connected with the development of credit consumption in the United States, and also greatly promoted the development of personal credit assets; this also became one of the reasons for flourishing consumption and a high proportion of consumption contribution in the United States. It may be said on this basis that personal credit rating in the United States has developed relatively maturely; it is a very good reference for China in developing credit rating companies, and is also an important means for China at present in transforming its mode of economic growth and raising consumption and domestic demand.

5.3 Building a Sound Social Safety Net Can Effectively Promote the Transformation of the Consumption Model

It has already been demonstrated in the theoretical part that “precautionary saving” in consumer behaviour is not only rational but also profoundly affects the proportions among saving, assets and consumption. This “precaution” is mainly directed at various uncertain events that may occur in the future, such as illness, retirement, children’s education and other contingencies. Events of this kind do indeed possess “uncertainty” in their nature, but over the long term they are again “certain”, so that meeting the large payments arising when these events occur becomes the main purpose of precautionary saving. But there are many ways of financing future uncertain events. In a society lacking social insurance mechanisms, the individual’s own saving is the only route; when incomes are extremely low, borrowing among acquaintances, or relatives and children providing insurance for one another, are the routes available; in modern society, commercial insurance and similar insurance mechanisms provide another route of financing. These two routes have the same purpose, but because the manner of contribution differs, consumer behaviour differs completely. Under a manner of financing that relies wholly on one’s own saving, completing the saving target as early as possible becomes the ultimate aim, and to this end consumers will depress current consumption and other outlays against a possible emergency, because apart from this the individual has no other way of insuring for the future. If such insurance is wholly provided by the state or by commercial insurance, then consumers need not save for future uncertainty and may raise current consumption. It can be seen that under the latter model consumers are completely insured, that is, they live in a secure economic environment; such an economic environment composed of various complete forms of insurance is a social safety net, and the degree of completeness of this net determines to a great extent a society’s degree of stability and thereby determines economic growth and development. But whether one proceeds from efficiency or from raising the consumption rate, the construction of a social safety net cannot be free of charge to the individual, so its coverage and the degree of that coverage need to be decided in combination with the level of economic development. In the planned economy period China implemented complete insurance in the cities, but because the level of economic development could not support insurance at so high a level, this mode of complete insurance was in the end of low efficiency. After entering market-oriented reform, China moved to the other extreme: under conditions in which the level of insurance was not high and coverage was confined to the cities, individuals paid the greater part of insurance costs, which made the social safety net not safe, with the result that consumers, from the precautionary motive, greatly increased “precautionary saving” and reduced current consumption, causing China’s consumption rate to fall year after year. China’s construction of a social safety net should therefore undergo greater reform in step with the level of economic development. For Chinese households and consumers, the protection they currently face in this area comprises mainly four parts: medical care, old-age provision, education and housing. Because of the special nature of housing, this dissertation places it under assets for separate analysis; this part mainly discusses the relationship between the other three items and consumption and the transformation of the consumption model, and the direction of future reform.

5.3.1 Improving the Medical Insurance System

Establishing an efficient medical protection system is a real problem that the governments of all countries must face: on the one hand, because the medical system involves everyone, reform in the medical sphere bears on social stability; on the other hand, because the cost of medical expenses rises day by day, reform of the medical system bears on economic efficiency.

The development of China’s urban medical protection system has mainly passed through two stages. From the early years after the founding of the state to the mid-to-late 1980s, China implemented completely publicly funded medical protection for urban residents and students in school: individuals bore no cost, or very little, and the rest was wholly borne by public finance. At that time China’s total population and urban population were not large, and the age structure of the population was far from ageing, and in addition the overall level of medical care and its cost were not high, so this system could still operate. But as China’s urban population increased, this system began to strain at the seams and in actual operation could not achieve complete bearing of medical expenses. Thereupon, with the gradual deepening of reform and opening up and of the process of marketization, from 1989 China’s medical protection system began to move towards a new stage of development, and some provinces, cities and regions began to reform this system. The first problem with the original system of complete protection for urban residents was that its cost was too high and could not possibly be maintained at China’s current level of economic development; another problem was that it did not include rural residents, which was unfair. In 1994 the Third Plenary Session of the Fourteenth Central Committee formally proposed establishing a medical protection system of individual contributions and social pooling, and in that year piloting began in four cities including Jiujiang; in 1996 this system was extended across the board. If the transition of the medical system from complete protection to partial protection is a thorough institutional change, then the influence of this institutional change on consumers is also profound.

First, looking at the proportions in which medical expenses are paid: before the medical reform they were borne wholly by the government or the “work unit”, and the individual basically bore nothing; but as can be seen from the tables below, after the medical reform the government’s share of the burden fell from the theoretical 100 per cent of before to less than 20 per cent, while the individual’s share of the burden rose to more than 50 per cent, and the remainder is borne by enterprises and other institutions. This means that medical expenses have not only been transformed from implicit costs into present costs, but that the individual’s share of the burden has clearly increased: that is, when a consumer needs to pay medical expenses, not only must the money be paid at once, but more is paid than before.

The analytical conclusion of this dissertation is: illness cannot be controlled, but by reforming the medical insurance system medical costs can be shared more effectively and reasonably, and the individual’s share of the burden reduced.

Illness is something that no one can completely avoid, and medical expenses in particular increase gradually as age increases. But looking at China’s medical costs in aggregate, the individual has to bear more than 50 per cent, and in fact individuals bear still more. There are two reasons for this: first, the government bears too little; second, the irrational pricing system for medicines makes medical costs high.

Combining this with the data above, one finds that since market-oriented reform, government fiscal revenue, after a short period of decline, has begun to increase steadily, but the share of medical costs it bears has not increased; in absolute terms, then, the total paid by the government has fallen, and it is in this very period that individuals’ medical outlays began to increase. This rise and fall shows that the process of the socialisation reform of urban medical care in China is precisely the process of the government withdrawing from the core position in paying for medical care. This “withdrawal” directly increased individual consumers’ medical outlays, and what is more important, this kind of institutional change gave consumers a strong tendency towards “precautionary saving”, and in the end became an important cause of the insufficiency of China’s consumption and domestic demand.

On the other hand, after the reform of the medical system began, China’s pharmaceutical production enterprises acquired a great deal of freedom in selling medicines, and pricing according to the market became the most important method. When medicines cannot be sold directly to patients, enterprises promote them through the channel of hospitals and doctors, and all the expenses arising from this are passed on into the price of medicines and in the end borne by patients, increasing medical outlays. In addition, because the profit margin on low-priced medicines is limited, pharmaceutical production enterprises, driven by profit, are more willing to produce high-priced medicines, and even raise prices by changing the name of a medicine without changing its formula, which has also become an important cause of falsely high medicine prices. Analysing the existing system, the government has no incentive to control such falsely high prices, because the higher an enterprise’s profits, the more fiscal tax revenue the government receives, and provided the government’s share of medical costs does not change, the government will even tacitly permit enterprises to raise medicine prices. So in order to lower medical costs one must first raise the government’s share of the burden, so that the government together bears high prices; at the same time one must sever the chain of interests among pharmaceutical production enterprises, doctors and medicines, so that medicines can flow only from pharmaceutical plants to the bearers of medical costs, such as national medical funds or commercial insurance companies, and then let the insurance companies decide whether a prescription can be insured and in what proportion.

China’s present medical system clearly does not yet have any such institution or practice, so it is very important to establish a specialised public institution to examine and approve prescriptions, and it is also important to bring commercial insurance institutions and medical funds into the process of medicine circulation. If the sharing of medical costs is reasonable, then consumers’ misgivings in this respect — the “precautionary” motive — will diminish, and they will consume with a more easy mind.

5.3.2 Improving the Old-Age Insurance System

The root cause of the old-age problem is that at a certain age, for physical reasons, workers are no longer suited to working, but without work there is no income, so pensions need to be paid regularly to the elderly who have retired but are still living. The sources of this payment are, first, the workers’ own saving; second, subsidies from government and enterprises; and third, socialised pooling, that is, the part prepaid by the young but not yet usable by them being used to pay the pensions of the already retired elderly. In China, besides these methods, support of parents by their children is also an important form. All of these sources of pensions operating within a market framework already exist and operate in China since the reform of old-age insurance; why, then, is the pension problem still a motive for households to undertake precautionary saving?

First, the level of payment is not high. In the planned economy era, the payment of pensions in China, like medical insurance, was borne wholly by the state, but after reform the main form became one in which the individual has a part deducted from wages, the enterprise pays in a part, and public finance subsidises a part. Because this reform has been implemented for only a limited time, the total amount of old-age funds in society as a whole is limited; although, as individuals’ incomes rise and young people’s wage incomes rise, the total amount of the funds grows, when it is apportioned to each elderly person receiving a pension very little remains. Even so, compared with international levels, China’s level of pension payment is still on the low side: in Germany, Sweden and Denmark this ratio is at its lowest 4.5 per cent and at its highest close to 6 per cent. Although these countries’ population structures and levels of economic development differ from China’s, the low level of China’s pension payments is an indisputable fact.

Second, there exists in China an obvious urban–rural dual division, and the coverage of the old-age insurance system has by no means penetrated deep into the vast countryside, yet the rural population of 900 million is the main part of China’s population — which is to say that for a considerable majority of China’s workers there is no old-age insurance. In these circumstances self-saving and support by children become the only solutions.

Finally, because China has long implemented the family planning policy, the arrival of ageing in China has been brought forward, and because the proportion of the young population relative to the elderly has fallen — according to sample survey data, China’s old-age dependency coefficient rose from 10.06 in 1995 to 13.04 in 2008 — at the same time, because of the marked fall in the share of the young population, the phenomenon of gaps appearing in pension receipts and payments grows ever more serious.

This reality strongly constrains individuals’ economic behaviour. In order to guard against the possibility that in the future pensions may be insufficient to maintain their original standard of living, or even insufficient to maintain a basic livelihood, rational individuals will raise saving and the level of assets held, to meet this uncertainty that may occur in the future. But raising the saving rate affects the consumption rate; from the macro standpoint, the influence of perfecting the old-age insurance system and raising the level of payment on raising the current consumption rate is sufficient and direct. Starting from the viewpoint of relaxing the constraint that pension payments place on consumers’ consumption, the resolution of the pension problem can be approached from several such angles. First, the retirement age of workers can be appropriately raised, and the retirement ages of male and female workers can be made the same, so that workers who originally received pensions become workers who receive wages and pay in pension contributions, which can clearly ease the contradiction of the “population dependency ratio”. Second, a specialised investment company can be established to operate the pension funds, and under conditions guaranteeing a fixed return, to let the pension funds obtain excess returns on the capital market as far as possible, opening up new sources of income. Third, the proportion paid in by individuals can be lowered and the proportions paid in by enterprises and public finance raised, while at the same time unifying the level of pension payment nationwide and bringing the agricultural population of the regions with higher levels of economic development also within the old-age insurance system. Fourth, in the formulation of the pension system, “hard constraints” should be adopted as far as possible — that is, reducing changes back and forth — which can effectively lower uncertainty and thereby reduce precautionary saving. Finally, the family planning policy should be amended, to avoid in the future still more serious problems of the dependency ratio of the population structure, and of insufficient labouring population.

Although China lacks consumption data at the micro level, we can still connect certain facts and macro data to find the association between the old-age system and the level of consumption. In China there exists a relatively obvious gap between urban and rural areas and a gap between east and west, so the adult labour force of the less developed provinces and the countryside flows in a concentrated manner to the economically developed regions and cities, and those who remain in their home places are basically the elderly, women and children. As regards total income, the household income of workers working away from home is higher than that of purely farming income, so it seems one may infer that the consumption rate in these places will not be low, especially as, with competition among the labour force, these people’s incomes are no lower than those of the local urban population, so it seems we may infer that the household consumption rate of these migrant workers should be comparable with the urban consumption rate. This dissertation has randomly selected, from the east, centre and west, partial relevant data on the towns and countryside of six cities, mainly disposable income and consumption expenditure. By calculating the ratio of consumption to disposable income (the “consumption rate” spoken of in this section), it can be discovered that, in whichever city, the consumption rate of urban residents is clearly higher than that of rural areas, and that the trend of change of urban residents’ consumption rates is basically identical — the consumption rate gradually declines while the marginal propensity to consume in each case presents a stable state. Next, from east to west, as the degree of economic development weakens and as the levels of economic development of different cities differ, the differentiation of rural residents’ consumption rates is obvious, but the general trend is that the consumption rate is negatively correlated with the degree of economic development. Such a result gains meaning when connected with the population structure and the old-age system. First, the coverage of China’s old-age insurance system in the countryside is very low, which compels the rural labouring population, on the basis of greatly raised incomes, to continue increasing saving in order to meet future payments for old-age provision. Second, looking at the household population structure, the average number of persons per rural household is greater than in the cities, so as the ageing society arrives, rural residents’ old-age payments are greater still; this becomes another important reason why rural households raise saving and reduce consumption. Of course, the causes of a low consumption rate in the end must be composite, but the precautionary saving that households undertake for old-age provision does indeed crowd out a large amount of residents’ disposable income, and is one of the important causes of reduced consumption.

5.3.3 Improving the Education System

Education, like medical care and old-age provision, is a rigid outlay that China’s residents must pay, especially since the transition from free education in the past to fee-charging education at present (the actual costs in the compulsory education stage have in fact not decreased but increased). Looking at the number of schools, the numbers of China’s kindergartens, primary schools and junior middle schools fell respectively from 172,322, 766,072 and 71,953 in 1990 to 138,209, 280,284 and 56,167 in 2009, while in 2001–2008 the numbers of primary schools and junior middle schools fell from 556,789 to 358,555, a reduction of 35.6 per cent. At the same time, over this period the changes in China’s population aged 6 and above (2001: 1,138,165; 2008: 1,106,434) and aged 15 and above (2001: 945,741; 2008: 974,433) allow one to calculate that the population between 6 and 15 years of age fell from 192,424 to 132,001, a reduction of 31.4 per cent. So, looking at quantities, the supply of and demand for educational resources are still in balance, at least no worse than before; why, then, have the expenses that households now use on their children’s education increased to the point of becoming a household “burden”, even affecting the structure of daily consumption outlays?

First, the transformation of the model of higher education stimulates citizens to prolong their years of education, with the result that total expenditure on education rises. China implements nine years of compulsory education; education at senior middle school level and above is optional. Before the expansion of enrolment in higher education institutions, the scale of recruitment by China’s higher education institutions was very small, and higher education was not fee-charging — that is, the so-called “elite education” was implemented. In these circumstances individuals chose in advance, according to their own conditions, whether to receive higher education or to enter the vocational education system directly. But after China’s higher education reform, with expansion of enrolment and the implementation of a fee-charging system, the elite education model changed into a mass education model, which directly raised the expectation of the population of the relevant age that they would receive higher education, notwithstanding that fees must be charged. As a result, a part of the labour force that would originally have begun to obtain income at the age of 18 joined the ranks of higher education, and in the four years of university not only has no income but requires the household to pay out costs on its behalf. This “one less and one more” gradually became an important part of the household’s budget of receipts and payments.

Second, the unbalanced allocation of educational resources gives rise to vicious competition and pushes up expenditure on education. After the reform of higher education, although fees are charged, national regulations must still be followed; but the large expenditures arising in the compulsory education stage, which ought to be free, have to a great extent already been marketized, and state regulation is basically ineffective. Of these, the two largest parts of expenditure are, first, extracurricular tutoring classes, and second, expenditures of the “school-choice fee” kind. In theory, individuals can completely choose not to incur these two parts of expenditure, because the requirements of the education system lay down no hard stipulation; but in reality these two outlays are basically both rigid, and the reason is precisely the unbalanced allocation of high-quality educational resources. Every student wants to enter a good school and at the same time every school wants to recruit good students, so how to distinguish the “good” requires costs, and because schools occupy the initiative in this game, the great majority of the costs are borne by students. Students must prove their excellence through results, activities and experience beyond their results in school — that is, they must emit “signals”. What is more important, this part of the cost does not occur within the schools, but is provided by enterprises operating in a marketized manner. So the arising of these expenses does not violate the regulations on compulsory education, yet becomes a heavy burden on households in the compulsory education stage. On the other hand, the phenomenon of schools charging high fees other than tuition fees is widespread, and these fees are much higher than the fees of the higher education stage, so this cannot but become an important consideration in the household’s budget of receipts and payments. Vicious competition from the students’ side not only increases their own economic burden and psychological burden, but at the same time the vigorous demand it produces pushes up the price of extracurricular tutoring, forming a vicious cycle of competition. Analysed from the standpoint of economic theory, when supply is rigid, an increase in demand produces “rent”; if a limited supply is allocated by methods of rationing such as queuing, this leads to the dissipation of rent, and in order to be more efficient the market needs to retain this rent and convert it into profit — but such an institutional arrangement, in which rent produced inside the system is seized by enterprises outside the system, directly leads to an increase in the burden of household expenditure, and this is precisely what is irrational in China’s current system of elementary education. The most fundamental direction for changing this system is to balance the allocation of educational resources throughout the country, and to prohibit marketization in the sphere of elementary education.

This dissertation regards medical care, old-age provision and education as important contents of the construction of a social safety net, not only because these problems bear not only on households’ daily budgets of receipts and payments and levels of consumption, but also on social stability. At any time, the daily life of ordinary consumers is nothing but food, clothing, housing, transport and daily necessities, medical treatment, old-age provision, and children and descendants; these problems are in fact the problems that economic development ultimately needs to solve, and are therefore a foundation stone of a society’s stability, and the development and progress of other aspects of society and the state all depend on the proper solution of these problems. China’s society has its special characteristics: the population is numerous, urban and rural areas are divided, economic development is unbalanced, and so on. The population problem cannot be changed in a short time, but other institutional problems can be changed in a short time. In the planned economy era, the solution of these problems relied on their being uniformly undertaken by the state and the work unit, and individuals basically did not need to pay fees directly; the market economy pursues efficiency, but this does not exempt the government from its obligations in these respects — it is only that the original implicit costs have been made explicit, and the complete bearing of costs by the state and the work unit has become joint bearing. So the government’s institutional construction and fiscal input in the respects bearing on the protection of households and consumers directly determine a society’s stability, and thereby also determine household behaviour, economic behaviour in particular. If the government can issue and perfect policies in this respect guaranteeing that consumers have no so-called “worries about what is behind them”, then consumers, apart from normal saving, will correspondingly reduce precautionary saving, and this part of saving may be transformed into actual consumption demand. In the long term, the influence of such institutional construction on the raising of the consumption rate will be systematic rather than technical, and will thereby, at a higher level, fit together with the systematic change that is the transformation of the consumption model.

5.4 The Stock of Household Assets Is the Core of the Transformation of the Consumption Model

The transformation of the cash consumption model into the balance-sheet consumption model is the transformation of debt-free consumption into debt consumption; it is the consumer using future income as collateral to consume a certain product in advance. So in this process of transformation the simple cash-flow method of accounting can no longer satisfy requirements, and a balance sheet must be used. Let us suppose a general situation: on the assets side there are mainly cash, saving, housing, large consumer goods such as cars, securities, various insurance funds, and so on; at the same time, on the liabilities side there are mainly various long-term mortgage loans payable, short-term debts payable, credit debts, and so on. The sources of income are mainly labour income; besides this there are also subsidies from the government and the returns on various investments under the assets side. On a healthy balance sheet not only are assets greater than liabilities, but the consumer is able to pay debts falling due on time. The enrichment of the contents of the assets side and the increase in total assets will raise the consumer’s utility, while on the other hand an increase in liabilities lowers utility.

Under these accounting concepts it can be discovered that the cash-flow consumption model is in fact only a special form of balance sheet: that is, its liabilities side is blank, and the consumer’s consumption behaviour is only the conversion of the form of assets under the assets side, such as cash being converted into saving, or saving into a car. The greatest advantage of this model is that the consumer can never fall into bankruptcy — that is, assets are always greater than liabilities and there are no debts falling due — but its disadvantage is that the speed of renewal of assets is slow and the cycle of purchasing large assets is very long; where the cash flow of income is limited, this cycle may even be longer than the life cycle, with the result that the conversion of the form of assets cannot be completed and the consumer’s utility is lowered. But the balance-sheet model has no such disadvantage: with the assistance of a good financial system, the individual can consume a certain product in advance by the simultaneous increase of assets and liabilities, expanding the balance sheet and raising utility, and at the same time, provided assets are greater than liabilities and debts falling due can be paid — that is, there is no bankruptcy — this balance sheet is healthy and sustainable. Although the balance-sheet model brings other risks, such as interest-rate, income and credit risks, under the requirements of the current development of the economy and the upgrading of the consumption structure, the transformation of the consumption model will be the trend.

After a stable flow of income and a relatively perfect protection system have been established, the consumer’s focus naturally turns to how to raise his own utility through the gradual upgrading of the content of consumption, and how to complete the capitalisation of income, credit or other assets, and ultimately to complete the expansion of the balance sheet and the gradual raising of utility. Rational consumers also have a certain order in choosing assets under the new model: first, the assets with a high total price that are most important to the household; next, assets that can increase cash flow; and last, ordinary assets. For Chinese households, the importance of housing is important in whatever sense, so the object of Chinese consumers’ debt consumption is generally housing. Precisely because of this, housing mortgage financial business in China has also been developed earliest, has the widest coverage, and is the financial method most easily accepted.

Because the transformation of the consumption model leads consumers to begin to bear liabilities, how to guarantee normal operation becomes the main goal of household operation, and the solution lies in increasing cash inflow. Generally speaking, besides labour wage income, financial assets such as securities become an important content by which households increase cash flow — especially when there are many assets under the assets side that can generate returns, so rational consumers will convert the form of their existing cash flow and existing interest-bearing assets as far as possible into the asset with the highest return, in order to reduce the risk of bankruptcy and increase returns. So analysing the balance sheets of Chinese households is beneficial for explaining high house prices, high saving and low consumption, and for the formulation of macroeconomic policy.

5.4.1 Housing Assets

The utility of housing to the consumer is unique; it cannot be replaced by other consumer goods and investment goods, and it is precisely this uniqueness of housing that determines its position in the consumer’s catalogue of consumption. But China’s population is relatively large compared with its land area, and the unbalanced economic development in particular has led to a high concentration of population, so the direct problem this brings is that the price of commercial housing is high, far exceeding the capacity of ordinary households to bear it, to the point that huge potential demand and limited supply form a sharp contradiction. This dissertation does not discuss at length the causes of the formation of high house prices, but directly accepts this fact, and on this basis focuses on discussing the formation and conversion of housing assets in the household balance sheet. Suppose that after the household’s most basic consumption has been satisfied, it must first complete the various tasks within the life cycle, the first being housing. Let us record the speed of increase of income as $y(t)$, the rate of change of house prices as $p(t)$, and the magnitude of increase of the most basic consumption as $\delta$ times the magnitude of increase of income (0 < $\delta$ < 1). Suppose that at time $T$ the household is able to complete the task of purchasing a house; then the following equation (1) holds:

$$ \int_0^T p(t)\,dt = \int_0^T [y(t) - \delta y(t)]\,dt \qquad \text{(1)} $$

Differentiating equation (1) with respect to its upper limit of integration gives:

$$ p(T) = (1 - \delta)y(T) \qquad \text{(2)} $$$$ \frac{p(T)}{y(T)} = (1 - \delta) \qquad \text{(3)} $$

From (3) it can be seen that, as time changes, if house prices rise faster and faster, then in order to complete the task of purchasing a house the household must reduce the proportion of basic consumption and increase saving — that is, reduce the consumption rate and increase the saving rate. Because completion of the planned tasks is subject to the strong constraint of the life cycle, in the special case in which the household foresees that, relying on income alone, it is impossible to complete the task within the first cycle, $\delta$ may take a negative value — that is, the household purchases a house in advance through borrowing, locking in the total price of the house, and then repays within the remaining life cycle, or even has the next generation continue to repay.

The reality in China is that, because the fundamentals of supply and demand determine that housing will remain a scarce product for a relatively long time, rising prices are the rational expectation of all consumers, and the reality of the real estate market continually proves the correctness of the expectation of a rise. At the same time, the saving interest rate has for a relatively long time been maintained at a low level, and if the level of inflation is calculated, China has already entered an era of negative interest rates; so even without considering the direct use value of housing, the value of investment returns alone is sufficient to become the motive force for households to convert other assets into real estate. Such asset conversion is the rational behaviour of consumers, especially for consumers purchasing their first house. The problem now is that natural persons, and even enterprise legal persons, with high incomes all use capital to convert assets into real estate, causing a bubble to exist in house prices, and in the end making consumers who originally had the ability to purchase housing unable to purchase it. This makes those who have not purchased housing bear losses in both use value and potential asset value. If this situation does not change, then consumers will be divided, according to whether they have housing, into two parts, and the balance sheets of these two parts of people will appear extreme: those with housing improving more and more, those without housing deteriorating more and more. So this dissertation holds that there are no more than three such methods for changing this potential trend. The first is to increase workers’ incomes and improve the pattern of income distribution, especially to increase the income cash flow of those who have not purchased housing; concretely, a subsidy method can be adopted to distinguish the two categories of people. The second is to broaden individuals’ investment channels, guiding capital to flow towards the real economy and increasing investment returns. The third is, through financial innovation, to transform the appreciated part of consumers’ own-use housing into actually usable returns, to be used to repay housing loan debts in advance. Doing this can change the constraint conditions under which banks issue housing loans, which also constrains the repeat purchase of housing by those who have already purchased, without damaging their balance sheets.

5.4.2 Financial Assets

The financial assets discussed in this dissertation are mainly securities other than saving and housing, especially stocks, bonds, funds and insurance; these assets are the third type of asset usually held by China’s consumers. The most important reason why assets of this kind become an important part of the balance sheets of households and consumers is that their potential returns are higher than those of saving, while their liquidity is also stronger than that of housing; although financial assets of this kind also involve risk, because their liquidity is relatively strong consumers can adjust within a short time and reduce losses. But assets of this kind did not exist from the beginning; they gradually entered individuals’ balance sheets only as China’s process of marketization gradually deepened. Because the profit situation of assets such as stocks and funds is closely related to the operation of enterprises and changes in the macroeconomic environment, changes in the external economic environment can affect the health of the individual’s assets and liabilities through this channel, and correspondingly, because households undertake debt consumption, the individual’s level of assets and liabilities also affects the operation of enterprises. It can be said that financial instruments link enterprises and individuals more closely together, linking the micro individual with the macro economy that it constitutes and with macroeconomic phenomena. So discussing the influence of financial assets on the individual’s balance sheet is the micro foundation for analysing macroeconomic phenomena.

The expansion of investment channels not only added financing channels for enterprises, but also broadened consumers’ channels of return. But unlike directly raising income, consumers’ raising of returns through purchasing financial assets does not depend wholly on the direct increase of cash flow brought by stock dividends or by buying and selling; the “wealth effect” is also another important channel. The wealth effect refers to the consumer’s total individual assets increasing because financial assets appreciate, on the premise that the balance sheet is healthy — that is, “wealth increases”. Under conditions of increased wealth, consumers regard this as equivalent to an increase in cash flow, and thereby raise the consumption rate or other outlays. Qu Liqing and Wang Hongli (2007), using monthly data from June 2001 to December 2006 to carry out an empirical study, discovered that the influence of China’s stock market on residents’ consumption reverses according to the cyclical fluctuation of the stock market. There is much similar research, and the conclusions are relatively similar: that is, the wealth effect of China’s stock market exists but is not strong.

According to Friedman’s theory of “permanent income”, a rise in transitory income does not raise consumption; only a rise in permanent income can raise consumption. According to this viewpoint, the reason why China’s stock market, having developed to the present day, with both market value and tradable market value markedly expanded, nevertheless has a very limited pulling effect on consumption, is that the returns brought by financial assets such as stocks are all transitory returns. Why is it that, after nearly twenty years of development, the stock market’s aggregate growth is marked, yet it cannot bring growth in permanent income? The reason is that the fluctuation of China’s stock market is too violent; the strong fluctuation leads consumers, when investing in the stock market, to be more “short-sighted” — that is, to attend to short-term interests. 5.5 The Expansion of the Middle Class Is the Guarantee of the Transformation of the Consumption Model

Marx was the first to recognise and analyse the important role of the middle class in social development. In the Communist Manifesto Marx pointed out that in capitalist society, the process of the dissolution of the intermediate strata and the gradual sinking of the intermediate strata into the proletariat in the end leads to the intensification of the contradictions of capitalist countries, and in the end becomes the foundation of social turmoil and proletarian revolution. Whether or not Marx’s analysis of how capitalism develops holds, there is no need to doubt the stabilising effect of the middle class in society and the economy — Marx merely recognised this problem from another angle. Although “middle class” is at present still a term that is widely used yet offends against strict definition, however it is delimited, the social function of the middle class is always positive, in politics and in economics alike. This positive stabilising characteristic manifests itself in the economic sphere, first, in that the people of this stratum have stable incomes and social status: their income can guarantee the maintenance of daily consumption and of large outlays in respects such as housing, medical care, education and old-age provision; their social status is not only clearly delimited by occupation, educational record, work and so on, but also obtains the positive recognition of other people in society. It is precisely on the premise of these “stabilities”, and with the protection of private property rights and the total quantity of wealth as guarantee, that these people’s consumption behaviour is determined to differ from that of the strata at the two extremes. The existence and stability of the middle class are an important content of, and guarantee for, the transformation of the consumption model.

For China, whether the standard that a household with an income greater than 60,000 yuan belongs to the middle class is set too low or too high, obtaining data on the total assets of households is not an easy task. So this dissertation does not strictly define the middle class in respect of concrete standards, nor construct an indicator system for it; it only analyses the association between the balance-sheet consumption model and the management of the balance sheet on the one hand and the judgement of social strata on the other.

According to the unified thought of this dissertation, which considers the balance sheet, the total quantity and the kinds of the assets of the individuals and households of the middle class must all reach corresponding standards, and the balance sheet must be healthy. That is to say, this dissertation holds that individuals and households of the kind that have no liabilities, lack non-saving assets such as housing, stocks and bonds, and whose daily life is paid for wholly by the cash flow of labour income, do not belong to the middle class. Only individuals and households that have the ability to maintain a reasonable and healthy level of liabilities, whose total assets are at the same time greater than the social average, and whose kinds of assets are not single, are the middle class.

The reasons for defining it in this way are, first, that the middle class is certainly not the stratum in society relatively lacking in economic resources, but relative to the rich stratum it again cannot attain complete dependence on the cash flow generated by investment to maintain the balance sheet — that is, the middle class still needs to work to obtain labour income in order to guarantee that its cash flow is sufficient to pay debts falling due. Second, there already exists very stable asset income in the middle class’s cash flow, whether this income comes from housing or from financial assets. Finally, because the middle class’s manner of managing income and assets has evolved into the balance-sheet form, their consumption model has correspondingly been transformed into the balance-sheet form, and debt consumption and the management of liabilities and risk have become important contents. It is because the middle class possesses these economic characteristics that the influence on it of changes in the macroeconomic environment is major, while at the same time the aggregation of its modes of behaviour has a major influence on the macro economy — especially as the middle class gradually becomes the mainstream of a healthy society.

5.5.2 The Unique Role of the Middle Class in Economic Operation

The unique role of the middle class in economic operation originates in the middle class’s economic condition and manner of operation. First, the middle class is active in both the labour market and the capital market. Its cash flow contains both labour income and asset income, and this dissertation holds that the proportions of asset income and labour income determine whether it is closer to a higher or a lower stratum. This characteristic of the middle class determines that on the one hand they must participate in competition in the labour market, and on the other hand they must attend to changes in the capital market and rationally adjust the structure of their assets and liabilities to avoid losses. What is more important, this part of the population has a stable marginal propensity to consume and possesses relatively strong capacity and desire to consume — that is, it conforms to “demand” in the economic sense — so developing consumption and domestic demand and raising the consumption rate should attach importance to this part of the population.

Second, the middle class accepts and is accustomed to liabilities, and has the ability to manage and operate its own balance sheet. A rational person, whichever mode of consumption he adopts, will certainly have optimised the result; this means he will not accept liabilities or credit consumption beyond the scope of risk he can accept. Looked at from another angle, liabilities are caused by consumers’ rational demand, not by innovation on the supply side. That the middle class can accept liabilities under certain conditions has three main causes: first, its income is relatively high and stable; second, its channels of income are diverse; third, its relatively higher grade of consumption still requires liabilities. So these people have the desire to guarantee the health of the balance sheet, and to this end they need still more stable jobs and a healthy capital market to guarantee sufficient cash flow to pay debts falling due; so the middle class possesses the need, the motive force and the ability to maintain a healthy balance sheet. Considering these factors, those policies and economic fluctuations that have a major influence on the middle class have a great influence on their balance sheets, and in turn may possibly influence their consumption decisions. For financial institutions, these people’s situation as regards assets and liabilities is closely associated with financial enterprises’ rate of bad debts and profitability, and in turn with whether macroeconomic operation is stable. Once they change their economic behaviour — for example, by converting the proportions of the assets they hold, or changing their ratio of liabilities — the impact on the macro economy will also be obvious. So maintaining the middle class’s balance sheets is not the ability and responsibility of individuals alone; policy makers also have the duty of helping them to maintain them.

Third, according to the usual economic regularities and under healthy results of economic operation, the numbers of the middle class, compared with the rich and the poor, are absolutely the greater, and the social stratification presents the stable structure of being large in the middle and small at both ends. This situation of numerical majority is favourable to social stability. According to theoretical definition and practical experience, a society in which the middle class is in the majority displays relatively stable performance in economics, politics and other respects, while those with great polarisation between rich and poor, whose social stratification takes a “dumbbell” form, are generally unstable. This is mainly because the middle class is not at the bottom in economic and political status, and has channels and the ability to continue developing upwards, so this part of the population’s requirement of the political and economic environment is precisely stability — it can be said that this part of the population is society’s “stabiliser” or “buffer zone”. Thus if the formulation of economic policy and the results of development continually expand the middle class, then this society is certain to grow stably and healthily.

5.5.3 The Middle Class Is an Important Link in Building a Harmonious Society

China is today building a harmonious society and advocating healthy and stable development, so strengthening the cultivation of the middle class should be part of the meaning of this. But thirty years after entering the process of marketization, the economic aggregate has grown markedly, yet workers’ feelings have not grown; on the contrary, every stratum has many dissatisfactions with the current policies, and the contradictions among strata have gradually appeared. These show that China’s economic development still takes the extensive mode and aggregate development as its goals, neglecting the ultimate purpose of economic development. If in the period of capital scarcity in China such a development strategy still had a staged significance, then in circumstances in which China has already become the third-largest economy, the largest creditor of the United States, and its volume of trade is stably in the world’s top three, single-minded attention to the simple quantitative growth target that is GDP seems far from sufficient. Under the requirement of the national strategy of building a harmonious society, the formulation of economic policy should turn towards favouring the cultivation and stabilisation of the middle class, and through the gradual development of the middle class drive the stable development of the country as a whole.

As discussed earlier, the middle class is a relative concept; for China, the middle class is those workers who possess a stable inflow of cash and a healthy balance sheet. According to this standard, those whose structure of cash inflow contains only asset income or only labour income certainly do not belong to the middle class, and nor do those who have no liabilities in their balance sheets or whose structure of assets is single. This dissertation holds that, to build a harmonious society, the expansion of the middle class must start from several such aspects. First, because housing accounts for the largest proportion of assets, the magnitude by which it raises the household’s utility is the most obvious, and its effect on social stability and harmony is therefore the greater, letting the number of people who own housing expand is an effective way of expanding the middle class, and is also an important step in building a harmonious society. There are many ways of letting households own housing, and the problem should be solved in tiers. For high-income groups, who are completely able to pay in full, the existence of high-grade commercial housing on the market should be permitted. For low-income groups, the government must guarantee these people’s right of residence through the construction of public housing to be rented out. For consumers with a stable cash flow, efforts should be made to help them purchase housing through loans, becoming the middle class as defined in this dissertation. But the speed at which China’s current house prices are rising is too fast, causing the total price of housing far to exceed ordinary households’ capacity to pay — even the down payment requires all the members of a household to save for a long time before it can be completed — so house prices rising too fast have become the greatest obstacle preventing ordinary consumers from crossing into the ranks of the middle class. In order to expand the middle class, house prices must be restrained, not only controlled as regards the speed of price increase but also controlled as regards the total price. Letting housing assets enter ordinary consumers’ balance sheets as soon as possible is the most important route to building a harmonious society and expanding the middle class. Second, employment and the securities market should be stabilised, which can stabilise the middle class’s inflow of cash and keep the balance sheet healthy. If housing assets successfully enter the balance sheet, the most important thing for the consumer is to maintain the health of the balance sheet, and guaranteeing sufficient cash flow to pay debts falling due is then the most important task. For this part of consumers, both parts — labour income and asset income — must be kept stable. Thereupon the healthy development of the employment market and of the securities and housing markets becomes the focus. As regards the development of the employment market, the lawful rights and interests of ordinary workers must be protected, equal pay for equal work strictly implemented, and the various insurances and the housing fund strictly paid in. Third, the kinds of the middle class’s assets and liabilities should be expanded, actively developed especially in respect of consumer goods; developing credit consumption can effectively raise the level of the middle class’s utility. The middle class’s capacity and desire to consume are stronger and more stable than those of the other two strata, but consumption behaviour often lags because income lags, so developing credit consumption and other forms of debt consumption helps to expand China’s currently depressed consumption rate. Although credit consumption is still different from debt consumption, considering the middle class’s motive force and ability to maintain the balance sheet and a good credit record, this type of consumption is still risk-controllable for individuals and financial institutions alike. Fourth, the obstacles to mobility between strata should be demolished, such as household registration and other privileges. At present the resistance obstructing the expansion of the middle class comes not only from economic respects such as income; its deeper causes are non-economic factors such as household registration and power. Under the requirement that the process of marketization gradually deepens, the flow of factors is accelerating, which requires that the flow of people must also operate according to the laws of the market. The household registration system is a system peculiar to China’s planned economy era; its purpose was to rely on the agricultural surplus to guarantee the rapid growth of industry, but at present the necessity for this system’s existence no longer exists — on the contrary, it has become an obstacle to economic growth. Artificial definition of identity brings it about that unfair competition exists in the labour market, thereby obstructing the optimal allocation of labour resources and economic efficiency. In addition, China’s economic aggregate relative to its population is still very limited, and economic development is unbalanced besides, so the ways of competing to obtain resources are diverse, and in particular power’s interference with free competition seriously damages economic efficiency. So, considering China’s longer-term growth and development, China needs to carry out reform in its political system, resolutely demolishing these barriers that obstruct free market competition, letting resources — labour resources especially — flow freely and leave channels for upward development. This is the foundation of social stability, and only thus can the sustained growth of the economy be promoted in the long term.
