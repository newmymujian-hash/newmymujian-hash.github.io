Restructuring the Regional Development Model

Full English translation. A reconstructed framework of regional development models, built on a new resource classification and macro differential rent.

✦ 23 min read

Jian Mu

Originally published in Chinese in Journal of Hexi University, 2005, Vol. 21, No. 4, pp. 14–17.

Abstract: By summarising regional development models and, at the same time, drawing on a new classification of resources and on the concept of macro differential rent, this article reconstructs the framework of regional development models, incorporating the old models into the new framework so that they stand alongside the original models without contradiction. Within the new analytical framework, it also analyses each specific region with its own characteristics, so that in reality a specific region can more easily find the development model suited to itself.

Keywords: regional development model; macro differential rent; reconstruction

Chinese Library Classification No.: F061.5; Document code: A; Article No.: 1672-0520 (2005) 04-0014-04

I. A Summary of Abstract Models of Regional Development

Whether we observe the process of regional development longitudinally or compare the current state of development of developed and underdeveloped regions horizontally, we can see that the process of regional development runs from absolute closure gradually towards a relatively open economy, and finally develops into a completely open economic regional system. The spatial structure of a region accordingly also divides into the following different stages: (1) The isolated system stage. At this stage the spatial structure of the regional system is a balanced, static structure. Local centres are relatively independent; every city is the centre of a small region, forming a balanced state of rest. (2) The relatively open regional system stage. This stage has the typical core–periphery structure. Because capital is relatively scarce in the initial stage of regional development, only a small number of cities — those possessing comparatively abundant natural resources, or a dense population, a broad market, convenient transport, or proximity to foreign markets — can be selected as the priorities for development; central cities therefore appear in succession. The capital and labour of the outlying areas move towards the central cities, and new development areas develop around them. (3) The open system stage. When the polarisation effect and the trickling-down effect of a region act evenly, the social and economic systems of the whole region fuse into one, its efficacy is brought fully into play, and the region has the greatest potential for growth, thereby forming the spatial structure of a functionally interdependent urban system. As the current state of regional development evolves and the spatial form of a region evolves, the regional development models that accompany them should also adapt to this change. (See Figure 1.)

II. Constructing a New Regional Development Model, and a Concrete Analysis

Surveying present-day regional development models, we have no difficulty in discovering that there are many constraining conditions and restrictive factors, such as resources, geographical location conditions, and the level of science and technology. But among these many factors, we can classify them, according to a new standard, into three categories (in the short term): the first is factors whose quantity or quality can change and which are endogenous — that is to say, factors that can be formed by a region’s own strength under isolated conditions, such as human resources, the level of science and technology, and institutions; the second category likewise can change, but its change is exogenous, such as inter-provincial highways, the internet, and communications networks, which are indispensable when contact with the outside world takes place; the third category is invariable, such as mineral resources. The new standard adopted here is the concept of macro differential rent. Macro differential rent means the differential rent into which is transformed the excess income that, as between different regions, some regions obtain because of their advantageous occupation of the factors of economic development. It mainly has the following types: (1) Macro differential rent I: its condition of formation is abundant natural resources, mainly comprising the reserves of minerals and the convenience of exploiting them. Different regions differ greatly in their reserves of resources; regions with abundant resources that are convenient to exploit naturally have excess income that other regions do not possess, and become the entities of macro differential rent I. (2) Macro differential rent II: its condition of formation is a superior location for opening to the outside. Different places within a country, because their positions differ, differ greatly in the convenience with which they engage in economic exchanges; regions situated on the front line of opening display more advantages than other regions in attracting funds and technology and in conducting trade, and thereby obtain higher excess income than other regions, forming differential rent II. (3) Macro differential rent III: its condition of formation is the differences in transport and in the dissemination of information. Differences in these respects will lead to differences in the level of production and operation and in income levels, thereby forming differential rent.[1] Thus, starting from the concept of macro differential rent, we can divide all regions, according to these three standards, into a few representative categories; representing them in three-dimensional coordinates gives the following (Figure 2): the points of these representative regions are points A, B, C, G and O, and zones I, II and III (points D, E and F are assigned to the various faces for discussion). Starting from this new concept of macro differential rent, we can then construct new regional development models for each region, proceeding from the differences in its differential rent.

When each region chooses a development strategy suited to itself, it must not only proceed from its own conditions, but must also conform to the circumstances of the spatial structure of the regional development in which it is situated; only then is it possible to combine its own development model and development strategy with that structure, incorporate itself into the whole large economic system, and thereby obtain rapid development. This paper, accordingly, takes these three factors as the analytical starting point and the logical starting point for regional development models, and combines them with the laws of evolution of regional space, thereby constructing a new theoretical framework. In Figure 2, different regional development models are adopted according to the circumstances of different regions, and the existing regional development models are incorporated into this new theoretical framework, so that each region with its different characteristics can choose the regional development model and development strategy suited to itself in the light of its actual circumstances.

The present state of a region is like an arrow in the cube of Figure 2: it is a vector, and the purpose of development is to make every arrow point towards point E, or point towards point E to the greatest extent, because in the view of this paper point E is where the degree of development is the deepest and the broadest — this is precisely the goal of development. What, then, is the driving force behind the movement of this vector’s change?

First, there is the top-down pull of government. Although under market economy conditions the role of enterprises and of the market is the principal one, no form of development can do without the support of government. If, for example, one wishes to move closer to the variable axes, a large amount of funds is needed to build public infrastructure; normally, only government has the strength to make such enormous investments in public goods. In addition, when development is carried out, the coordination and unification of social goals, economic goals and ecological goals likewise require the strength of government.

Second, there is the bottom-up push of enterprises. In a genuine market economy, it is the various market actors that play the leading role; therefore, whether in a trough or at a peak of the economy, there is always a batch of enterprises with vigour and strength serving as the power source of regional development, and the vitality of a regional economy likewise comes from these enterprises. From another aspect, the best way for a region to carry out development is to cultivate enterprises that have potential in some respect.

Finally, the force of innovation will push the arrow’s movement. This includes scientific and technological innovation, institutional innovation, and so on. These are at once a driving force of resource advantage, but in essence they are a driving force.

III. The Choice of Model at the Isolated Stage

Under isolated circumstances, because the links among cities or regions are almost non-existent, regional development at this stage cannot choose a network development model; at this stage it is mainly a matter of a development model that relies on the region itself while at the same time also giving consideration to developing outwards.

As regards point A, its advantages in talent and in science and technology are extremely prominent, but its natural resources are scarce and its communication with the outside world is not smooth (this situation is not common in reality; because what this paper discusses is a limiting case in theory, this point may be passed over without discussion).

As regards point C, it is a typical transport hub, but is only a transfer station; it can rely on the advantages of its transport lines, concentrate on developing human capital, and transform its institutional conditions.

As regards point G, all three of its resources are relatively abundant, and it may be called a “triply advantaged” region; its levels of economic and of scientific and technological development are situated in a high-gradient zone, its capacity for innovation is relatively strong, and it also has advantages of resources and of location. This region developed early, its economic foundation and economic strength are solid, its industrial structure is rational and its industrial levels are high, and it is also in an advantageous position in human capital. Therefore, in the economic policy for its regional development, it is appropriate to choose to invest comprehensively in every field of the economic system and in every industry, forming a strong pull of investment; the various industries mutually provide markets for one another, and the enormous markets for capital goods and for consumer goods in turn pull the region’s economic growth along, making it the pacesetter of economic growth. A region situated at this point has a strong power of attraction for the various factors of production: the funds, talent, information and other resources of the surrounding regions flow into it in an unending stream, producing a polarisation effect in geographical space and forming what Perroux called a “growth pole.”

As regards regions situated at point B, they are typical regions possessing only resources (like Karamay in our country). For regions whose social, economic, scientific and technological development is relatively backward but which possess resources and regional advantages, one injects capital and manpower and chooses a model in which the development of resources drives the local economic development; when formulating a strategy for social and economic development, it is necessary to base oneself firmly on the local advantages of resources and location and to develop a characteristic economy. The resource-development type of economic development model is generally suited to regions whose natural resources are very abundant: through the production, processing and export of resources, capital accumulation is realised, the deep processing of resources is then deepened, and pillar industries standing on the advantage of resources are thereby established. Through the driving effect of the pillar industries, related industries are developed, and a relatively rational economic structure and system are finally formed.

As regards regions situated at point O, they have neither natural resources granted by nature, nor regional advantages, and their social, economic, scientific and technological development is also relatively backward. How to accelerate their economic development is a great problem that troubles economists and the governments of all countries. Many countries have, to different degrees, a “western problem,” and a great commonality of the western problem is backwardness of economic and of scientific and technological development. Their economic location is in an inferior position, the initial conditions of their development are relatively poor, historically they developed rather late, and therefore they are in a relatively backward position in every respect. The basic characteristics of such a region are: vast land and a sparse population, backward infrastructure and investment environment, and conservative thinking and concepts — as, historically, with the West of the United States, the West of China, and so on. For such regions there are two lines of thinking on development: one is to develop characteristic agriculture and labour-intensive industries; the other is to develop a characteristic economy, including high and new technology, and realise a leapfrog development of the economy. That resources are scarce, the geographical location is at a disadvantage, and the economy and science and technology are relatively backward does not mean that social and economic development has no way out. For regions that are in an inferior position in every respect, if they choose the correct development strategy and persist in unremitting effort, it is entirely possible for them to realise a leapfrog development of society and the economy. In the history of regional development in the countries of the world, the rise of Utah in the United States is a full illustration of this: a region in an inferior position, so long as it has correct thinking on development and the support of specific preferential policies, can also create an economic miracle. Utah is situated in the mid-west of the United States, with an area of 220,000 square kilometres; its geographical conditions are similar to those of the north-western provinces of our country: there are many mountain ranges, 35 per cent of the land is desert and arid land, and its climate is close to that of Lhasa in Tibet in our country. Utah’s population is about two million, of whom 77 per cent live in the state capital, Salt Lake City, and in the mountainous areas around it; before the 1990s its economic development was slow and it was relatively backward among the states of the United States, a small and poor state. But after 1990, Utah’s economy rose rapidly and became the largest software base in the United States. The road that Utah took in this period is one of relying on high technology for a new economic growth point, using high and new technology to transform traditional industries, adjusting the economic structure, formulating a corresponding development strategy, developing a diversified economy, and using high technology to drive economic development. In the cube of Figure 2, as regards the regions on the OC axis that are very close to point O, because their conditions and foundation for economic development are poor, if they are to achieve an economic take-off they must attach importance to investment in human capital. Another important experience of Utah’s rise is to “give full play to the strengths of talent, and avoid the weaknesses of resources.” Utah, a “third-line” region of the United States, lacks natural resources and its economy is relatively backward. After the end of the Cold War, the government of Utah attached great importance to bringing its local advantages into play and improving the soft and hard environments. At the same time, it accelerated the construction of information networks, constantly improved the highway network, accelerated the transformation of the power grid, improved the conditions of water supply, and increased investment in education. The state government devoted 60 per cent of the government budget to education, and the illiteracy rate in the whole state was only 6 per cent, the lowest among all the states of America. From this it can be concluded that, for underdeveloped regions, under conditions in which the advantages of resources and of technology are lacking, an important experience for accelerating economic development is to take precautions in advance, accurately grasp the new trends in the development of the world’s economy and of science and technology, and, in the light of changes in the social and economic environment, adjust the economic structure in good time and realise a leapfrog development of the economy.

As regards zone I, it is a region of quality, possessing a large quantity of resources and a potential in developable human capital, but because it lacks links with the outside world it has turned itself into a relatively isolated region; this is in fact not beneficial to regional development. Therefore, such a region should choose a point-axis development model: taking itself as the centre, it should vigorously develop links and communication with the outside world, so that in its form of development this region transitions towards being relatively open or absolutely open, and truly transforms its own advantages of resources and of talent into economic advantages.

As regards zone II, it is a region that possesses resources and communicates well with the outside world, but the degree of development of the endogenous variable factors inside the region is not high — for example, the quality of the local population is not high, or the institutional environment is poor — thereby restricting its use of its existing advantageous resources and making it unable to become, like point G, a region of a high degree of development. The recommendation for the development of such a region is to learn from the advanced experience of developed regions in the development of manpower and in the building of internal institutions, formulate development goals suited to itself, or construct a loose institutional environment, attracting talent from other regions to enter into this region’s development and make up this region’s deficiencies.

As regards zone III, it is a typical resource-scarce region, possessing good institutions, excellent talent, and superior conditions of location (such as Hong Kong and Shenzhen in China). The development strategy of such a region is likewise very clear: it is the advantageous-location development model — that is, relying on its own advantages, it chooses cutting-edge science and technology as its pillar. It has the advantages of economic and technological development, that is, historically its economy and culture have been relatively developed and its production technology relatively advanced. In choosing a development model, a resource-scarce region should give full play to its advantages in capital and in technology, and choose a capital- and technology-type development model. This region may be called a Perroux region. Because science and technology are a powerful driving force promoting economic and social development, although there is a certain distance between the level of development of science and technology in our country, and of the regions of our country, and that of the world’s scientifically and technologically developed countries, in scientific research funding and so on, nevertheless, so far as our own country is concerned, some regions possess a comparative advantage in economy and in technology, and in some local fields even possess a certain comparative advantage internationally. If they persist in taking science and technology as the core, grasp the trends in the development of the world’s economy and of science and technology, and, at the early stage when new technologies and new products enter the market, invest and tilt policy in a conscious, purposeful and focused way, they will form a polarisation effect in geographical space and attract a large inflow of the funds, technology, talent and information of the surrounding regions, producing a centre of regional economic growth. What Perroux’s growth pole theory describes is precisely that certain large propulsive industries produce, in a specific region, a driving and promoting effect, and, when a diffusion effect appears, produce a powerful driving effect on the economic development of the surrounding regions. In choosing a capital and technology development model, the starting point in selecting the leading industries must be high: one should select for key support, and make into the pacesetter of regional economic growth, industries and products that represent the direction of development of the world’s economy and of science and technology, have broad market prospects, and have relatively strong linkage effects, lateral effects, backward effects and forward effects.

IV. The Relatively Open Stage

When the spatial structure of a region breaks away from the isolated form and forms a relatively open pattern, each part of the cube should have a different development model. First, because its links with the outside world have strengthened, its development model can have richer choices — for example, the gradient development model, and the leapfrog development model.

It is still a matter of point G. Its conditions are clearly superior to those of the other regions, so this region is a high-gradient zone that carries out innovation in new products, new technologies, and even new management methods and methods of organising production; its mission is to carry out innovation in order to maintain its leading position in every field. At the same time, it must also resolutely transfer those technologies and industries that are about to decline to low-gradient regions. If this is not the case, then other regions — for example, the regions in the zone II part — may possibly catch up with and surpass it and take the place of point G’s position. Because at this time we assume that links with the outside world exist, and moreover are not occasional, and in addition the region’s natural resources are given, the exogenous variable axis and the invariable axis in the cube of Figure 2 already exist; zones I and III therefore do not need to be discussed. At this time only zone II remains. It can be observed in the cube of Figure 2 that, to push a regional economy towards a higher gradient, the important variable factor is the endogenous variable factors — that is, as defined in the text, such things as the quality of the population and the institutional environment. Therefore, after the economy of this region has been incorporated into the whole economic environment, natural resources and the gradient position in which its own industries stand are exogenous; only through developing science and technology and education, raising the quality of the population, raising the productivity of labour, and further improving the institutional environment can it obtain further development. Those regions whose institutional environment and quality of labour are not good may adopt a leapfrog development model, use the science, technology and industries transferred down from the high gradient to develop their own science and technology, and, while making use of advanced science and technology, create an institutional environment suited to scientific and technological development and innovation, thereby making this region move towards a higher gradient step by step.

V. The Open Stage

When the economy further develops into the third form, that is, the absolutely open form, all regions, seen from the macroscopic level, have already been incorporated into the economic network; but at a more microscopic level there still exist regions that have not been fully developed. That is to say, when we magnify those regions that are at the growth poles, we can still discover that the various situations described above exist; therefore we must again use the same method to analyse at a more microscopic level and carry out further development, until the regions at every level have been fully developed and the region forms, in its morphology, a region with rational industrial structure, appropriate proportions, and industrial chains and urban belts with the capacity for sustainable development.

VI. Conclusion

Through the analysis above of the characteristics of regional development in different periods, and through reconstructing regional development models under the new conditions of resources, this paper has concretely analysed which development model the regions with their respective characteristics within the new framework are suited to choose. For China, where the process of urbanisation is becoming faster and faster, the importance of every city and region finding a development model suited to itself is self-evident, and the analytical framework above is all the more suited to a China that is in the midst of change.
