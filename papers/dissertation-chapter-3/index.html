Chapter 3 The Formation and Development of the Cash-Flow Consumption Model

The economy is a complex composite in which universal connections exist among its factors; problems in the sphere of consumption therefore cannot be connected with the sphere of consumption alone, nor can the solution to consumption problems be sought only at the link of consumption itself. It is necessary to analyse comprehensively income, finance, money, financial markets, the age structure of the population, years of work, the social security system, and every other aspect. It may be said that consumption is the final link in the whole cycle of society’s material production, and that the various contradictions in the sphere of consumption are in large part the concentrated expression of contradictions at the links of production and circulation. The primary reason for insufficient consumption demand, for example, may be that income is low, which in turn leads to insufficient consumption demand; or income may increase year by year while unfair income distribution leads to structural insufficiency of consumption demand. It is not necessarily the case that continually growing household saving alongside persistently sluggish consumption means that the return on saving is higher than the return on other investment; it is more likely that an increase in households’ expected risk has raised the precautionary motive; and the dependence of households’ consumption model for large consumer goods on saving rather than on income and future income is not necessarily caused by consumption habits, but is rather a rational decision made in pessimistic expectation of future employment, income, and social security.

Problems of this kind are numerous in the sphere of consumption, but existing research basically explains them by studying the relationship between consumption and one factor or another, without combining consumption and the other aspects into a unity. Such analysis may be profound, but it detracts from comprehensiveness, and because it neglects the connections among different factors its conclusions lose generality. This dissertation therefore proceeds from the standpoint that can reflect these problems in a concentrated and unified way — namely, the consumption model. The consumption model is not merely the sum of what is consumed, what is used to consume, and how consumption takes place; it must rather be discussed together with growth, income distribution, the age structure of the population, the construction of the social safety net, and so on, if an objective and correct analytical result is to be obtained. The consumption model is a concept of comprehensive significance that organises these factors within a framework possessing universality. At the same time, analysis of the transformation of the consumption model needs still more, on the basis of analysis of the individual quantities, to emphasise the connections among factors and the influence of other constraints upon the core that is consumption. The comprehensive analysis that synthesises these individual quantities and connections is what this dissertation calls the study of consumption models.

A consumption model is composed jointly of income, expenditure, and risk. A consumption model in which the levels of income and expenditure are low, risk is low, and uncertainty is small lacks elasticity; at a low level of economic development it can basically satisfy the needs of the household, which resembles China’s situation before the marketization process. Accompanying this, a clear consumption model gradually took shape, whose core characteristic is that daily expenditure depends on the cash flow of income and large expenditure depends on saving; large expenditure in particular has an obvious stage-based character — that is, an expenditure peak appears in a certain period, with a considerable interval of time between the peaks. In the era of the planned economy, the consumption model of Chinese households was very simple: the necessities of life were subject to a rationing system, and households basically had no power of consumption choice; the purchase of large consumer goods such as the “three big items” required many years of saving, or private borrowing among acquaintances. Because there was no financial market directed at private individuals, consumers paid entirely in cash; this dissertation therefore calls this consumption model the “cash consumption model” or the “cash-flow consumption model”. The formation of this consumption model was built first of all upon a basis of commodity shortage: an economy of relative overproduction in production cannot universally exhibit the cash model. Secondly, for this consumption model to operate over the long term corresponding systems of control are also required, controls embodied in the state’s all-round management of consumers’ status, consumption behaviour, enterprise behaviour, and so on. Under the cash consumption model, although the standard of living was not high, ordinary consumers had no worries about housing, employment, old-age provision, and so on. This matching, at a low level, of consumption content and consumption behaviour formed the starting point of China’s cash consumption model.

3.1 The Income Basis on Which the “Cash-Flow” Consumption Model Was Formed

The so-called cash consumption model means that when households carry out transactions they use cash entirely as the means of payment, and that the source of this cash derives in its great majority from the household’s own saving; only in very few cases is financing obtained through acquaintances rather than through financial institutions. The cash consumption model was not only the concentrated expression of the level of China’s economic development at the time; this consumption model also restricted the rise of households’ consumption level and restricted the formation of a modern consumption outlook among Chinese households.

From the founding of the country to the economic transition in 1978, China practised the planned economic system. Its industrialisation level was not high and its economic connections with foreign countries were not close, which made the economic aggregate small and residents’ income low. Reflected at the level of material consumption in society as a whole, this reality was an all-round “shortage”: a “shortage” of income and a “shortage” in the supply of consumer goods alike. Lowness on both the income and the expenditure sides forcibly suppressed the consumption level of Chinese residents. At the same time, under public ownership, labourers did not need high expenditure on medical care, old-age provision, education, and housing; the low-level but relatively equitable security borne by the state still reduced household expenditure to a considerable extent, which to a large degree was equivalent to receiving a subsidy. As regards household structure, first, because the family planning policy had not been implemented, household size was generally large, and households consisted mostly of young people with the capacity to work, so that the dependency ratio was low; in a country with deep traditional conceptions such as China, the connections among household members are close not only in residence and in sentiment but also in economic matters, which to a certain degree formed economies of scale and an internal household “insurance” mechanism. Under the joint constraint of these income and expenditure conditions, the “cash-flow consumption model” gradually took shape.

3.1.1 Explicit Wage Income and Interest Income from Saving

In the early period after the founding of the country the economic foundation was weak, and systems controlling labourers’ income, such as “graded wages”, were widespread; they strictly restricted the total amount of income, its rate of growth, and its differentials, until in 1952 the state promulgated the Directive on a Uniform Increase of Allowances for Personnel under the Supply System throughout the Country, which formally prescribed the wage grades and corresponding amounts for the armed forces and state personnel. In August 1955 the Order on the Universal Implementation of the Wage System for State Organ Personnel and the Reform of the Monetary Wage System was promulgated, which further straightened out the wage system operating on the dual track of rationing in kind and monetary wages. Wage grades were also adjusted to twenty-nine grades, the highest wage being 649.6 yuan per month and the lowest 21 yuan per month. After rationing in kind was abolished, the wages of state personnel rose by a relatively large margin; this was not only an expression of the state’s increased strength at the time, but also the making explicit of implicit wages. The state subsequently also implemented a system of allowances for special posts, raising the wage income of workers and technicians in order to speed up the process of industrialisation — that is, to increase input into industry. Because the wage level at the time was very low, the great majority of income was used for payment, the Engel coefficient was very high, and even where a portion of monetary surplus existed it could not be spent on normal consumption because of quantity controls on commodities; a part of the consumption thus suppressed was converted into saving, which became another channel of consumers’ income. First, surplus income had no investment channel apart from saving; only saving could still bring a partial increment of income. Second, because capital was severely scarce at the time, the interest rate on saving was relatively high, and even limited income could generate incremental cash flow; this became an important reason for residents to save. Third, apart from meeting daily consumption outlays, households also had to face the potential rigid large expenditures discussed above, and this part of payment could be accomplished only out of saving. Although interest on saving in the 1960s was relatively low, because surplus money could not be converted into real purchasing power, and given the cumulative character of interest income, interest income was nevertheless considerable. If we calculate on the basis of an average wage of 600 yuan and an average interest rate of 10 per cent, and in accordance with the basic relationship between income and consumption set out above, so that every year half of income becomes saving, then in the first year there would be 30 yuan of income, and thereafter the amount would increase year by year as the base of deposits grows. This is only the case of a single employed worker in a household; if several members of a household all have fixed wage income, the amount would be larger still. This explicit interest income from saving was therefore also an important component part of households’ explicit income.

Apart from the two important aspects above, explicit income had almost no other channels; at the same time, income from these two aspects was stable and could be anticipated, and may be regarded as “risk-free income”. Moreover, because the mode of employment in the era of the planned economy was special — that is, so long as one participated in employment one could obtain the income of the corresponding grade — this became another important institutional guarantee that income was smooth and low-risk. The stability and low risk of the cash flow of income became the basis on which the cash-flow consumption model of Chinese households was formed. Besides this, another important factor that lowered income risk and formed the cash-flow consumption model was “implicit income”, namely the social security in housing, medical care, and other aspects. These aspects also played an important role in the operation of the cash model, to the extent that dismantling this security might lead to a transformation of the consumption model.

3.1.2 Implicit Income from Housing Subsidies and from Social Security

In the period of the planned economy, housing in China was built by the government and the work unit, and was used by households free of charge or at an extremely low rent, with no time limit prescribed for its use. Under this quantity rationing, in the absence of private property rights, housing expenditure accounted for a very low proportion of total household expenditure, and the stability of the system also guaranteed the stability of labourers’ expectations. This system of rationing housing by quantity therefore in fact provided households with an anticipatable and stable implicit income. This implicit income likewise became an important reason for the formation of the cash-flow consumption model.

Around the time of liberation, China’s population was about 400 million, while the total housing floor area of the whole country was only 270 million square metres. Nevertheless, in view of the economic and military situation of the time, the central authorities put forward the development strategy of “production first, life second”, so that at that time residential housing construction lagged seriously behind. After three years of restorative construction, China began formally to carry out industrialisation in 1953, which required a thorough transformation of the cities, and the corresponding land and real estate became important objects of that transformation. The state nationalised urban housing and land by such means as redemption and the payment of rents to house owners, which established the institutional foundation of public ownership of housing in China’s era of the planned economy. Between 1950 and 1957, the proportion of funds used for housing construction investment in China averaged 9.83 per cent. Over a considerable length of time, neither the housing system nor labourers’ expectations of the housing system underwent a fundamental change: that is, the system of publicly owned housing would continue, and individuals neither needed to nor could purchase housing by cash or other financial means.

Because of its character, this implicit subsidy income is not counted in household income, but when we comprehensively examine the economic influence of the system of publicly owned housing on households we cannot neglect this point. This risk-free, or low-risk, implicit housing subsidy should therefore properly become an important component part when households calculate their income. This was equivalent to increasing households’ income and potential consumption capacity (although actual consumption capacity must still take into account the level of cash income, it cannot be denied that this increase in certainty gave households implicit subsidies in other respects). Although we cannot strictly depict this situation through data, it does indeed exist. These factors all played an important role in the formation of the cash-flow consumption model. Besides this, China’s traditional conception of the household, and the personnel structure of the household, are also an important foundation for the formation and maintenance of the cash-flow consumption model.

3.2 The Holism of the Household (the household as an integrated unit) and Scale Effects under Economic and Traditional Constraints

Fisher, in his celebrated work The Theory of Interest, said that “income is the starting point and the end point of economics”. The household, and not the individual, is the basic component of Chinese society; this striking feature determines that the “household as a whole” is the micro starting point and end point for analysing China’s economic problems. This dissertation discusses the household within the scope of economics, but it still needs to take social and cultural factors into account. In Chinese society, the rearing and love of the elder generation for the younger and the filial respect of the younger for the elder are important media binding household members closely together, and the force of these media is so strong that virtually everyone acts in this way. Some relatively distinctive economic phenomena have thus arisen in China. When children marry, for example, both sides need to produce a certain sum of money to meet the costs of housing and furniture, and even to give the woman’s side a “bride price” as economic compensation. This expense is so large that many households need to begin saving from the moment a child is born in order to prepare for this need. China’s traditional morality requires parents to bear complete responsibility for rearing and educating their children, so when a new generation is born, outlays on rearing and educating children are also relatively large. Conversely, the economic support of the previous generation by the later generation also constitutes an important item of expenditure for the labourers within the household. This tradition deeply influences the economic behaviour of Chinese households: whether in the era of the planned economy or in the process of marketization, the conception of the household as a whole has always been valued. The key role of this conception in household economic behaviour is that the economic behaviour of the individual never takes the individual as its starting point and final purpose; the household as a whole is always the object of optimisation.

As the marketization process deepens, this “holism” has begun to come under impact — the free migration of the labour factor that economic development requires, for example, has an influence on household holism — but the mutual economic interdependence and help among household members has not disappeared; on the contrary, in housing, old-age provision, medical care, and other aspects, mutual support among members is still the most important means. The impact of the economy on household holism is also expressed in the shrinking of household size; moreover, household holism appears more readily when the economy is underdeveloped and the population birth rate is high.

First, if the total income of the household is relatively low, then the more labouring population there is, the greater the probability of raising total income, and vice versa. Secondly, according to Becker’s theory, a child is also an economic good; when the cost of rearing is not high and the potential return may be very large, having more children and expanding the size of the household is an act of optimisation. Chinese households of that time were therefore relatively “large”, and “three-generation households”, and even “four-generation households”, were widespread. With economic development, household size gradually decreases; at the same point in time, moreover, household size in economically developed regions is smaller than in economically underdeveloped regions. The lowering of the natural rate of population growth also became an objective reason for the shrinking of household size. The logic of the shrinking of household size does not, however, contradict the optimisation of household holism: with the increase of only-child households and the ageing of the population, this characteristic of the household shows itself ever more clearly, most obviously in the structure and mode of expenditure. A reduction in the number of children, for example, requires a corresponding rise in quality, so expenditure on rearing and educating children increases; the resolution of the problem of old-age provision likewise needs the help of the later generation. Reality of this kind on the contrary strengthens the holism of the household.

The existence and continuation of this characteristic of holism in Chinese households can also be explained in economic terms. Under the condition that individual income is very low, living alone faces rising risk; out of considerations of safety, an increase in household members also raises total income and lowers risk. If the problems of inheritance and support are taken into account, the “altruism” between generations can be regarded as a “transaction”, except that this transaction takes place inside the household and does not display an explicit price. A network of insurance of this kind, mutually provided among household members, is small in scale but the risk of insurance failure is small. Under these conditions, the expansion of household size is beneficial for enlarging the insurance network and providing insurance of a higher level. If the social insurance system were sound enough to replace this network formed among household members, it could reasonably be inferred that household size would decrease.

The discussion above of the characteristics of Chinese households and of the structure of income characteristics is intended to show one aspect of the foundation on which the cash-flow consumption model was formed: namely, that income was limited and stable, the coverage of social security was broad, and the characteristic of household holism was prominent. These aspects influence one another and are cause and effect to one another, and it is for this reason that the corresponding “consumption model” could be formed. On the other hand, apart from the influence of income, the formation of a consumption model also involves the effect of expenditure. The content and structure of expenditure of Chinese households are also an important inducement in the formation of the cash consumption model, and the upgrading of consumption from subsistence consumption to enjoyment consumption became one of the motive forces of the transformation of the consumption model.

3.3 The Content of Expenditure under the Cash-Flow Model

In discussing the structure of expenditure, it must be emphasised that before the economic transition the system of the planned economy practised strict quantity constraints, so that the factor with the greatest influence upon households in consumption and other payments was not money and prices but quantity. This dissertation divides the content of household consumption into two large parts: “basic subsistence consumption expenditure” and “luxury expenditure”. The former mainly includes outlays on food, clothing, and daily life, while large luxury expenditure includes expensive products conforming to the characteristics of their era, as well as rigid expenditure within the life cycle, such as expenditure on weddings, funerals, and marriages.

3.3.1 Basic Subsistence Expenditure

Basic subsistence expenditure refers to the means of life a household requires to maintain its most basic daily subsistence needs, such as those used for food, clothing, and daily requirements. Above a certain level of social development there always exists an average level of subsistence consumption; this consumption level can guarantee the most basic material consumption of the members of society, and as society progresses and the economy develops, this level rises accordingly. Under the condition of limited income, this basic consumption accounts for the great majority of expenditure. Whether in terms of the total amount of saving or the propensity to save, therefore, Chinese households were long in a state of cash-flow shortage. According to the estimate of Zang Xuheng (1994), before reform and opening, durable consumption for subsistence (including food) accounted for more than 90 per cent of income; at the same time, because cash-flow shortage caused residents’ saving level to be low, under conditions lacking effective financial markets and financial instruments, households’ rigid large expenditures came to depend still more on saving, and this formed a “vicious circle” of the cash-flow consumption model on the consumption side: the cycle before large assets entered the household was prolonged, and consumption levels in this period were very low. The direct cause of this vicious circle was that the prices of luxury consumption were too high, and its most fundamental cause was cash-flow shortage. This problem could in principle have been completely solved by financial means, but the extreme backwardness of the financial environment directed at individuals in the period of the planned economy and the early stage of marketization reform thoroughly consolidated the cash-flow consumption model.

3.3.2 Large-Scale Consumption and Luxury Expenditure

What proportion, in the end, do large consumption expenditures and rigid expenditures occupy in the structure of expenditure of Chinese households? What, in the end, does a household need to do in order to complete this payment process? This dissertation answers the questions above by adducing some data and materials.

The large rigid expenditures spoken of here are such expenses as those required for “weddings, funerals, and marriages”, and the additional outlays needed to meet emergencies such as illness and accidents; luxury expenditure has different content in each era, and refers mainly to expenditure on durable consumer goods not required for daily life. At the beginning of the founding of New China, watches, bicycles, and sewing machines were typical luxury outlays; around the period of reform and opening, refrigerators, colour televisions, and washing machines were luxury outlays, and today this content has been transformed again into automobiles and privately owned housing. Luxury outlays not only express people’s demand to raise the quality of life and reflect the present state of economic development at the time, but can also reflect the consumption model of China’s consumers at the time through the mode of purchase and the quantities held of these products.

From the records in Shanghai’s local gazetteers of light industrial products produced in Shanghai, we can roughly arrange the development of some products and their connection with household expenditure. Apart from watches, the bicycle, as another important luxury consumer good — especially as a product usable by all members of the household — had an importance and practicality higher than the watch, and the level and changes of its price also influenced households’ saving and consumption behaviour at all times. Combining the data on residents’ income and saving, it can be discovered that the only route to purchasing these commodities was saving, and that the larger the household size, the easier this was to achieve, because a larger total population means a larger total income. On the other hand, under the influence of Chinese traditional culture, the purchase of these commodities usually took place at the time of marriage, so that after these consumer goods were combined with traditional customs they gradually became “rigid”, or at least “low-elasticity”, expenditure. In the end, this expenditure became a stage in the household’s life-cycle circulation that could not be surmounted, and the vast majority of households strove to save in order to cross this stage as early as possible. Many interesting institutional arrangements inside the household also arose from this — the whole family saving first for the eldest child, or for the child who marries earliest, and so on in sequence, for example. From another aspect, this economic behaviour encouraged the expansion of household size and the consolidation of the characteristic of household holism. More importantly, because China’s level of financial development at the time was not high and there were no financing instruments directed at individual consumers, when a household had a financing need the most frequent route was to save in advance and finance itself, or to obtain financing among relatives and friends. This strong financing constraint also became another important latent motive force for households to increase saving in order to guard against large outlays. It may be said that households’ low consumption level and high saving rate were not a simple phenomenon, but rational behaviour under various constraints; only by analysing and transforming these constraints is it possible to raise consumption demand.

3.4 The Institutional Conditions under Which the “Cash-Flow” Consumption Model Was Formed

The formation of the cash-flow consumption model was constrained not only by economic conditions, but also by the various institutional constraints of the time. The quantity rationing system, for example, severely restricted residents’ consumption capacity, and forced the cash flow they had to be converted into saving to provide funds for industrial development; a financial system developed mainly for industrial enterprises had almost no financial business directed at individuals apart from saving, so a lack of financial instruments also caused consumers to depend still further on saving to smooth risk; the population management model of urban–rural division severely restricted the free movement of labour resources, and at the same time, because the private operation of enterprises was prohibited, consumers’ modes of employment were single, which further caused the income structure to be single and its level not high; finally, because institutional reasons negated private ownership of assets, residents had no demand in respect of asset holding — especially no demand in respect of ownership of houses — so that housing, whose value as household property is the highest in a market economy environment, possessed only a consumption function, without an asset-income function or investment value, which also from another side impelled the formation of the cash-flow consumption model.

3.4.1 The Rationing System under the Shortage Economy

The implementation of rationing of products was one of the most basic economic systems China practised after entering the construction of the primary stage of socialism; within it, control over articles for daily use extended to every aspect — apart from daily articles such as grain coupons, cooking-oil coupons, cloth coupons, and non-staple food coupons, which needed to be purchased with money together with the coupons, large items such as bicycles also needed to be purchased against coupons. The formation of such a coupon society was not only because China’s economic aggregate was low at the time and the economy was developed and industrialised under the guiding thought of the “catch-up strategy”; more importantly, the household registration system of urban–rural division and the system of unified state purchase and marketing of commodities served as its foundation, so that this quantity rationing could operate effectively.

Facts prove that suppressing consumption and developing industry through the accumulation from agricultural production did indeed set China quickly on the road of industrialisation, and the output of various industrial products also rose rapidly, but the people’s standard of living obviously did not obtain a corresponding rise throughout. The various coupons survived for different periods, existed in different ways, and existed at different levels; it was not until 1992 that the coupon system throughout the country thoroughly withdrew from the historical stage as the economy developed — a span of fully 40 years in between.

3.4.2 The Level of Development of the Financial Environment

From the early period after the founding of the country until the early 1990s, when the marketization process deepened further, China’s financial environment was simple: that is, the state monopolised finance and a single banking system was practised. Such an institutional arrangement was still closely connected with the strategy of economic catch-up laid down by the leaders of the time, and was also connected with China’s level of economic development at the time. In the early period after the founding of the country, the nature of the state determined that the government had to mobilise all resources to promote and decide the direction of economic development according to the government’s will; the establishment of a single financial system with the People’s Bank at its core was therefore very beneficial for concentrating and mobilising financial resources to support industrialisation, and to serve the catch-up strategy. Complete control over the financial system enabled the state to control the production surplus and the rate of accumulation and to provide sufficient capital for heavy industry, and on the other hand also enabled it to concentrate the scattered idle funds in society into the state’s hands, to serve industrial production.

This centralised financial system avoided the risk of resource scarcity when, at a not-high level of economic development, large amounts of resources are mobilised to develop one part of the economy in a concentrated way. This financial system, however, completely neglected individual consumers’ demand for financial instruments, and individuals were entirely under financial repression. When income was low and there were no surplus commodities to consume, saving was the only channel of capital operation that generated a return. Although China practised a system of high welfare and generalised welfare in that period, and apart from basic consumption there was no need for excessive expenditure in other aspects of security, this still restricted residents’ consumption demand, and also formed the habit of Chinese residents preferring saving and depending on saving.

3.4.3 Restrictions of the Household Registration System on Modes of Employment

The household registration system is a special population management system China still uses today. In the more than 60 years from the founding of the country to the present, the development of the household registration system has passed through roughly three stages, characterised by a movement from free movement to strict control and then to gradual relaxation.

The first stage ran from the early period after the founding of the country to 1957, during which China did not restrict citizens’ free migration and movement, and did not manage or control citizens’ status.

The second stage ran from 1958 to 1978, during which household registration management was characterised by strict control of the urban population, and the dual household registration system of urban–rural division took shape. The main cause of this major transformation of the population system was the free movement of population in the early period after the founding of the country: as the urban economy gradually recovered, the rural population began gradually to flow into the cities and form “drifters”, which influenced the normal order of economic construction, to the extent that the state repeatedly discouraged this blind movement. In 1959 the national urban population was 120 million; one year later it had reached 130 million; taking 1957 as the base period, within three years the urban population grew by 33 per cent. At this time China’s industrialisation construction had only just started, and the funds construction required came almost entirely from the agricultural surplus; reducing the size of the cities and artificially depressing consumption demand therefore became the urbanisation strategy serving industrial construction. Under the original population system, however, citizens possessing urban household registration could enjoy rationing of commodity grain, and real interests made it the rational choice for the rural population to enter the cities. The large-scale flow of the rural population to the cities led consumption to increase too fast, so that the process of industrialisation risked slowing down. On the other hand, the reduction of the agricultural population caused a shortage of agricultural labour and a slowing of the speed of production. Under the action of these two unfavourable factors, and against the background of the state’s catch-up strategy goal and industrialisation, China began to implement strict household registration management. In December 1961 China began to register “the number of non-agricultural population households and persons”; from then on, “agricultural household registration” and “non-agricultural household registration” became labels accompanying the Chinese people.

The third stage runs from 1979 to the present; in this stage, although the household registration system of urban–rural division still exists, it has begun to loosen. As China’s national strength has increased and the speed of urbanisation has quickened, this household registration management system of dual urban–rural division has gradually become an unfavourable factor for economic development.

While China’s distinctive household registration system of this kind strictly restricted the movement of population, it naturally also restricted modes of employment. Rural labourers could obtain income only through agricultural production and could not be employed in the cities; under the conditions that agricultural product prices were controlled by the state and unified purchase and marketing was practised, these people had almost no other route to obtain additional income. On the other hand, the urban population could also be employed only in its own “work unit” and obtain income; apart from this there were no other routes to employment and to obtaining income. If this mode of population management can be said to have suited the needs of China’s economic development at the time, and its rigid and single mode of employment to have conformed to the characteristics of the planned economy, then this mode also strictly restricted the effective allocation of labour, and restricted the capacity of some people to raise their income and at the same time raise their consumption. The evolution of China’s household registration system is closely connected with the level of economic development and with development strategy; as economic strength has gradually increased, this system has gradually shown its insufficiencies — especially at present, when China’s gross domestic product has already broken through 30 trillion yuan and the urbanisation process is gradually accelerating. Breaking this restriction on population movement, while letting various modes of employment coexist and letting labourers obtain as much income as possible, is greatly beneficial for raising China’s currently relatively low consumption demand.

3.4.4 The Influence of Constraints of the Property-Rights System on Residents’ Consumption Behaviour

Property rights are the bundle of rights of possession, use, benefit, and disposal over an object; these rights possess value in the market, and when the rights are traded in the market a price is generated; it may be said that the modern market economy is a market economy of property-rights transactions. For property rights to be traded smoothly and generate benefit, two basic conditions must be present: first, the recognition of private property rights; second, clear definition of property rights.

In the early period after the founding of the country, public ownership was practised throughout the country — that is, the legitimacy of private property rights was negated at the level of the state system, and all rights belonged to the state in public ownership. In respect of factors, peasants did not truly own the land; the land belonged to the state, and peasants only cultivated it and obtained remuneration through some concrete form of sharing. It may be said that this was a de facto system of “tenancy”. In the cities, workers living on wages in fact did not possess complete property rights over their own labour power either. Although workers had personal freedom, because there was no marketised labour market, individuals with special talents who might have obtained higher income could not obtain corresponding income within the strict hierarchy, and changing occupation was still less possible, so the low efficiency of labour was seen everywhere. This inefficient allocation of human resources induced shortage from another side, which led in turn to low worker income and a low consumption rate. Seen from the side of enterprises, because all of an enterprise’s assets belonged to the state, the factory director or manager was only an agent of the superior level; more importantly, this agency relationship was not of one layer, but of delegation and agency layer upon layer from the centre to the localities, and this form of agency could only lead to serious information asymmetry and thus to the low efficiency of enterprise managers. Under this system, enterprises only took orders from the state’s plan and could do nothing about possible profit opportunities they discovered, which obviously seriously influenced the efficiency of the use of capital. At the same time, enterprises also had to bear more of workers’ medical and old-age expenditure, and even had to run hospitals and schools in order to satisfy workers’ needs and embody the superiority of socialist public ownership. For enterprises, with low efficiency on the one hand and heavy burdens on the other, this public ownership generated no positive effect in respect of enterprise production and capital efficiency.

The negation of private property rights was expressed not only in the low efficiency of the use of land, labour, and capital; at a deeper level it negated the functions of the market in pricing and allocating resources. The modern economic viewpoint holds that under certain conditions the market can accomplish the allocation of resources and satisfy the requirements of efficiency. Under public ownership, no person or institution could clearly define the property rights of goods or resources — apart from marking them “publicly owned”. The result of resources being unable to obtain efficient allocation is therefore low production efficiency; its influence on enterprises is low profit, and on individuals low income; and low income in turn determines a low consumption rate. It may be said that the influence of the negation of private property rights on the consumption rate, although not direct, is nevertheless profound.

3.5 The Formation and Characteristics of the “Cash-Flow Consumption Model”

3.5.1 The Meaning of the Cash-Flow Consumption Model

Through the exposition of China’s system of planned economy before reform and opening and of the nearly 20 years of the later period of reform and opening, it is already basically possible to reach the following conclusions: (1) the consumption structure of Chinese households has not undergone a “leap”, but still unfolds along the path of gradually improving living conditions; (2) the causes of the formation of consumption hot spots are many, and are connected with the level of income, the degree of industrialisation, the level of financial development, and so on; (3) the financial instruments for converting potential consumption into real consumption are severely lacking, forcing households to raise saving and to adopt private borrowing to complete consumption so as to maintain or catch up with the consumption hot spots of the time. On the basis of the conclusions above, the author holds that research on the transformation of China’s consumption model cannot, first, depart from the macroeconomic reality of China’s gradual transition; secondly, analysis of consumption should first attend to the constraints on consumption, such as income, employment, and the mode of growth; and finally, it should devote itself to analysing how to develop consumption financial instruments suited to China in order to promote the conversion of potential consumption into real consumption.

Because the cash consumption model is a synthesis of the three research directions above, this section examines the consumption model of China’s consumers and the characteristics of the consumption market in this period. The most striking characteristics of the cash consumption model are the following: (1) income is low relative to commodity prices; (2) financial market development is deficient, the financial instruments usable to promote consumption are lacking, and “borrowing constraints” exist widely; (3) the objects of consumption are ordinary commodities rather than investment goods or luxury goods; (4) household income sources are single. Given the characteristics above, this society’s consumption model tends to develop into what this dissertation calls the “cash-flow type”.

Figure 3-2: Diagram of the cash-flow consumption model

[Figure image: image72.png]

The diagram is a flow chart of the model: employment generates income for the household, and the household’s income is divided between basic subsistence consumption and saving; saving earns interest, which flows back into income, and saving provides the financing for non-basic consumption.

The cash-flow type of consumption model is the simplest consumption model: income is obtained through employment, and then, after basic subsistence consumption has been satisfied, the demand for consumption at a higher level is satisfied through saving. In the cycle of income and expenditure, the consumption subject has no investment channel apart from saving with which to increase income. The characteristics of this consumption model have a natural matching quality with the economic development demands and the level of economic development of China’s period of planned economy.

First, in the period of the planned economy, urban–rural division formed an artificial dual economic system; within the cities, employment, labour, old-age provision, medical care, and education were all undertaken by the state and the enterprise work unit, so income and social security were basically equal and without risk — while at the same time this equalisation also meant that the income level was low. Secondly, because China’s level of economic development at the time was not high, and the catch-up strategy was implemented, the great majority of the state’s funds were used on heavy industrialisation, so the means of basic subsistence were scarce, which severely suppressed consumers’ consumption demands; but the result was that basic consumption was very easily satisfied, after which there was basically no surplus income, or the surplus income was entirely converted into saving. After China entered the marketization process, the factor markets were gradually opened up and the manner in which labourers obtain income underwent an essential change; but because the starting point of Chinese household consumption was low, although household income increased obviously, this manner of depending on saving to purchase durable consumer goods did not change. Thirdly, although China had embarked on the market track, because the catch-up strategy was still to a large extent dominant, developing an export-oriented economy by relying on cheap labour costs became China’s macroeconomic strategy. This development model, however, emphasised the expansion of external consumption markets to earn foreign exchange, rather than the invigoration of domestic consumption and the development of the internal economic cycle; so over a very long time China’s enterprise profits, fiscal revenue, and foreign exchange reserves all rose substantially, but domestic consumption never became the main driving factor of economic growth. Most importantly, the distribution of income between labour and capital was unbalanced, and the steadily increasing fiscal revenue also did little in the redistribution of income, so domestic consumption remained sluggish throughout. This also became the reason why, after China entered marketization reform, the consumption model of Chinese households still underwent no substantive change.

3.5.2 The Instability Internal to the Cash Consumption Model

The cash consumption model depends mainly on the household’s own saving to accomplish payment financing, which indicates that between the beginning of a consumption plan and the completion of payment for the commodity there exists a time lag; the length of the time lag depends on household income, commodity prices, and the convenience of financing channels. What is more important is that after a payment is completed, the household’s saving is exhausted; after one consumption peak appears, the household will for a long time lose the capacity for large consumption, and during this time the household will reduce consumption and increase saving in preparation for the next consumption peak. Thus, naturally, at the household level the consumption path corresponding to the cash consumption model will present obvious pulse-like fluctuations: this is the instability of the cash consumption model.

Figure 3-3: The consumption path of the cash-flow consumption model

[Figure image: image73.wmf]

The diagram plots consumption C against time T: the consumption path runs flat at a low level and rises in three sharp, evenly spaced spikes, dropping vertically back to the baseline after each peak.

For different households, the time at which the consumption peak appears and the content of consumption may both differ: for young people, for example, housing and children’s education may be the consumption peak; for the elderly, old-age provision and medical care are the more urgent payment content. For consumption of automobiles and household appliances, whose prices are relatively high, the time between two peaks may be somewhat shorter; but for housing, whose price is very high, the interval will be very long, and during this interval the consumption rate will remain at a low level. Seen from the macroeconomic level, differences in household structure cause, at one and the same point in time, consumption demands of various types to arise — one part of households needs to buy food, one part needs to buy housing and automobiles, and another part needs to raise its level of medical care. At this level, therefore, the final effect of the superposition of peaks and troughs may not have the strong fluctuation depicted in the figure above; but from a realistic standpoint, the consumption demands of households in a society still have basic trends and basic content. As in China now, the payment peaks almost all households face are the same — mainly housing, old-age provision, and medical care — so the superposition of these micro subjects’ consumption demands will necessarily form still stronger fluctuations.

This instability internal to the cash consumption model is expressed at the micro level as the result of the mutual interaction among the household’s concrete consumption plan, its income, and the prices of its consumption goals; at the macro level it is expressed as economic fluctuation — especially when the proportion of households of the same type is too high, still more violent fluctuation will be provoked in respect of some consumption content. We know that fluctuation is a deficiency of the market economy that needs to be overcome in the practice of economic growth, because repeated fluctuations, especially uncontrollable and unforeseeable fluctuations, often increase the uncertainty of the system, and the existence of this uncertainty will undoubtedly increase social costs. Within the system of the planned economy, owing to the dual action of quantity rationing and the low level of consumption, this fluctuation did not display strong instability at the macroeconomic level; but after 1978, as the rationing system disappeared and the consumption structure was upgraded, the appearance of consumption peaks for specific products often brought about the result that the speed of economic growth first increased and then declined, and apart from this caused inflation within periods of flourishing consumption. A stable monetary background and stable relations of supply and demand, which the market economy requires, therefore need solutions to be sought not only from the aspects of industry and money, but also a breakthrough to be sought from the aspects of consumption and of the consumption model.

3.5.3 The Cash Consumption Model Is Not Conducive to the Sustained Expansion of Domestic Demand

When a society’s consumption is expressed mainly as the cash consumption model, besides the disadvantages above, the problem of insufficient consumption domestic demand also arises. The basic diagram of the cash consumption model is as above; between two consumption peaks there then exists a relatively long time during which the level of consumption is at a very low level. One reason for this is that income itself is low, so that what can be used for consumption is not much; another is that in order to satisfy the next consumption goal a certain proportion must be taken from limited income for saving, and this must continue for a relatively long time.

In the period of the planned economy, the state of Chinese households could basically be depicted by the diagram above: although households had no worries in housing, education, medical care, and old-age provision, which were fully insured by the state, large consumption apart from these still faced stage-based character of this kind. Those who lived through that era should all know that the luxury consumer goods represented by watches, bicycles, and radios were not something a household could purchase and pay for directly; in order to buy one of these products it was a universal phenomenon for the whole family to economise on food and clothing and increase saving, so that during this period consumption not only could not rise but might even fall, because whatever consumption path was chosen, the total amount of payment in the end still came from income. The consumption level of the trough between the two peaks of the fluctuating consumption path is therefore certainly lower than the consumption level of a smooth consumption path.

Figure 3-4: Comparison of a smooth consumption path and a fluctuating consumption path

[Figure image: image74.wmf]

The diagram superimposes two paths on the same C–T axes: a dashed horizontal line representing the smooth consumption path, and the solid, three-spiked line of the fluctuating consumption path, whose peaks rise above the smooth path and whose troughs run along below it.

On the other hand, an important characteristic of the cash consumption model is the lack of financial instruments usable for intertemporal payment, so the fluctuation not only exists but cannot be eliminated. Credit, mortgage loans, financial leasing, and the like are all financial instruments able to “cut the peaks and fill the troughs” in time; but under the system of the planned economy, the limitations on consumption choice, the non-recognition of private property rights, the one-sided understanding of finance, and the almost non-existent risk and uncertainty gave these instruments simply no space and no necessity in which to survive. Under the conditions of a market economy, however, the situation is greatly different. Although income has risen significantly, the large consumer goods households face are not limited to bicycles but include automobiles, housing, and so on, so the situation households face has no essential difference from the problem under the system of the planned economy. The difference is that the market economy, which dislikes fluctuation, does not itself supply households sufficiently with the financial instruments usable to improve the consumption path, and the conditions and environment in which these financial instruments exist are also not yet present; China’s consumption domestic demand therefore hovered at a low level for a very long time, while household saving displayed strong growth under all conditions. To change the situation of insufficient consumption domestic demand in China, therefore, setting out only from the direction of stimulating consumption is open to question; setting out instead from the angles of income, the prices of the objects of consumption, and the availability of financial instruments might make it possible to find a breakthrough. The fundamental change in the consumption environment brought by the fundamental transformation of the economic system is an important basis for the transformation of the consumption model; considering the expansion of consumption domestic demand only within the sphere of consumption is not the correct direction for solving the problem of insufficient consumption.

If, in the “income–expenditure” cycle, the object of analysis is the household, some aspects of still greater implication can be obtained. First, several generations of the household appear as a whole; according to the analysis above, this mode of household organisation is economical and optimised. Secondly, under the system of the planned economy, the employment of household members was low-risk; further, under the condition that wage grades were also delimited by the state according to standards, the total amount of labour income may be said to have been basically risk-free. Thirdly, because total income was on the low side, the great majority of the household’s only cash income had to be spent on basic subsistence consumption, as can be seen from the Engel coefficients of Chinese residents at the time. Fourthly, under the constraint of the quantitative rationing system, and under the condition that the prices of non-basic consumer goods were high relative to income, households necessarily converted their only cash surplus into saving: on the one hand they could obtain a part of interest income, and on the other they could provide the only financing channel for the high-priced non-basic consumer goods. Finally, why do households not give up non-basic consumption? Seen from the content of non-basic consumption, these consumer goods are in fact not “non-basic”, but are closely connected with daily life; it was only because the state laid stress on developing heavy industry that the supply of light industrial products was small and their prices high, so that they became “luxury goods” in the sense of a low level of economic development. On the other hand, as mentioned in the theoretical part above, Chinese households attach great importance to the several rigid stage-based processes they must face in the life cycle, such as marriage, the rearing of descendants, and the support of the elderly. These links are also the times when household outlays increase substantially; under the conditions of low income together with large outlays, saving in advance in order to pass smoothly through these stages became an important reason for households’ efforts to save.

Summarising this consumption model by means of the balance sheet and the cash-flow statement, the balance sheet of Chinese households will present the special form of having no liabilities:

Seen from the angle of the account books, therefore, the property and the income and expenditure of Chinese households basically do not need to be summarised by a balance sheet, because the liabilities item is empty. Such an asset structure means that households do not need at all to consider asset profit and loss, investment portfolios and risk, or asset combination problems; they need only guarantee that the cash-flow statement is healthy. Under the cash-flow model, therefore, the economic events households care most about are employment, changes in income, the prices of rigid expenditure, and the total amount of saving. Under this consumption model, various distinctive phenomena arise: limited consumption scale because income is limited, consumption depending on saving, fluctuation of consumption expenditure, and so on.

Although from the angle of aggregates China’s saving and per capita saving were very small, both in total amount and relative to the proportion of GDP, combining the prices of household consumption structure and large expenditure discussed above, households converted almost all of the cash flow remaining after basic consumption into saving. This cash-flow type of consumption model and dependence on saving of Chinese households were therefore not formed by the so-called frugality tradition of the Chinese, but a rational, optimised consumption model under the conditions of aggregate constraints and structural constraints. Does this “stage-based character” at the macroeconomic level also have a realistic foundation at the micro level, and is this stage-based character closely connected with the cash-flow consumption model? How, then, is this stage-based character at the micro household level to be defined? And how are the macroeconomic phenomena to be explained?

3.5.5 Microeconomic Manifestations of the Formation of the Cash-Flow Consumption Model

The “stage-based character” defined in this dissertation refers to the existence, within the household life cycle, of low-elasticity large expenditures that differ at different points in time, and, under the cash-flow consumption model, to the dependence for these expenditures mainly on saving. Between the two events of having no saving and saving enough to pay these expenditures there therefore exists a certain time lag, which generates the “stage-based character” of expenditure. If the speed of saving could match the natural timing of households’ large expenditure, or be faster than it, no abnormality would appear in the whole consumption model; but if the speed of saving is slower than it, the household will certainly be unable to complete the stage-based tasks. Under the condition of rational expectations, households will depress basic consumption in advance and increase saving, in order to satisfy the requirement of the total saving goal. Under the system of the planned economy, because there was no more cash available for saving and basic consumption was already very low — sufficient only to meet subsistence — potential consumption demand was suppressed: this is the so-called “consumption suppression” of the shortage economy. There are many preconditions for the existence of this stage-based expenditure, such as low income, a subsistence-type consumption structure, and a high saving rate; among them must also be included the rigidity, or low elasticity, of large expenditure. For Chinese households, the several large expenditures enumerated in the figure above are all rigid demands, and the points in time at which these demands occur are closely connected with the changes of the individual and household life cycle.

At the same time, the low consumption rate is a striking characteristic of this model of stage-based expenditure, and the influence of this model on household consumption behaviour makes households attend to cash flow and neglect balance-sheet relations. In the era of the planned economy before the economic transition, because of the constraints of the property-rights system, households had no concept of private assets or investment goods, so when calculating and optimising their own consumption behaviour they did not consider the division of income between consumption and investment. This caused households to lose the means of smoothing the consumption path, and to lose the possibility of “cutting the peaks and levelling the troughs”. Under the conditions of limited total income and rigid large expenditure, increasing saving and constructing insurance inside the household is rational behaviour. On the other hand, this model of consumption expenditure displays relatively strong stability. Because employment and wage income were stable, household members had no unemployment risk, and the disappearance of risk lowered precautionary saving; secondly, because there was no asset market, the more variable asset prices and returns could not influence households’ optimising behaviour, and there was no need, because of fluctuations in asset prices, continually to adjust the proportions between consumption and investment, so consumption displayed an abnormally smooth characteristic across all periods, which also played a positive role in stabilising the macro economy. It must be pointed out, however, that because the level of cash flow was extremely low, the stability of this consumption model guaranteed the stability of the existence of the stage-based character, and did not level out the stage-based character!

It may therefore be said that stage-based expenditure, the cash-flow consumption model, and the low consumption rate stimulate one another and are cause and effect to one another. It was precisely because of the external constraints of economic aggregates and of systems that households gradually formed this cash-flow type of consumption model of depending on saving and having a relatively low level of consumption. This model was adapted to China’s specific macroeconomic environment at the time — or rather, the specific aggregate created the specific individual. If the aggregate undergoes a major change, the individual will inevitably change with it. When China entered the path of marketization, the macroeconomic constraints did undergo a qualitative change, and the economic behaviour, including consumption behaviour, of households and individual consumers necessarily changed as well. Although the effects of marketization are very obvious, this process is also slow; many constraints cannot all be relaxed at once into complete marketization, especially in an era when the global economic pattern is also changing profoundly. The process of transformation of the cash consumption model therefore likewise displays an obvious gradual character.
