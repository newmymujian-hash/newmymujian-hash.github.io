Acknowledgements

Although two months still remain before I bid a final farewell to my student days, I already have the air of a working man rather than of a student. In these circumstances, the thought that the dissertation defence soon to come will formally, and for the last time, divide the long road of my life into a student era and a non-student era fills me, involuntarily, with emotion. In this transitional period in which the two join together, I go on savouring the freedom of the student while also experiencing the busy life of the working man. As the last portion of a doctoral dissertation, and as a summing-up of my student days — of my years of doctoral study in particular — these brief thousand-odd words are required to carry far too much, just like this transitional period of two months.

Looking back over three years of study, they have truly galloped past like a white colt glimpsed through a crack in the wall! I now ask myself in what respects I have grown, and the answer is just four simple things: listening, speaking, reading, and writing! What I listen to has passed from pinyin to lectures in English; what I speak, from constructing sentences to a dissertation defence; what I read, from textbooks to abstruse monographs; and what I write, from essays of a few hundred words to the lengthy treatise of today. These changes are not merely an enrichment of my stock of knowledge: after a process like that of bees brewing honey, quantitative change has gradually turned into qualitative change, until in the end all my knowledge has gradually shed its disorder and confusion and begun to take on structure and layers, and new knowledge arriving later can always find its proper home in my mind. More importantly, this structure of knowledge has begun to acquire a clear direction — the seeking of truth and the seeking of freedom! This, I think, is the way in which a trained mind treats information and knowledge. Whenever I confront a new problem, my mind begins actively to mobilise its reserves of knowledge, and always hopes to express the result by the most reasonable method available. Hegel said that “method is not an external form, but the soul of the content”; so one’s own particular method of listening, speaking, reading, and writing is the soul both of one’s existing stock of knowledge and of the new knowledge one will accept in the future. Only at this moment have I profoundly understood the influence that one’s “world outlook” and “outlook on values” exercise over a person: these two outlooks are the oxygen in which an individual’s structure of knowledge exists and develops.

I am not a person of outstanding natural gifts; that I have come as far as today rests entirely upon earnestness and solid, steady work, and my supervisor’s final appraisal of me — “diligent” — could hardly be more fitting! With this one word, three years of doctoral study are enough! My supervisor, Professor Mao Zhenhua, is a famous Chinese entrepreneur, yet one senses no pomposity in him. I particularly remember one occasion, during an interval in a conference, when he ate a boxed meal in his office and then hurried straight back to the meeting room to go on chairing the proceedings; it doubled my respect for him — for such a regard for efficiency is precisely the spirit of the Chinese entrepreneur! As an outstanding pupil of Mr Dong Fureng, my teacher’s understanding of economics itself is likewise profound and original; at every kind of important conference and forum, it is so often my teacher who advances a distinctive and courageous point of view. That objectivity, rationality, and courage in facing reality are the inner core of temperament of the entrepreneur, and at the same time of the scholar. I am my teacher’s first doctoral student, and because of this we have both, teacher and student alike, shouldered a certain pressure. I have always hoped to do a little more, so as to lessen my teacher’s burdens and troubles, while my teacher has always afforded me every convenience, to the point that in my heart he is more like a senior member of my own family; and what I can give him in return is this — not to have him drag me along, and not to have him push me along, but only to have him point out a road to me, and then to walk it myself! Although on this road I still stumble along even now, my teacher’s gaze of expectation will forever be the inexhaustible driving force behind my courage to press forward!

That I have been able to come this far is owing to hardships and efforts on the part of my family that words cannot fittingly express. My father says that I am a man in whom feeling outweighs reason — and is he himself not just the same? Otherwise, how could he remain silent for so long, without a single word, every time I leave home to return to school? My mother is a little stern, but that cannot prevent her from wrapping, layer upon layer, the braised pork she has painstakingly prepared the day before, and stuffing it into my already bulging luggage; she knows that I seldom eat the meat in the canteen. So that I would not worry, my mother told me the truth about her glaucoma operation only after it had taken place, and to this day I am filled with guilt over it. My elder sister and my brother-in-law have always been straightforward with me, so that throughout my years at school I never once found myself embarrassed by an empty purse. Bear in mind that by this time I was already past thirty — no longer a child! Each of these kind acts by my family has always added to my pressure, and the only way to discharge that pressure is to read more books, for that is my proper duty. Yet just as I was torn between remaining in Beijing to work and returning home to become a teacher, a half-hidden barrier in my communication with my parents made me realise that I had already, without noticing it, embarked upon the road of the “so-called intellectual”. My mind is filled with the desire to come to know the real world afresh and to subject it to criticism, while my parents hope only that I will pass my life in tranquillity. To this day I have been unable to make them accept my various pronouncements, which makes this son of theirs seem a complete stranger before them; and at the same time I cannot escape the guidance that my already-formed way of thinking exercises over my own conduct. In the end I chose to remain in Beijing, while my parents, between regret and blessing, concluded that this was all the result of reading too many books and addling my brain. By now there is no turning back — forgetting, after all, is a hundred times harder than remembering — so all I can do is to continue along this road of the “so-called intellectual”. On this road I am, in fact, not alone: apart from the many people I do not know and am not acquainted with, there is one person closest of all to me — my wife. My wife was my classmate during my master’s studies; I am today, just as I was then, penniless, yet my wife remains beside me now just as she did then. In a world awash with material desire, to have such a breath of pure air accompanying me is the greatest of good fortune! So that I could graduate without hindrance, my wife went far from her native place and worked alone in Qingdao for two years, and never once spoke to me of the hardships involved. Whenever I hear that classmates around me have bought houses and cars and settled into stable lives, my heart, as a husband, fills with apology. All I can do now is to go on working and studying hard, so that my wife may be happy and our life to come may be filled with passion and hope.

Only at this moment have I come to appreciate that time moves in one direction only, and that the trajectory along which thought develops is, like the division of the cells of the human body, irreversible. The flesh, however, will perish, while thought grows ever richer! The individual human being — and especially the individual who believes himself to be of intellectual character — is precisely one who hopes, by offering up his own ideas and views, to earn a pass through the gate that leads from the small self to the greater self. In this sense, thought and knowledge are what live for ever! This individual of mine, insignificant as a speck of dust, is likewise walking this far from level road; and looking about me, I find that I am in fact not alone.

Only at this moment have I truly come to appreciate that the attainment of a goal can never be accomplished by the strength of one person alone: besides one’s own unremitting efforts, it requires the help of others. That the trajectory of my life has been able to reach where it is today is owed not only to the favour of my supervisor, Professor Mao Zhenhua, in first recognising my worth, and to his careful guidance over these three years, but also to the guidance given to my doctoral dissertation by Professors Cao Fengqi, Chen Yanbin, Li Yiping, Liu Yuanchun, Wang Jinbin, Liu Yingqiu, Guo Min, and Wang Zhongming; to Dr Dang Guodong, for a friendship of more than twenty years; and to my fellow students and good friends Dr Zhang Jiawei, Dr Miao Yongwang, Dr Hou Xiangqun, Dr Fei Tai’an, Dr Sha Jianyu, and Dr Chen Yingnan, and to all my classmates and friends. Without the support of these people, the road of my life would have become dull and difficult! These fine teachers and good friends are, like me, walking the road from the small self to the greater self. The road ahead will be travelled in their company — what good fortune that is!

Having finished writing these acknowledgements, my heart will truly set out upon a brand-new life. Upon my shoulders there will then rest, beyond the responsibilities of family, profession, and society, something more: the expectations attaching to a “so-called intellectual”. I hope that through my own thinking I may lead more people to think; I hope that economic theory will become a redoubt of thinkers rather than a territory monopolised by “craftsmen”; I hope that intellectuals, and not engineering and technical experts alone, will be held in regard; I hope that the great hand of the market will lead the Chinese economy onto a path of stable and healthy development; and I hope that the Chinese people will have more of the citizen in them, and less of the slave.

Facing the future, I strive on — and wait and see!

8 May 2011
