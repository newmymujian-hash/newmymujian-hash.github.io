Abstract

Production and consumption are the two key foundations on which human society exists; everything else, such as the construction of the economic, political, and cultural aspects of the social structure, is built upon them, so that developing production and satisfying consumption is the root of any society’s ability to continue and develop. At the birth of economic theory, because of the limitations of society’s average productive capacity, and hence the influence of social reality on the formation of theory, almost all economic theories concentrated on how to develop production and how to raise the output of capital goods and consumer goods. Theories aimed at explaining how productive capacity can be raised — the division of labour, specialisation, economies of scale, and the like — were therefore the important topics of the economics of the time, while consumption itself appeared only as an auxiliary, after the production stage. Only when society’s productive capacity had grown stronger and the crises of “relative overproduction” generated by the capitalist stage began to appear did reality remind us once again that, in economic practice, single-mindedly developing production while neglecting consumption — that important foundation and purpose of development — would produce very serious negative results. Economic research therefore began to attach importance to the role of consumption, and macroeconomics, beginning with Keynes, placed the management of “demand” in the foremost position in economic development, consumption occupying an important place within it. Thereafter, whether research on consumption proceeded from the macro or from the micro standpoint, it treated consumption as an important driving force and purpose of economic development. In economic practice, consumption, together with investment, trade, and government, became one of four parallel economic sectors and an endogenous driving force of social development.

Looking back over the history since the founding of New China, the course travelled in economic practice is extremely similar to the general course of economic development outlined above. Between 1949 and 1978, China’s economy was relatively backward in both industrial and agricultural productive capacity. This economy of “shortage” fitted together with, and formed the basis of, the planned economic system, and, under the guidance of the central government’s “catch-up strategy”, made it possible to accumulate capital for the country’s heavy industrialisation. In this period, therefore, the main force driving economic growth was investment; domestic consumption demand was suppressed by the institutional environment and by economic reality and was not an endogenous driving force of China’s economic growth. What is important is that, in this period, a “cash-flow model” gradually took shape at the level of the micro consumer: the individual had no assets and no liabilities, daily consumption depended on the cash flow of wage income, and large items of consumption depended on saving; because income was low, the volume of both daily consumption and large-scale consumption was relatively small, and the improvement in consumers’ utility was limited.

After China entered the market-oriented reforms of 1978, the country’s economic foundations and manufacturing capacity improved by a wide margin, and the profound changes in the economic system released latent economic vitality, so that in a fairly short time China passed from “shortage” to “relative overproduction”. At this time, the guiding thought for China’s economic development still focused first on GDP growth, so that developing foreign trade on the strength of the advantage of low labour costs and continuing to rely on investment to drive growth were still the most important development strategies of this period. Now, after another thirty years of development and improvement, China’s economic aggregate has risen to second place in the world, its manufacturing capacity and level have improved by leaps and bounds, and, in particular, as its transformation to a market economy and its integration into the world economy have deepened ever further, the pulling of sustained and healthy economic growth by domestic consumption demand has become an intrinsic requirement of further macroeconomic development. For a long time, however, the consumption model of the planned economy era has persisted under the new conditions of the market economy. The daily consumption of Chinese consumers still relies on the cash flow of income; although they own private property (such as housing or other financial assets), raising saving and avoiding liabilities as far as possible remains an important goal of the ordinary consumer. Under a market economy of “relative overproduction”, such a consumption model is not conducive to enterprises realising sales profits, is not conducive to raising consumers’ utility, and ultimately is not conducive to sustained and healthy economic development, so that a transformation of the consumption model becomes an inevitable choice.

Analysing China’s consumption model under market economy conditions, one can discover that although consumers’ incomes have risen, risk has increased together with employment risk; that although society recognises private property rights, the price of private housing far exceeds the level of individual income; that the returns and risks of financial assets are difficult to control; and that the construction of social security and safety networks concerning the individual’s old age, medical care, unemployment, and education seriously lags behind the needs of economic development — all of which increases consumers’ “precautionary saving” and seriously suppresses consumption.

This dissertation therefore holds that, for the healthy and sustainable development of China’s market economy, a transformation of the consumption model must be carried out — that is, a transformation from the “cash-flow consumption model” to the “balance-sheet consumption model”. The direction of government investment should be coordinated with this transformation process, mainly developing and improving the construction of the social security network so that consumers “dare to consume”; at the same time, a middle class with a certain level of liabilities and a stable capacity to repay should be actively developed, and the balance sheets of such people protected against bankruptcy. Provided that measures of this kind are effectively implemented, the balance-sheet consumption model will steadily raise China’s domestic consumption demand, so that consumption becomes an endogenous driving force of China’s economic growth and returns to the origin of economic development.

Chapter 1 Introduction

1.1 Statement of the Problem, and the Purpose and Significance of the Research

From the founding of New China to the present, taking 1978 as the dividing line, the economic system can be divided into a period of planned economy and a period of socialist market economy of roughly equal span. In the first stage, the planned economic system achieved considerable development in mobilising limited economic resources and carrying out heavy industrialisation, and the people’s standard of living also improved to a certain degree. However, because the planned economic system itself lacked an effective mechanism of economic incentives, and because the overall development strategy and guiding thought of the state were not, at the same time, oriented towards raising efficiency, these factors jointly caused China’s latent productive forces to remain unexploited throughout: in the sphere of production, investment was emphasised and consumption neglected as a general phenomenon, and the people’s life was long maintained at a low level of distributive equality. Because incomes were low, a universal rationing system for consumer goods was practised, and public ownership constrained consumers’ expectations, consumption behaviour was relatively simple: consumers had no obvious behaviour or means of smoothing their consumption path, and the contribution of consumption to the economy, relative to that of investment, was low.

After China entered the process of marketization, its economic potential was gradually brought into play; under a market system with efficiency as its goal, China’s various economic indicators have gradually risen to the forefront of the world, and today China’s GDP is already the second largest in the world, behind only that of the United States. Accompanying the earth-shaking changes in the face of the economy, Chinese consumers have completely escaped the constraint of scarcity of consumer goods and, as consumption has gradually been upgraded, have entered instead a new constraint, in which income cannot fully satisfy the demand for consumption — that is, they have passed from shortage to relative overproduction. From the standpoint of macroeconomic growth, if sustained and healthy development is to be achieved, the most urgent problem to be solved at present is how to transform the mode of economic growth from one driven by investment and external demand to one driven by domestic demand, and the problem of insufficient consumption demand is the pivot of this transformation; from the standpoint of the micro consumer, insufficient consumption demand manifests itself more as having the desire to consume but lacking the ability to consume, or not daring to consume. In the final analysis, the existence of the consumption problem at the micro level comes down to the fact that such problems as employment, income, and social security have not been fundamentally and effectively solved. It is precisely these problems that restrict consumers’ consumption behaviour, leaving consumers “unable” or “not daring” to consume. As to specific data: relative to investment and exports, the contribution rate of consumption — of household consumption in particular — to economic growth has not only failed to grow along with GDP, but has declined year by year. From 2001 to 2007, the average contribution of final consumption to GDP was 4.1 per cent, while that of investment and net exports over the same period was 6.1 per cent. Although, calculated at comparable prices, consumption still maintained a fairly high growth rate during this period, the consumption rate (final consumption expenditure / GDP) declined year after year, from about 60 per cent in 2000 to 48.8 per cent in 2007, and during 2003–2007 this ratio was even maintained for a long time at less than 40 per cent.

The purpose of economic development is, in itself, to satisfy the demand for consumption and to raise productive efficiency so as to change the state of material scarcity; yet the fact is that materials are abundant but cannot be consumed, which obviously departs from the ultimate goal of economic development. On the other hand, the share of household consumption in final consumption in particular has declined year by year, from close to 80 per cent in 1978 to 72 per cent in 2008. Within household final consumption, moreover, particular note should be taken of the fact that, while the consumption level of urban residents declined slightly, that of rural residents declined markedly, from 62 per cent of household final consumption in 1978 to 25 per cent in 2008. This shows that the development of China’s market economy raised the economic aggregate and lifted manufacturing and productive capacity, but that household consumption did not rise in step, and that the urban–rural disparities that existed in the planned economy era persist to this day. The facts above can give a rough outline of the transformations, and the problems, experienced by China’s economic development and household consumption in the course of the transition from a planned economy to a market economy.

First, the excessive pursuit of the speed of economic development, together with government intervention in the economy, has in part caused China’s economic structure to deviate from the ultimate goal of development. For any country or economy, the ultimate goal of economic development is first to satisfy the consumption demand of its internal consumers — in simple terms, “GDP is produced for the sake of consumption”, and not the reverse. Because throughout the planned economy period China took the “catch-up strategy” as the guiding thought of economic development, it was an inevitable result that speed and aggregate were pursued first, while economic structure and consumers were neglected. After China entered market-oriented reform, this guiding thought still did not change: in economic practice, China’s growth rate did indeed maintain high growth for a long time compared with other countries, and its economic aggregate has now risen to second place in the world, but the irrational economic structure created in return has also become a shackle that at present obstructs further high growth of the economy. For a long time, China’s low-cost advantage, its demand for foreign exchange, and the gradual improvement of its manufacturing capacity all made the foreign trade sector an important driving force of growth; on the other hand, the fact that investment — government investment in particular — drives growth quickly and with obvious effect also made it another convenient path of growth. These two aspects gradually developed and influenced one another, and the macroeconomic pattern ultimately formed in China is one that relies on trade and investment to drive economic growth, while consumption stagnates. As domestic labour costs gradually rise and foreign demand fluctuates, China’s trade growth has encountered resistance, which has led China’s economic growth to rely mainly on investment. It is the emergence of this economic structure that is the important reason why the goal of China’s economic development has turned towards developing domestic demand, consumption in particular, while no longer excessively emphasising the speed of growth.

Second, in the course of the transition from a planned economy to a market economy, reform in income distribution and social security lagged behind economic growth, thereby severely restricting residents’ capacity and motivation to consume. In the planned economy era, although differentiated urban–rural management was practised and the average material standard of living of consumers nationwide was generally low, the housing, medical care, old-age provision, and income of urban residents were all in the end closely tied to employment: so long as one had a job, these problems could in the end all be solved within the “work unit”; the livelihood security of rural residents rested entirely on the land. As China stepped through the gate of the market economy, responsibility for social security shifted from being borne wholly by the work unit and the state to being borne jointly by enterprises, the state, and individuals; at the same time, the state’s formal recognition of private property rights also provided consumers with a wholly new economic domain. These changes completely altered consumers’ economic behaviour and mode of expectation in the planned economy era, and gradually made Chinese consumers into “rational economic men”. Specifically, consumers’ mode of employment has also undergone a profound transformation as reform has deepened: gradual marketization has brought an optimal allocation of human capital and raised efficiency, but has at the same time increased employment risk, which has become an important aspect of the increased risk attaching to income, housing, and other social security. In order to withstand this risk, consumers continually optimise in education investment, saving, housing, and other assets, and they also make use of the “altruism” between generations — which manifests itself very clearly in China — to carry out overall optimisation with the household as the unit. The result of this optimisation, however, is a reduction of consumption! At the same time, while government fiscal revenue has grown uninterruptedly along with the economic aggregate and government investment has risen continually, public expenditure on social security has been seriously insufficient; and the changes in the policies of primary and secondary income distribution have alike failed to set out on a path favourable to the sustained increase of consumers’ incomes and the stabilisation of consumers’ expectations.

It is precisely on the basis of the facts in these two aspects that the root of China’s problem of insufficient consumption demand does not lie in the sphere of consumption itself. It is not wholly because of a scarcity of the means and instruments of consumption, or an insufficiency of productive capacity; rather, it is rooted in the disadvantageous position of consumers in the sphere of distribution, which causes insufficient income, and in the excessive risk faced by individual consumers in employment and hence in income — which in the end compels rational consumers to cut consumption, raise saving, or purchase assets that preserve and increase in value, in order to offset these risks. This, and nothing else, is the root cause of China’s insufficient consumption demand! If China’s economy is to grow healthily and sustainably, the problems of the consumption sphere must therefore be thoroughly resolved; and this requires that consumption theory and practice should not look for answers within the sphere of consumption alone, but should look for solutions rather in the non-consumption spheres.

As regards research method, research that aims to solve China’s consumption problems by studying the sphere of consumption itself will inevitably move towards the questions of how to raise the consumption rate and the aggregate of consumption; accordingly, encouraging consumption, creating new instruments of consumption, and improving the consumption environment become the important aspects of such research. It cannot be denied that these aspects have a certain effect in improving consumption, but logically there is no definite answer to such questions as: what share of consumption in GDP is the “correct” one? There is no fully sufficient reason to hold that a consumption rate of about 75 per cent in the United States is the target that China and the other countries of the world should reach; nor, equally, is there any reason to hold that certain consumption indicators of other countries should become the test of whether China’s marketization has succeeded. If we abandon the line of thought of “solving consumption problems within the sphere of consumption” and move towards the line of thought of “solving consumption problems outside the sphere of consumption”, we can on the contrary obtain certain more valuable methods and conclusions. Why, for example, do American consumers not only consume out of current income but also dare to consume out of future income? Why was the consumption rate of Chinese consumers in the planned economy era also very high? Why is the consumption rate relatively high in high-welfare countries such as Sweden? The essence lies in differences in the consumption model. A consumption model is not simply a few factors such as the mode and means of consumption; it exists within a country’s macroeconomic environment and is a composite formed jointly by the various factors closely connected with consumers — among which economic growth, the primary distribution of national income, economic development policy, and the international economic environment are external macroeconomic factors; employment and income are the beginning of the consumption model; social security is the foundation of the consumption model; and the mode and content of consumption are the end of the consumption model. The consumption model of each country is different, so consumption rates cannot be completely identical; hence how to construct a consumption model that conforms to China’s current state of development and to its purposes is the goal of research on the consumption problem, and this is also the main purpose of this dissertation. To this end, this dissertation puts forward the “cash-flow consumption model” and the “balance-sheet consumption model” in order to summarise and subsume, in a unified way, all the various factors above that ought to become the content of consumption research, and, on this basis, analyses a unique characteristic of Chinese consumers: the altruism that exists between generations. This characteristic, interacting with macroeconomic development and the various economic policies, jointly determines China’s present economic face — in particular, the reality of insufficient consumption demand. Only when theoretical research turns towards research on consumption models, and economic practice in turn shifts from aggregates and growth rates towards consumption, can the various threads of the Chinese economy truly be clarified, economic development kept from deviating from its correct track, and the value of economic development ultimately realised.

1.2 Research Methods

The research methods of this dissertation are based mainly on logical analysis, with corresponding use of certain technical methods of analysis, such as the comparative method of analysis and methods of data analysis. In addition, in its specific methods this dissertation seeks to combine dynamic analysis with static analysis; this is not only a requirement of consumption theory itself, but also a requirement of scientific economic research methods. It also combines microeconomic analysis with macroeconomic analysis: research on the consumption problem needs not only to proceed from the standpoint of the micro individual, analysing its consumption structure, its structure of income and expenditure, and even its household structure, but also to proceed from the standpoint of the macroeconomic sectors, analysing the macroeconomic phenomena formed by micro behaviour and their relationships, if correct conclusions are to be obtained.

1.3 Structure of the Dissertation

Chapter 1 is the introduction. It briefly introduces the background against which this dissertation was written, its research basis and methods, and introduces the structure of the dissertation. This dissertation mainly adopts a method combining theoretical analysis with empirical research, also using econometric methods and model analysis to verify some of its conclusions.

Chapter 2 is the review of the literature. The author divides the theories of consumption in economic theory into two broad classes according to whether they contain “uncertainty”. Under conditions of certainty, it mainly reviews the thought on consumption theory of the classical period and the macroeconomic consumption theory of Keynes; on the microeconomic side, it mainly reviews the “life-cycle theory” and the “permanent income theory”. These two aspects — the macroeconomic and microeconomic consumption theories — constitute the foundation of consumption theory and the starting point of all research. Subsequently, the “random walk theory” and the “precautionary saving theory”, which contain uncertainty and risk, advance consumption theory to a level closer to reality, and their descriptions of consumers’ behaviour gradually become scientific and objective. Together with the development of theoretical thought, econometric techniques have contributed richly to the progress of consumption theory, making important contributions especially in verifying theory. Finally, China’s economists, in the area of consumption theory, have mainly drawn on foreign theory and on relatively mature consumption functions, using China’s data to verify the existence of consumption functions or to construct consumption functions that conform to China; among these there is also research that takes into account the intergenerational altruism that exists in China. All this has made China’s economists’ research on consumption distinctive in its own way.

Chapter 3 puts forward the “cash-flow model” and the “balance-sheet model”, and, starting from the planned economy era before China’s marketization reform, dissects the causes and foundations of the formation of the “cash-flow model” and its interaction with the macro economy. While affirming the merits of the “cash-flow model”, it also points out that its existence requires an extensive and stable foundation of social security; this was relatively easy to achieve in the planned economy era, but after marketization reform it faces many problems. On the other hand, as the economy has developed and consumption has been upgraded, the changes in consumers’ demand for consumer goods have also raised new requirements for the cash-flow consumption model, but these requirements have already exceeded the functions of the cash-flow model.

Chapter 4 analyses the transformation from the cash-flow consumption model to the balance-sheet consumption model. According to the differences between the two, this dissertation holds that the transformation of the consumption model requires, first, the maintenance of stable growth on the income side; at the same time, residents’ balance sheets must be further enriched; and, by changing income distribution, there must gradually be formed a middle class of the greatest significance for social stability and economic growth. These measures will effectively promote the transformation of the consumption model, and the transformation of the consumption model will promote the sustained and healthy development of the Chinese economy.

Chapter 5 dissects the obstacles to, and risks of, the transformation of the consumption model. It points out that the balance-sheet consumption model itself also has various risks, and that the existence of these risks is very sensitive to economic stability. China is in a period in which the economic structure is changing rapidly, the process of urbanisation is gradually accelerating, and the adjustment of the industrial structure is accelerating; add to this the risk of changes in the international economic environment, and all of these affect the healthy development of the balance-sheet consumption model. The transformation of the consumption model should therefore be considered at the level of the state, and the construction of the consumption model considered as a whole.

The final chapter compares, from a country standpoint, several countries that are typical in respect of consumption models: the United States, Japan, India, and Sweden. By comparing the similarities and differences of their consumption structures and models with China’s, it shows once again that looking for a solution to the problem of insufficient consumption demand within the sphere of consumption itself is not enough; the ultimate solution lies in changing the consumption model as a whole. Only by constructing a consumption model suited to the goals and current state of China’s economic development can the problem of insufficient consumption demand that currently exists be solved at the root. Finally, the success of the transformation of the consumption model requires policy support; among such support, the rational adjustment of income distribution policy, fiscal policy, and industrial policy will play an important role in ultimately forming a “balance-sheet consumption model” suited to China’s national conditions.

1.4 Innovations and Shortcomings

The main innovation of this dissertation is that it puts forward the “cash-flow consumption model” and the “balance-sheet consumption model” and redefines the consumption model; on this basis it systematically analyses the consumption problems that exist in China’s consumption sphere and macroeconomic sphere and puts forward solutions to them. The word “model” is used to express a state that has universality and broad applicability; the existence of a model is the result of various conditional constraints, not an isolated or accidental existence. This dissertation therefore particularly points out that research on consumption models needs to consider the constraints as a whole, rather than examining the isolated model itself. Under the guidance of this thought, this dissertation’s research on consumption does not take how to raise the consumption rate as its focus, but concentrates on consumption models and their constraints. This holistic line of thought in analysing the consumption problem is also one of the innovations of this dissertation. Finally, on this new analytical basis, this dissertation proposes that, in order to construct a “balance-sheet consumption model” suited to China, efforts must be made jointly in the directions favourable to raising consumer utility and equity in such aspects as the goals of economic development, the mode of growth, fiscal policy, and income distribution.

The shortcomings of this dissertation are, first, that its research on micro consumption individuals lacks the support of micro data. Although this defect is caused by the fact that China’s economic data generally lack statistics on micro data, the negative effect of the insufficiency of data on the persuasiveness of the dissertation is nevertheless objectively present. Second, the insufficient use of econometric instruments and model analysis owing to the scarcity of data is another shortcoming of this dissertation. These therefore also become the direction in which this dissertation may continue to be improved and its persuasiveness enhanced: namely, to collect and organise micro consumption data over a fairly long time — data sets of a dynamic kind, like those collected by economists in the United States — and to subject them to modelled econometric analysis; and, at the same time, to observe the connection between the constraints on consumption models and the consumption models themselves in economic practice, further furnishing evidence from practice for the theory of this dissertation. In this way, not only can China’s consumption problem be researched more scientifically, but this dissertation can also be powerfully supplemented and improved.
